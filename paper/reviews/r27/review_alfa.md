# Peer Review — Reviewer alfa

## Summary

The paper asks whether autoregressive TTS models' well-documented failure to correctly render text that repeats a word or phrase many times ("looping"/miscounting) is driven by repetition itself or is simply a byproduct of longer inputs (attention span, exposure bias, duration budgeting). Using a 180-item "repetition ladder" — each repeated-word stimulus paired with a length- and word-count-matched control whose repeated token is replaced by distinct fillers — the authors test six checkpoints across three TTS architecture families (Llasa 1B/3B/8B, Qwen3-TTS 0.6B/1.7B, XTTS-v2), plus non-autoregressive baselines (VITS, F5-TTS) and a repetition-penalty ablation. The headline result is a large, robust gap between exact-count rendering on repeated text (18.2%) versus length-matched controls (94.3%) at k≥6, which the authors argue survives decoding changes, judge choice, and exclusion-rule variation. A secondary "period ladder" shows the deficit is graded with period rather than requiring verbatim adjacency. A Lean-4-verified theoretical account (contraction of a per-repetition state map bounding counting horizon) is proposed and then empirically measured and rejected as false for every checkpoint tested.

## Major concerns

1. **Issue** — The study's central quantitative claims rest on analytic choices (the k≥6 threshold, the exclusion rules, the choice of cycled vs. never-cycled control fillers) that were selected *after* inspecting the data, with no stated pre-registration of the primary comparison prior to data collection.
   **Where** — Section 2 ("a threshold set after seeing the data" in the Abstract; Section 3.1: "The k ≥ 6 threshold was chosen after seeing where the arms diverge"); the 420-specification robustness sweep in Section 3.1.
   **Why it matters** — The specification-curve exercise (34.4–86.4 points, median 70.5, sign never reversing) is a genuine and commendable attempt at post-hoc exculpation, but it cannot substitute for pre-registration: it tells us the *sign* of the effect is robust to the authors' own menu of forking choices, not that the *magnitude* reported in the abstract (67.6–75.4 points) is unbiased. Because the threshold and exclusions were chosen with knowledge of the outcome, the headline numbers likely sit near the favorable end of what the garden of forking paths could produce, even if by construction they cannot cross zero. A skeptical reader has no way to distinguish "the effect is this large" from "this is the largest defensible cut of an effect that exists at some unknown, possibly much smaller, magnitude."
   **What would address it** — Either (a) pre-register the primary threshold/exclusion set for a follow-up replication ladder and report that as the confirmatory result, treating the current data as exploratory, or (b) report a single pre-specified "primary" estimate (e.g., the unconditioned k=1 aggregate, which the authors do note in the abstract: 46.8 points) as the headline figure, relegating the k≥6-conditioned 67.6–75.4-point figures to a clearly labeled exploratory analysis.

2. **Issue** — The "length-matched control" is not actually free of periodicity for k>8, because the filler pool is cycled every 8 tokens, reintroducing a period-8 structure into the control itself for k=12,16,24,32 — exactly the items driving much of the headline gap.
   **Where** — Section 2 ("the repeated token replaced by fillers from a per-template pool of eight, cycled above k = 8"); Section 3.1 ("Above k = 8 the cycled control is itself period-8, so that contrast is p = 1 against p = 8 rather than against aperiodic text").
   **Why it matters** — The authors acknowledge this and report a "never-cycled" comparison (67.6 by family vs. 75.4 cycled), but it is unclear from the text which version underlies the flagship Figure 1(a)/Table 1 numbers (18.2% vs. 94.3%, and the per-checkpoint medians in Table 1) — the "never-cycled" analysis appears to be a separate side computation, not the number that appears in the abstract and Table 1. If the primary reported effect uses the cycled-control version, then for the highest-k items — which weigh heavily in a k≥6 aggregate — the "control" is not actually the clean length-matched contrast the paper's core logic depends on; it is confounded by weak periodicity of its own, which (per the period ladder in Fig 1b) should itself suppress the control's exact-match rate somewhat, meaning the true repeated-vs-control gap for those items may be understated, not inflated. But the ambiguity itself is the problem: which comparison licenses which stated number is not traceable from the text.
   **What would address it** — State explicitly, item by item or at minimum figure/table by figure/table, whether cycled or never-cycled fillers were used, and report both versions side-by-side for every headline statistic, not only for the aggregate family/checkpoint gap.

3. **Issue** — The recognizer/judge calibration ("audit") that licenses the switch from Whisper to a CTC model — the single most consequential methodological decision in the paper — is based on extremely small samples (n=12 for Whisper, n=24 for CTC) of synthetic, concatenative ground-truth audio, not on the actual model-generated pathological audio (loops, truncations, garbling) that the study exists to measure.
   **Where** — Section 2, "Behavioural measurement, and why the obvious judge is wrong."
   **Why it matters** — Concatenated clean word tokens are acoustically well-behaved (clean onsets/offsets, no coarticulation artifacts, no runaway prosody). The actual TTS failure modes under study — models that "stop early, run on, or lock into a loop" — likely produce audio with very different acoustic properties (glitches, unnatural transitions, artifacts at the loop boundary) that a CTC recognizer's greedy best-path decoding may handle differently than it handles clean concatenative audio. The paper never validates the *chosen* judge against ground truth on the model's own pathological output (only against synthetic ground truth), so the entire quantitative infrastructure of the paper rests on an n=12/n=24 calibration that may not transfer to the regime it is deployed on. This is a classic instrumentation-validity gap: the instrument is validated in a setting systematically easier than the one it is used in.
   **What would address it** — Validate the CTC judge (and ideally cross-check against human transcription) directly on a sample of the actual model-generated audio under study, stratified by outcome type (correct, over-count, under-count, loop, truncation), not only on synthetic concatenative controls. The 165-clip "checkable by ear" sample mentioned under Code/data could serve this purpose if it were formally scored against independent human transcription and reported as a validation statistic rather than offered only as an auditability artifact.

4. **Issue** — Decoding/sampling settings are not held constant across model families ("Sampling uses each system's shipped defaults... they make Qwen3-TTS the most stochastic family"), so cross-architecture comparisons (the mixed-effects model with architecture as a random effect, the family-level partial pooling) confound architecture with an uncontrolled nuisance variable — stochasticity/temperature — that plausibly interacts with counting behavior.
   **Where** — Section 2, "Code, data, settings"; Section 3.1's mixed-effects and partial-pooling analysis.
   **Why it matters** — The paper treats "architecture family" as the random-effect grouping variable whose generalization (the posterior predictive interval [5.9, 93.3] used to make an ex ante prediction for CosyVoice 2) is a load-bearing piece of evidence. But if decoding temperature/top-p differ systematically by family and covary with the outcome, the "architecture" random effect is at least partly a decoding-hyperparameter effect mislabeled as an architecture effect. The paper does show the deficit survives an explicit penalty sweep and greedy-vs-sampled comparison *within* two architectures (S15, S22) — good — but this does not establish that between-family default-setting differences don't bias the between-family variance component used for the CosyVoice 2 prediction.
   **What would address it** — Re-run at least the k≥6 contrast for all six checkpoints under matched, explicitly stated decoding hyperparameters (temperature, top-p, top-k) in addition to shipped defaults, and report whether the family-level random-effect estimate changes materially.

5. **Issue** — The causal-manipulation experiment meant to arbitrate between "count erased from state" and "count survives but readout fails" is severely underpowered (n=36 for the null-effect claim on Llasa-8B) and its null result is over-interpreted as informative.
   **Where** — Section 5, "The rival we could not decide" ("It is registered but carries nothing, steering only at 16× its magnitude; on Llasa-8B it is never detectable, bounding only 19% at n=36").
   **Why it matters** — A non-significant/near-null causal intervention effect at n=36, admitted by the authors themselves to only "bound" the effect at 19%, is consistent with both "the readout genuinely doesn't cause the count" and "the rank-1 patch is simply too weak or mis-targeted an intervention to move behavior, regardless of the state's causal role." The paper's own honest framing ("we could not decide"; "it is registered but carries nothing") is appropriately hedged in the prose, but the section still reads as contributing evidence toward "the deficit is a readout failure" when the design cannot actually support that inference at this sample size and intervention strength.
   **What would address it** — Either scale up the intervention sample and try a graded range of transplant magnitudes with a pre-specified detection-power target, or drop the causal claim to an explicitly flagged open question in the limitations rather than a discussion subsection with quantitative framing.

## Minor concerns

- The blinding status of the "165-clip stratified sample... so a judgement can be checked by ear" is not stated — was manual audit performed blind to condition (repeated vs. control) and to model identity? This matters for the credibility of the human-checkable component of the evidence chain.
- Item-level replication is unclear: three seeds per item are described, but it is not stated how many *distinct carrier sentences* underlie each k value, so it is hard to assess whether item (rather than checkpoint) is adequately modeled as a source of variance in the mixed-effects analysis.
- Missing citations on Whisper's specific propensity to hallucinate/loop under repetitive or degenerate acoustic input, which would directly bolster the paper's "Whisper is the wrong judge" argument, e.g. Barański et al., "Investigation of Whisper ASR Hallucinations Induced by Non-Speech Audio" [2501.11378], and Jasiński et al., "From Text Metrics to Model Internals: A Study of Whisper ASR Hallucination Detection" [2606.23060].
- The Venkatesh citation [13] (arXiv:2605.09239) is used to support "It is not erased: it stays decodable late," but that paper studies text LLMs, not TTS; the analogy is reasonable but its scope (linear-probe decodability in a completely different modality/task) should be stated more cautiously than the current single clause.
- "an odd period interpolating" (Section 3.1) and the k=24 odd-rung numbers (62.3%, 90.0%) are reported without confidence intervals, unlike most other headline numbers in the paper — inconsistent uncertainty reporting.
- Table 1's "Par." column mixes 1B/3B/8B and 0.6B/1.7B/0.4B without a unit label header beyond implicit "B" — minor formatting clarity.
- CosyVoice 2 is introduced only in Section 3.1 as an ad hoc validation case, without ever being listed among the "six checkpoints, three architectures" panel description in Section 2 — this should be reconciled (is it a 7th checkpoint / 4th family, held out by design, or an afterthought?).

## Verdict

**Major revision** — the core manipulation (length-matched controls) and the self-imposed robustness battery are genuinely strong, but the post-hoc threshold/exclusion selection, the ambiguous cycled-vs-never-cycled control basis for the headline numbers, and the thin (n=12/24) judge-calibration sample undercut confidence in the reported effect *magnitudes* even though the qualitative direction of the finding is well supported.

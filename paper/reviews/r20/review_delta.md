STANCE: Skeptical-but-fair

### Summary
The paper asks whether autoregressive TTS models' well-documented tendency to lose count on repeated words is a length effect or a periodicity effect, and separates the two with a "repetition ladder" of 180 stimuli in which each repeated-word item has a length- and word-count-matched control whose repeated token is replaced by distinct fillers. Across six checkpoints spanning three architectures (Llasa 1B/3B/8B, Qwen3-TTS 0.6B/1.7B, XTTS-v2), plus two non-autoregressive baselines (VITS, F5-TTS) and a later-added fourth architecture (CosyVoice 2), the authors report that repeated items are rendered exactly 18.2% of the time versus 94.3% for length-matched controls at k≥6, a gap that survives decoding-rule sweeps, exclusion-rule ablations, and re-scoring by four different ASR judges. They separately show that the standard judge (Whisper) is itself biased by the same autoregressive dynamics under study, motivating a CTC-based judge instead. Finally, they formalize a candidate mechanism (a Lipschitz-contraction argument, machine-checked in Lean 4) predicting the failure, measure its premise on one checkpoint, and find it false (the boundary-to-boundary Jacobian is expansive, not contractive), concluding the mechanism does not explain the phenomenon even though the phenomenon itself is real.

### Major concerns

1. **Issue** — The paper's central theoretical vehicle (a Lipschitz-contraction argument formalized in Lean 4) is presented as a from-scratch contribution without being situated against closely related transformer-expressivity and state-tracking work that bears directly on the same depth/precision/separation questions.
   **Where** — Section 4 ("THE CANDIDATE MECHANISM, MEASURED AND REFUTED") and the Related Work paragraph in Section 5.
   **Why it matters** — The paper cites a reasonable cluster on transformer counting expressivity ([15]–[20]), but omits directly relevant depth-based separation results (e.g., Merrill & Sabharwal's work on log-precision/log-depth transformer expressivity, arXiv:2207.00729 and arXiv:2503.03961, which formalize exactly the kind of "how much depth is needed to separate states/counts" question the Lean bound is trying to capture from a dynamical-systems angle). Without that context, a reader cannot tell whether the contraction-based no-separation argument is a genuinely new formal object or a reformulation, in Lipschitz-dynamics language, of results already established in the expressivity literature. Since the paper leans heavily on the formal-verification framing as differentiating novelty ("machine-checked in Lean 4... free of sorry"), the absence of this comparison is a real novelty-attribution gap, not a stylistic one — especially since the bound is ultimately refuted empirically, so its main remaining value to the field is as a formal artifact, which makes correct positioning against prior formal/expressivity work more important, not less.
   **What would address it** — Add a paragraph explicitly comparing the contraction-based argument to expressivity/state-tracking results with growing-depth or bounded-precision assumptions, stating what is genuininely new (the Lipschitz-contraction framing, or the mechanized proof) versus what recapitulates known limits.

2. **Issue** — The claimed contribution of "adding the causal half" to Venkatesh's [13] representational finding is substantially narrower than the framing suggests, resting on a single checkpoint and very small n.
   **Where** — Section 5, Discussion ("The rival the evidence favours... we add the causal half and the isolation of periodicity from length"); the underlying evidence is the rank-1 patch experiment (n=36) and splicing experiment on Llasa-1B only.
   **Why it matters** — [13] is confirmed to be a real, directly relevant, very recent (2026) paper on the exact representational dissociation this work extends causally. The extension here is legitimate but is explicitly flagged by the authors two sentences later as "single-checkpoint results... they refute the mechanism where tested, not panel-wide." Positioning this as a paper-level contribution alongside the panel-wide periodicity result (which spans 6 checkpoints / 4 architectures) overstates the generality of the causal claim relative to what was actually run, even though the authors do walk it back in the same paragraph — the imbalance between the headline framing and the n=36/1-checkpoint scope is a novelty-attribution problem the reader must dig for.
   **What would address it** — Hedge the contribution claim in the abstract/intro to explicitly scope it to one checkpoint (as is already done responsibly in the limitations paragraph), rather than stating it as flatly as "we add the causal half."

3. **Issue** — Reference [7], the paper's own unpublished companion study, is used to help frame Whisper's autoregressive bias as an established phenomenon, creating a citation dependency on a same-group preprint that cannot be independently checked.
   **Where** — Abstract/Intro framing ("companion to a spectral study of hallucination... [7]") and implicitly behind the motivation for treating ASR-side autoregressive bias as expected.
   **Why it matters** — This paper's own audit (Section 2) is fortunately self-contained evidence for the Whisper-judge critique, so the finding doesn't strictly require [7]. But citing an unreviewed same-author 2026 preprint as part of the paper's evidentiary scaffolding, without any indication of its review status, invites a self-reinforcing citation loop where two unpublished papers from the same group cite each other as support. This is a novelty/provenance issue reviewers should be able to evaluate independently.
   **What would address it** — Clarify [7]'s review/publication status, and make explicit (as the paper mostly already does) that the Whisper critique here stands on its own audit and does not depend on [7]'s findings.

4. **Issue** — The mixed-effects/partial-pooling architecture-level inference (Section 3.1) treats a 3-family random-effects estimate, and then a single out-of-sample confirmation (CosyVoice 2, n=1 new architecture), as strong validation of a wide predictive interval.
   **Where** — Section 3.1, the paragraph beginning "Partial pooling answers the question three families cannot answer directly."
   **Why it matters** — With only 3 groups feeding the random-effects variance estimate, the group-level variance component is essentially unidentifiable from first principles; the authors are honest that under "the widest prior on the group-level scales that interval does touch zero," but then treat CosyVoice 2 landing inside a wide 65.3-point-mean/[5.9, 93.3]-point interval as meaningful confirmation ("the agreement is closer than it entitles us to expect"). A single confirmatory point falling inside a wide interval is weak evidence of anything beyond "the true value probably isn't near zero for that architecture," which is a much smaller claim than the passage's rhetorical weight implies.
   **What would address it** — State plainly that n=4 architecture families is still far too few to support a generalizable claim about "typical" gap size across architectures, and that the CosyVoice 2 result is a directional (not quantitatively precise) replication.

5. **Issue** — The F5-TTS duration-supplying experiment, used to support a specific mechanistic story ("what plausibly separates" VITS from F5-TTS), is based on extremely small samples (n=30 below k=12, n=60 at/above it).
   **Where** — Section 3.2, paragraph on F5-TTS and duration.
   **Why it matters** — The conclusion "handed the right length the model still cannot place thirty-two repetitions inside it" is a fairly specific mechanistic claim (about where the count "lives") built on n=60 generations for the harder regime, with no error bars reported for the "0.0 gain" figure in that cell. Given how central this argument is to distinguishing the duration-model account from the token-based one, it needs uncertainty quantification, not a point estimate.
   **What would address it** — Report confidence intervals or a hierarchical estimate for the duration-supplying manipulation's effect size at both k regimes, and be explicit that this is a one-model, one-manipulation result (as the limitations section does for the VITS/F5-TTS "duration story" more generally, but not specifically flagged in-line here).

### Minor concerns
- The repo name (`icassp_antispoofing`) housing this TTS-counting study is confusingly unrelated to the paper's topic; the parenthetical "name predates this study" helps but a pointer to the specific tag/subdirectory for this paper's material would reduce reader confusion.
- The abstract's claim "replicates at 65.1–76.7 points across four recognisers" and the Spanish-language judge audit (S9, S27) sit close enough together that a reader could conflate "judge validated cross-lingually" with "main effect replicated cross-lingually," when Section 5 clarifies the actual Spanish periodicity replication was "unreportable." A one-clause disambiguation earlier in the paper (e.g., in Section 2) would prevent this.
- Table 1's Qwen3-TTS-1.7B row (0.0% median error gap, described in text as "the checkpoint whose median-error gap is zero") is a genuinely interesting edge case for the periodicity story but gets only a parenthetical mention; a sentence on why this checkpoint's median count error washes out while its exact-match rate (38.9% vs 98.9%) still shows the effect would help readers reconcile the two columns of Table 1.
- "S" cross-references (S1–S27) to supplementary material are used extremely densely (dozens of times) without the supplement included in the reviewed text; several load-bearing claims (S12's r=0.59 correlation, S16's timestamping, S26's patch experiment details) are unverifiable from the main text alone.
- Minor terminology drift: "contribute" list in the Introduction calls the judge methodology finding a "measurement," while Section 2's framing calls it "its own problem" — consistent in substance but could be tightened for a single consistent claim statement.

### Verdict
**Major revision** — the core periodicity-vs-length dissociation and judge-bias findings are well-supported and unusually thoroughly stress-tested, but the paper's novelty framing for its formal-verification contribution needs to be positioned against existing transformer-expressivity/state-tracking literature, and several of its most rhetorically weighted claims (cross-architecture generalization via partial pooling, the "causal half" contribution, the F5-TTS duration story) rest on samples or group counts too small to bear the interpretive weight currently placed on them.

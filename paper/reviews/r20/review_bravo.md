# Review

### Summary
The paper asks whether the well-documented failure of autoregressive TTS models to render exact repetition counts is a length effect or a periodicity effect, and isolates the two with a 180-item "repetition ladder" in which each repeated-word stimulus (k copies of a target word) is paired with a length-matched control (same carrier and word count, but k distinct filler words). Across six checkpoints from three architecture families, scored primarily with a CTC recogniser validated against Whisper on concatenative ground truth, repeated items are rendered exactly 18.2% of the time versus 94.3% for controls at k≥6 — a gap the authors show survives decoding-rule sweeps, exclusion-rule ablations, alternate recognisers, a held-out fourth architecture (CosyVoice 2), and (partially) never-cycled control fillers. A companion mechanistic study derives a Lean-4-verified bound under which a contracting per-repetition state map would preclude counting past a horizon, measures the contraction factor q on one checkpoint (Llasa-1B), and finds q≫1 (expansive, not contracting) — refuting the proposed mechanism where tested, and reporting a causal patching experiment suggesting the count representation is present but behaviourally unused.

### Major concerns

1. **Issue** — The stimulus design cannot separate "periodicity" from "verbatim token identity," yet the paper's title, abstract, and mechanistic story (§4) are built on periodicity as the operative variable.
   **Where** — §5 "What the design does not separate" admits it directly ("Periodicity and verbatim token identity vary together here, so 'periodicity' is the pair"); this qualification is absent from the title, abstract, and §3.1's framing ("the limit tracks periodicity, not length").
   **Why it matters** — An adversarial reading of the headline claim asks what would falsify it. A fully consistent alternative account is that models specifically fail at re-emitting the *identical* token/word many times in a row (e.g., a suppressed copying/induction mechanism, or a training-time bias against verbatim duplication), with no need to invoke periodic state contraction at all. Because every periodic stimulus in the ladder is also a verbatim-repeated-token stimulus, §4's entire mechanistic apparatus (attention flattening, boundary-to-boundary contraction) is motivated by a variable the design cannot isolate. This is a load-bearing confound, not a peripheral one — it undercuts the paper's own title.
   **What would address it** — Add a condition that is periodic but not verbatim-identical (e.g., an alternating ABAB… two-word cycle, or k copies of a paraphrase-equivalent but acoustically distinct token) and show the deficit persists; without it, "periodicity" should be relabeled "periodic verbatim repetition" throughout, including the title.

2. **Issue** — The evidentiary weight placed on "pre-specified" analyses and the CosyVoice-2 out-of-sample prediction rests entirely on a self-hosted, self-controlled timestamp (S16), not an independently verifiable pre-registration.
   **Where** — §3.1 ("a fit committed before the checkpoint's first waveform existed (S16 timestamps it)"); §3.1/§3.2 section headers labeled "Pre-specified."
   **Why it matters** — The out-of-sample CosyVoice-2 test is presented as the paper's strongest confirmatory result (a genuine falsifiable prediction that came true). But its evidentiary value depends entirely on the authors not having seen CosyVoice-2 data before fixing the prediction interval — a claim attested only by the authors' own git history, which they control. No third-party registry (OSF, a dated preprint, an escrow commit) is used. For a paper whose central rhetorical move is "we pre-registered X and it held," this is a meaningful gap.
   **What would address it** — Register the analysis plan on an independent, timestamped platform before running new-architecture checks, or provide a cryptographic/third-party attestation of the commit history's authenticity.

3. **Issue** — Hierarchical/mixed-effects and partial-pooling inference is reported as if precise, despite resting on only three independent architecture-family clusters.
   **Where** — §3.1 ("A mixed-effects linear probability model with architecture as a random effect... 76.4 points"; "the posterior predictive gap for an architecture outside the panel is 65.3 points [5.9, 93.3], P(gap>0)=0.996").
   **Why it matters** — With n=3 clusters, a random-effects variance component for "architecture" is barely identifiable; the paper's own text half-concedes this ("under the widest prior on the group-level scales that interval does touch zero"), but still foregrounds the point estimates as if reporting a well-powered result. Readers not looking for the hedge in the prose may take "76.4 points, all six interval constructions exclude zero" as stronger evidence than three clusters can license.
   **What would address it** — Either downgrade these to descriptive statistics with explicit caveats about cluster count, or add a fourth-plus independent architecture family before presenting formal hierarchical-model inference as a headline number (the paper does add CosyVoice-2, but as an out-of-sample check, not as a fourth cluster feeding the variance-component estimate).

4. **Issue** — The control's filler pool cycles at k>8, reintroducing periodicity into the supposedly aperiodic control exactly at the high-k range that drives the headline "even at k=32" claim.
   **Where** — §2 Stimuli ("replaced by fillers from a per-template pool of eight, cycled above k=8"); §3.1's never-cycled follow-up ("dissociation at 71.2 points against 84.6 for cycled controls (n=240)").
   **Why it matters** — At the top of the ladder, where the effect is reported as largest, the control itself becomes a period-8 sequence — the very confound the design exists to rule out. The authors' own never-cycled correction shows the cycling artifact is real and non-trivial (a 13.4-point residual), but this correction appears to be run on a subset of conditions (k=12…32, n=240) rather than propagated through the full panel's headline numbers in Table 1 / Fig. 1, and it is not clear how many of the six checkpoints contribute to that n=240.
   **What would address it** — Report the full panel's headline effect sizes using never-cycled controls throughout, not as a secondary check, and clarify how many checkpoints/architectures contribute to the n=240 correction.

5. **Issue** — The "where the count must live" architectural hypothesis (token-level vs. holistic duration) generalizes from one exemplar per class (VITS vs. F5-TTS).
   **Where** — §3.2 (VITS/F5-TTS paragraphs); abstract ("of two non-autoregressive baselines one is immune, one is not").
   **Why it matters** — This is an appealing mechanistic narrative, but it rests on n=1 non-autoregressive model per postulated duration-representation strategy. A single counterexample and a single confirmatory case is not evidence of a general architectural principle, however the paper elevates it into the abstract's headline framing ("Nor is the failure peculiar to autoregressive decoding") rather than confining it to the hedged limitations paragraph where the same admission already appears ("the VITS/F5-TTS duration story rests on one model per strategy").
   **What would address it** — Either test a second token-level-duration and a second holistic-duration architecture, or soften the abstract's claim to match the single-checkpoint caveat already given in §5.

6. **Issue** — The mechanistic refutation in §4–5 (Lean-verified contraction bound tested and rejected; causal patch shows no behavioural effect) is a single-checkpoint, small-n result promoted to a panel-wide conclusion in the abstract.
   **Where** — §4 ("On Llasa-1B, over 29 repeated items..."); §5 ("rank-1 patch... n=36"); abstract ("We measure that premise on one checkpoint... and reject it there... so it never described that decoder at all").
   **Why it matters** — Under an adversarial reading, the interesting question is whether q<1 (contraction) holds on *any* of the other five checkpoints, since that is precisely the condition under which the theorem would bind. The paper never measures it elsewhere, so the abstract's implicit generalization ("the failure... is periodicity, not length" combined with "the mechanism... is false") leaves the actual causal account of the other five checkpoints' failures completely open. The causal-patch null result (0.00 log-count effect, n=36, one checkpoint) is being used to support a strong claim ("the decoder ignores it") that a single small-n intervention on one 1B model cannot carry.
   **What would address it** — Measure q (or at least run the cheaper causal-patch check) on at least one more checkpoint, ideally from a different family (e.g. Qwen3-TTS or XTTS-v2), before generalizing the refutation beyond Llasa-1B in the abstract.

7. **Issue** — The exclusion pipeline (342 + 133 + 19 generations dropped) was authored and tuned by the same team running the experiment, with only an aggregate robustness check rather than a per-rule directional audit.
   **Where** — §2 ("we exclude 342 in one template whose fillers the judge never transcribes, 133 stopped by our token budget... 19 degenerate"); S18 "every rule off" gives 61.7 points.
   **Why it matters** — The "every rule off" check (§3.2) is good practice and shows the aggregate effect isn't manufactured by exclusions, but it doesn't show whether any *individual* rule (especially the largest exclusion, the 342-item template drop) was constructed post hoc in a way that happened to favor the effect. The paper reports the budget rule "removes 10.6% of repeated generations against 5.6% of controls, working against the contrast" — useful — but the 342-item template exclusion's directional impact on the gap is not separately quantified in the main text.
   **What would address it** — Report the gap with each exclusion rule removed individually (not only all-off), so readers can see whether any single rule, not just the union, is doing disproportionate work.

### Minor concerns
- The title's blanket claim ("Periodicity, Not Length") is in tension with VITS showing *no* dissociation (§3.2) — the paper's own data say the effect is architecture-contingent, which a more hedged title would reflect.
- The Whisper-vs-CTC judge-validity audit is founded on very small samples (n=12 over 6 donors for the initial audit, n=24 for the CTC ratio) for a claim ("the obvious judge... is demonstrably the wrong one") that the whole measurement methodology depends on; general ASR-hallucination literature independently documents that LLM-based/autoregressive ASR systems are prone to hallucinating repetitive, ungrounded output (e.g. [2601.00890]), which is consistent with but not cited in support of the paper's Whisper-bias argument.
- The introduction's "six hundred sixty-six thousand six hundred sixty-six" example is presented as an illustrative anecdote but it's unclear whether this exact stimulus is part of the tested ladder or purely rhetorical.
- Numeric precision is inconsistent across sections — some results carry bootstrap/credible intervals, others (e.g., "76.7 [68.4, 84.4]" vs. plain point estimates like "14.0% against 92.9%") do not, making it hard to gauge uncertainty uniformly.
- The Lean-4 proof is described only prose-side in the main text ("free of sorry and with an axiom audit"); readers cannot assess the formalization's fidelity to the informal contraction argument without leaving the paper.
- "S27" and other supplement pointers (e.g., the unreportable Spanish arm) are referenced as settled facts in the Discussion without any in-paper summary of what they contain.

### Verdict
**Major revision** — the core empirical dissociation (repeated vs. length-matched-control rendering) is carefully instrumented and largely convincing, but the title claim conflates periodicity with verbatim-token identity, the strongest confirmatory evidence (pre-registration, CosyVoice-2 prediction) relies on unverifiable self-attestation, and the mechanistic refutation and one non-autoregressive "immunity" claim are generalized from single-checkpoint/single-model evidence beyond what the data support.

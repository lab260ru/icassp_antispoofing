### Summary

The paper asks whether autoregressive (and some non-autoregressive) TTS models' tendency to lose count on repeated text is driven by input *length* or by *repetition* per se, and tries to isolate a candidate mechanism (state-space contraction across repeated attention boundaries) for the failure. Using a 180-item "repetition ladder" of carrier sentences with a target word repeated k = 1–32 times, each paired with a length-matched, non-repeating control, the authors score renderings with a CTC recognizer (justified by an audit showing Whisper undercounts repeats) across six checkpoints spanning three architecture families (Llasa 1B/3B/8B, Qwen3-TTS 0.6B/1.7B, XTTS-v2), plus out-of-panel checks (CosyVoice 2, VITS, F5-TTS). The headline finding is a large, robust gap between exact-count accuracy on repeated (18.2%) versus control (94.3%) text at k ≥ 6, surviving decoding, judge, and exclusion-rule perturbations, and shown by a period-manipulation ladder to track periodicity rather than raw length. A Lean-4-verified bound predicting the failure via state-map contraction is then measured directly and refuted (estimated contraction factor q ≫ 1 in all measured checkpoints), leaving the underlying mechanism unresolved.

### Major concerns

1. **Issue** — The paper's claims are stated as general properties of "neural text-to-speech" (title, abstract, §3.1's "the limit tracks repetition, not length") but the evidence base is a narrow, largely monolingual, synthetic stimulus set over a small convenience sample of checkpoints, and the one attempt to extend outside that envelope (Spanish) failed.
   **Where** — Title and Abstract (general claim); §2 "Stimuli" (180 templated items, one language for the main ladder); §3.1 ("Nor is the contrast an English artefact... in Spanish Whisper again returns nothing... mean 0.93, S9, S27"); §5 Discussion ("the Spanish arm was unreportable, needing a better judge (S27)").
   **Why it matters** — The only genuine out-of-language test the authors ran produced no usable result — the very judge instrument the paper spent a full subsection validating (Whisper vs. CTC) breaks down again outside English, and the CTC recognizer used everywhere else (wav2vec2-large-960h-lv60-self) is itself an English-only acoustic model. This means the measurement apparatus, not just the phenomenon, has been validated in essentially one language. An adversarial reading has to ask: what would it take to falsify "repetition, not length" as a general TTS property? A demonstration that the effect is absent, inverted, or of very different magnitude in a language with different morphology (e.g., agglutinative repetition-marking, tonal reduplication) would falsify the generality claim outright, and the paper cannot rule this out — it can only report that its one attempt at cross-lingual validation did not work. Likewise, six checkpoints across three architecture families is a convenience sample, not a representative one (no Bark/Tortoise/StyleTTS2/Parler-TTS/GPT-SoVITS/Moshi-style systems, no diffusion-based TTS beyond flow-matching F5-TTS). The paper is honest that "three groups barely identify a group-level scale" and that under a wide prior the extrapolated cross-architecture gap "touches zero (−1.6)" — which is itself an admission that the population of TTS architectures is under-sampled relative to the strength of claims like "never described any decoder we measured."
   **What would address it** — Either qualify every generalizing claim (title included) to "English, codec/attention-based autoregressive and two non-autoregressive TTS systems we tested," or actually close the external-validity gap: get a working non-English judge (a multilingual CTC model, e.g. a multilingual wav2vec2/MMS checkpoint) and rerun the period/repetition ladders in at least 2–3 typologically distinct languages before asserting the effect is a general TTS property rather than an English-corpus-frequency artifact.

2. **Issue** — The stimulus ladder itself (a word repeated verbatim 6–32 times in a fixed carrier) is an extreme, low-frequency construction whose ecological relevance to real TTS deployment (audiobooks, voice assistants, dialogue systems) is asserted but never measured.
   **Where** — §2 "Stimuli"; motivating example in §1 ("six hundred sixty-six thousand six hundred sixty-six").
   **Why it matters** — Under the adversarial reading, one plausible account of the whole result is not "TTS models cannot count" as a generalizable cognitive/architectural limitation, but "TTS models are poorly calibrated on a specific out-of-training-distribution string pattern (long verbatim token repeats) that essentially never occurs in the text these models were trained/fine-tuned on for speech." The paper's own frequency check partially cuts against this (§5: "under an independent LM the control is the less probable text in 87% of pairs" — so the repeated text is *more* probable, yet still fails more), which is a genuinely useful control. But LM probability under "an independent LM" is not the same as frequency of *exact k-fold repetition* in TTS-relevant training corpora, which the paper explicitly declines to measure ("Text likelihood is not corpus frequency, so it is weakened, not closed," §5). The severity/generalization question — does this failure mode actually matter for any realistic deployment scenario, or only for adversarially constructed inputs — is left open, yet the framing (e.g., "TTS models loop, truncate and lose count on text that repeats a phrase many times") implies real-world relevance.
   **What would address it** — Either soften the framing to explicitly scope the claim to constructed/adversarial-style inputs, or run the ladder against naturally occurring repetition-heavy text (numerals, chants, poetry, children's books, IVR menu prompts) to show the effect generalizes beyond templated carriers.

3. **Issue** — The k ≥ 6 threshold used for the headline 18.2%/94.3% contrast was chosen post hoc, after inspecting where the two curves diverge.
   **Where** — §3.2: "The k ≥ 6 threshold was chosen after seeing where the arms diverge, which is why the abstract gives the unconditioned figure too (46.8 points from k = 1) and the specification curve varies it."
   **Why it matters** — This is a textbook garden-of-forking-paths move: a data-dependent cutpoint chosen to maximize the reported contrast, reported prominently in the abstract (18.2% vs 94.3%) alongside a much smaller unconditioned effect (46.8 points from k=1) that is arguably the less-cherry-picked number. The authors' own 420-specification robustness curve (34.4–86.4 points, median 70.5) is reassuring that the sign never flips, but the *specific* headline numbers in the abstract are exactly the ones inflated by the post hoc threshold, and a reader skimming the abstract will anchor on 18.2%/94.3%, not the median 70.5-point robustness envelope.
   **What would address it** — Lead the abstract with the pre-specified/unconditioned effect (k=1 onward) or the specification-curve median, and present the k≥6 contrast explicitly labeled as the maximal, post hoc-selected cell rather than as the primary headline statistic.

4. **Issue** — The single mechanistic story the paper argues for ("periodic ordering" driving the deficit) is admitted to hold for only one of two architecture families tested, undercutting the unifying claim implied by the title.
   **Where** — §3.1, "Does the ordering carry it?": "that pool averages two checkpoints gaining 17.8 points against XTTS-v2 losing 30.0, intervals excluding zero both ways... Periodic ordering drives Qwen, not the panel (S28)."
   **Why it matters** — If shuffling token order at fixed composition *helps* Qwen but *hurts* XTTS-v2, then "periodicity" is not a single unified explanatory variable across the panel — the mechanism is architecture-specific, and the title's clean dichotomy ("repetition, not length") is doing more rhetorical work than the data supports once you look inside the "repetition" bucket itself. Generalizing "the limit tracks repetition, not length" to architectures outside the tested three when even *within* the tested three the sub-mechanism reverses sign is a stronger claim than the evidence licenses.
   **What would address it** — Reframe the abstract/title claim to acknowledge that repetition (broadly) beats length as an explanation, but that periodic *ordering* itself is not a universal sub-mechanism — it is directionally inconsistent across families, and say so as prominently as the headline number.

5. **Issue** — Central claims are unverifiable from the main text alone; nearly every quantitative claim cites a supplementary section (S1–S28) not included here, and key methodological details (exact carrier sentences, filler pools, template count breakdown, speaker/voice conditioning) are never given in-text.
   **Where** — Throughout §2–§4 (e.g., "S6," "S8," "S9," "S10," "S12," "S13," "S14," "S15," "S16," "S17," "S18," "S19," "S20," "S22," "S26," "S27," "S28").
   **Why it matters** — A reviewer (or reader) cannot check whether, e.g., voice/speaker prompts were held constant or varied for zero-shot cloning models (XTTS-v2, F5-TTS), whether "three seeds" refers to sampling seed only or also reference-speaker variation, or what the actual carrier templates look like — all of which bear directly on external validity (does the effect hold across the speaker-embedding space, or only for whatever reference voice(s) were used?). The paper states code/data are public at a pinned tag, which is good practice, but the *submitted paper* should be self-contained enough to assess these questions without fetching the repository.
   **What would address it** — Add a compact table of stimulus examples and a one-line statement of speaker/voice-conditioning protocol per model family in the main text.

### Minor concerns

- The abstract is extremely dense with numbers (dozens of statistics in a few sentences) and lacks any signposting of which are primary vs. robustness-check results; this makes the paper's actual central claim harder to extract than it should be.
- "n=3" is used in the abstract to describe the architecture-family sample size in a way that could read as a token nod to statistical caution, but the number is buried in running text rather than flagged as a genuine limitation on generalization.
- CosyVoice 2 is introduced in §3.1 as a held-out validation of a Bayesian partial-pooling prediction interval, but it is still English-only and drawn from the same synthetic ladder — it does not test the cross-lingual or naturalistic-text generalization questions raised above; the paper's rhetoric ("from a fit committed before the checkpoint's first waveform existed") over-sells what this check actually validates (architecture-level extrapolation, not language/domain extrapolation).
- The Discussion's limitations paragraph (§5) is commendably candid ("Periodicity is not separated from training-set frequency... panel on three families, and the Spanish arm was unreportable") but these caveats should be reflected in the abstract's framing, not confined to a closing paragraph.
- Minor terminological inconsistency: "checkpoint" and "model" and "architecture family" are used somewhat interchangeably in places (e.g., "76.7 [68.4, 84.4], positive in all 6" vs. "75.4 [71.1, 78.9] points, all three positive") — a brief glossary sentence would help readers track which N applies to which statistic.
- Reference [7] ("companion" paper cited as the arXiv ID arXiv:2604.08591, 2026) has a date that is inconsistent with a plausible arXiv ID sequence for 2026; worth double-checking this citation is correctly formatted.

### Verdict

**Major revision** — the core manipulation (repetition vs. length) and the mechanistic refutation are genuinely valuable, but the paper's generalizing claims substantially outrun its near-single-language, small-convenience-sample evidence base, and the headline abstract statistic rests on a post hoc threshold; both need to be scoped down or the evidence base extended before the claims as titled/abstracted are supportable.

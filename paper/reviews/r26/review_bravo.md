### Summary
The paper asks whether autoregressive TTS models' well-known tendency to miscount, truncate, or loop on repeated text is driven by input *length* or by *repetition/periodicity* per se, and attempts to further explain any repetition-specific deficit mechanistically via a Lean-4-verified contraction bound on per-repetition hidden-state dynamics. Using a 180-item "repetition ladder" (target word repeated k times vs. length- and word-count-matched non-repeating controls) evaluated across six checkpoints spanning three architecture families (Llasa 1B/3B/8B, Qwen3-TTS 0.6B/1.7B, XTTS-v2), plus VITS and F5-TTS as non-autoregressive comparators, the authors report a large and robust gap between exact-count accuracy on repeated (18.2%) versus control (94.3%) text at k≥6, which survives decoding-rule sweeps, exclusion-rule ablations, and rescoring with four different ASR judges. A companion "period ladder" shows the deficit is graded with periodicity rather than a step function, and a formally verified contraction bound that was proposed as the mechanism is measured directly (via Jacobian power iteration) and found to be false in every checkpoint tested (q ≫ 1 throughout), so the paper ends up refuting its own candidate mechanism rather than confirming one.

### Major concerns

1. **Issue** — The paper's central causal claim — that repetition, not length, drives the deficit — rests on comparing "the same carrier and word count" but does not clearly establish that repeated vs. control stimuli are matched on other confounds that co-vary with periodicity, particularly local n-gram predictability/perplexity and phonetic/prosodic redundancy, which a length-only control does not neutralize.
   **Where** — Section 2 ("Stimuli"), Section 3.1, and the "Related work" paragraph in Section 5 which itself concedes "under an independent LM the control is the less probable text in 87% of pairs."
   **Why it matters** — The authors' own admission that the control is systematically *less* probable under an LM (in 87% of pairs) is presented as evidence "weakened, not closed" against a frequency confound, but this is actually a design problem, not a minor caveat: if repeated text is intrinsically more predictable (higher local self-similarity, lower surprisal per token), a model's autoregressive sampling/attention dynamics could favor early stopping, looping, or degenerate collapse on high-redundancy sequences for reasons unrelated to any "counting" mechanism at all — this is exactly the classic neural-text-degeneration phenomenon the introduction cites (Holtzman et al.) but does not rule out for TTS specifically. The paper conflates "repetition" as an abstract concept with "local statistical redundancy," and the period ladder (Section 3.1, "Periodicity, or verbatim identity?") only partially disentangles this, since even p=2 alternating sequences remain locally far more predictable than the non-adjacent-filler controls.
   **What would address it** — Report matched perplexity/surprisal statistics (under an independent LM, as already computed for the 87% figure) for repeated vs. control stimuli at each k, and ideally construct an additional control condition that preserves low-order redundancy without semantic repetition (e.g., syntactically parallel but lexically varied templates) to isolate "same word repeated" from "locally predictable token sequence."

2. **Issue** — Whisper is used as the initial judge, found biased, then swapped for a CTC recognizer, but the CTC recognizer has a known confound (blank-collapse merging adjacent identical words) that is *only* partially addressed, and the paper's own multi-recogniser robustness check does not include a fully independent, non-neural, non-ASR ground-truth channel (e.g., forced-alignment against known text or human transcription at scale, beyond the "165-clip" spot check).
   **Where** — Section 2 ("Behavioural measurement..."), Section 3.2 ("Nor is it the recogniser...").
   **Why it matters** — The judge-swap logic is self-referential: the paper defends the CTC judge's validity using *other* ASR systems (a second CTC model, a robust-finetuned one, Whisper again), all of which share broad architectural kinship (attention/CTC-based acoustic modeling trained on similar corpora) and could plausibly share correlated failure modes on repetitive/degenerate audio (e.g., looping artifacts in the TTS output itself producing acoustically ambiguous repeats that *any* ASR model would undercount). The claim that disagreements between judges run "with the opposite sign to the confound" is reassuring but is derived from a small audited subset (n=12–24 for the audit, 165 clips for the "checked by ear" spot check) rather than the full 1558-generation panel.
   **What would address it** — Report full-panel (not just audited-subset) agreement statistics across all four recognisers with confidence intervals, and ideally include a genuine human-transcription baseline on a properly powered random sample (not a stratified convenience sample chosen partly for coverage) to serve as an ASR-independent ground truth for the headline 18.2%/94.3% numbers.

3. **Issue** — The "specification curve" and robustness claims (420 specifications, gap 34.4–86.4 points) are presented as ruling out researcher-degree-of-freedom explanations, but the paper explicitly declines multiple-comparison correction ("we correct across none of them") without establishing that these specifications are non-overlapping or independent enough for the "median 70.5, none reverses sign" framing to substitute for formal inference.
   **Where** — Section 3.1, end of paragraph beginning "Across 420 specifications..."
   **Why it matters** — Declining correction is defensible when specifications are genuinely a single robustness envelope over one comparison (as the authors argue), but the paper never reports how many of the 420 specifications are actually near-duplicates of each other (e.g., varying only a k-threshold by one step) versus meaningfully distinct analytic choices, so "none reverses sign" could be trivially guaranteed by redundancy among specifications rather than genuine robustness across independent design choices.
   **What would address it** — Report the effective number of independent specifications (e.g., via clustering or a pre-registered smaller "forking paths" set) and show the distribution is not dominated by near-identical variants.

4. **Issue** — The k≥6 threshold used for the panel-summary headline numbers (Table 1, 18.2% vs 94.3%) is admitted to have been "chosen after seeing where the arms diverge," i.e., it is a post-hoc, data-dependent choice, and while the abstract also reports the unconditioned k=1 figure (46.8 points), the paper does not pre-register or justify k=6 on any independent theoretical basis.
   **Where** — Section 3.2, "The k ≥ 6 threshold was chosen after seeing where the arms diverge..."
   **Why it matters** — This is a textbook researcher-degrees-of-freedom concern that the authors partially disclose but do not fully mitigate — data-dependent threshold selection followed by headline reporting of exactly that threshold's effect size inflates the apparent magnitude of the reported gap relative to an a priori design. The disclosure is commendable but does not neutralize the statistical issue; a fixed pre-registered k could show a materially smaller gap.
   **What would address it** — Either pre-register the threshold in a future replication or report the full k-by-k curve as the primary result (already partially done in Fig. 1a) with the k≥6 summary explicitly labeled exploratory, rather than using it as the number that appears in the abstract's headline framing.

5. **Issue** — The partial-pooling extrapolation to "an architecture outside the panel" is validated against exactly one held-out architecture (CosyVoice 2), chosen because it was predicted to be "the design most likely to be immune" — yet it was *not* immune, which the authors treat as a successful out-of-sample prediction test, but this is a single data point against a model class explicitly selected for its favorable prior, which is a weak basis for claiming generalization.
   **Where** — Section 3.1, CosyVoice 2 paragraph.
   **Why it matters** — With only n=3 architecture families in the core panel and one confirmatory case, the "prospectively registered" prediction interval [5.9, 93.3] is so wide that almost any positive gap would have "confirmed" it; treating this as validating the mixed-effects extrapolation overstates the strength of the generalization claim, especially since the authors themselves note "three groups barely identify a group-level scale" and that under a wider prior the interval touches zero.
   **What would address it** — Test at least 2–3 more held-out architectures beyond CosyVoice 2 before claiming the partial-pooling extrapolation is validated, or soften the "checkable rather than asserted" framing to acknowledge this is one confirmatory anecdote, not a validated generalization.

### Minor concerns
- The abstract and Section 4 report q = 21.0–347.7 as ruling out contraction, but no statistical treatment (CI, variance across seeds) is given for these estimates in the main text beyond "a bootstrap floor of 3.3" — the reader cannot assess estimator stability from the text alone.
- The claim that Qwen3-TTS's larger checkpoint's "null is not an artifact of conservative decoding" because it is "the most stochastic family" is asserted but the actual sampling temperature/top-p values are relegated to a supplement (S19) not shown here, making the claim unverifiable from the text.
- "n=3" is used both for architecture families (Section abstract) and elsewhere for donor counts in the Whisper audit (n=12 over 6 donors) — the overloaded use of small, differently-scoped n's throughout (n=3, n=6, n=12, n=18, n=24, n=90, n=465, n=1539) makes it hard to track which claims rest on genuinely small samples versus the full panel.
- The rank-1 patch experiment in Discussion (Section 5) reports a positive result "on Qwen3-TTS-1.7B neither does a carrier direction it decodes perfectly (1.46%)" — this sentence is difficult to parse and would benefit from rewriting.
- Self-citation [7] ("From dispersion to attraction...") is dated 2026 (arXiv:2604.08591), i.e., a future/inconsistent arXiv ID format relative the current date context, which should be checked before camera-ready.
- The related-work citation to Venkatesh [13], arXiv:2605.09239, is also a 2026 preprint used to support a claim about text models' count staying "decodable late" — worth double-checking this ID and date are correct at camera-ready, since malformed IDs in this range are easy to introduce by transcription error.

### Verdict
**Major revision** — the length-vs-repetition manipulation and multi-judge robustness work are genuinely careful and largely convincing, but the predictability/redundancy confound between repeated and control stimuli (acknowledged but not resolved), the post-hoc k-threshold, and the single-case out-of-panel generalization test need to be addressed before the headline causal claim can be accepted as fully isolating "repetition" from all correlated statistical properties of repeated text.

### Summary
The paper asks whether neural TTS models' tendency to miscount, truncate, or loop on repeated phrases is driven by input *length* or by *periodicity* per se, and attempts to answer this with a 180-item "repetition ladder" in which each repeated-word stimulus (word repeated k times) is paired with a length- and word-count-matched control in which the repeated word is replaced by distinct fillers. Tested across six checkpoints spanning three autoregressive TTS architecture families plus two non-autoregressive baselines, the authors report a 75.4-point (family-level) gap in exact-count-match rate between repeated and control conditions at k≥6, argue this survives judge choice, decoding changes, and exclusion-rule ablations, and then use a Lean-4-verified contraction bound to motivate a mechanistic account — which they measure directly (via Jacobian spectral radius) and report as empirically false in every checkpoint tested (q ranging 21.0–347.7, never <1). The headline claim is that the deficit "tracks periodicity, not length."

### Major concerns

1. **Issue** — The core manipulation confounds periodicity with verbatim lexical/token identity (and its correlate, training-corpus frequency), and no condition in the ladder disentangles these, despite this being logically necessary for the paper's central claim.
   **Where** — Method (Stimuli), and explicitly conceded in Discussion ("What the design does not separate... periodicity and verbatim token identity vary together here, so 'periodicity' is the pair, and neither is separated from training-set frequency").
   **Why it matters** — The title and abstract assert "periodicity, not length" as the resolved variable, but the experiment as designed cannot distinguish "the model struggles when the *same token* recurs many times" from "the model struggles with *periodic structure* regardless of token identity" from "the model struggles with text that is rare/OOD relative to its training distribution" (k-fold identical-word repetition is intrinsically much rarer in natural speech corpora than a matched sentence with k distinct words). All three predict the same directional result on this exact stimulus set. A genuinely periodicity-isolating design would need a condition that is periodic but *not* verbatim-identical (e.g., an alternating A-B-A-B-A-B pattern, or a periodically recurring paraphrase) contrasted against both the verbatim-repeat and the distinct-filler conditions. Without that third arm, "periodicity" is asserted, not demonstrated — the paper has shown "verbatim repetition vs. lexical variety," which is a narrower and previously-suspected confound (the representational-collapse account the paper itself cites via Barbero et al. is a token/prefix-convergence account, not strictly a periodicity account), not shown that periodic-but-varied text produces the same failure.
   **What would address it** — Add a periodic-but-non-identical control arm (alternating distinct tokens in a fixed period) and show the deficit still appears at the same period length even when no single token repeats verbatim. Absent that, retitle the contribution as isolating verbatim-token-repetition from length, not periodicity from length.

2. **Issue** — The paper's "pre-specified" framing rests entirely on internal, self-controlled evidence (git commit hashes) rather than any independently verifiable pre-registration.
   **Where** — Section 3.1 header ("Pre-specified: the limit tracks periodicity, not length"), the CosyVoice 2 confirmation ("from a fit committed before the checkpoint's first waveform existed (commit 6f34945, so the ordering is checkable rather than asserted; S16)"), and the k≥6 threshold used throughout Table 1 and the headline 75.4-point figure.
   **Why it matters** — A commit hash in a repository the authors themselves control and could, in principle, rebase, force-push, or selectively retain is not equivalent to a timestamped external registration (OSF, AsPredicted, a registered report). This is a materially weaker evidentiary standard than the "pre-specified" label implies to a reader, and the paper does not explain what prevents post-hoc curation of the git history before the "public" tag was cut. Separately, no rationale is given for why k=6 was chosen as the reporting threshold (rather than k=4 or k=8) — if this was chosen after inspecting where the two curves visibly diverge in Fig. 1(a), the headline number is not independent of the data that motivated the choice, and the extensive 420-specification robustness sweep (which the authors themselves note does not correct for multiplicity) does not resolve that: robustness-under-forking-paths establishes sign stability, not that k=6 was decided in advance of seeing the curve.
   **What would address it** — Provide an actual timestamped pre-registration (or an honest statement that k≥6 was chosen post hoc for effect-size reasons, with the exploratory/confirmatory distinction reserved for genuinely blind tests like the CosyVoice 2 addition). State explicitly whether k=6 was selected before or after visual inspection of the divergence.

3. **Issue** — Token-budget-truncated generations (133 of 2052) are pooled into the same exclusion accounting as judge failures and degenerate outputs, without confirming the truncation rate is balanced across repeated vs. control arms.
   **Where** — Method, "Behavioural measurement" paragraph: "133 stopped by our token budget rather than by the model."
   **Why it matters** — If repeated-word stimuli are, by the paper's own hypothesis, more prone to looping (i.e., generating pathologically long output), they are mechanically more likely to hit an experimenter-imposed token ceiling than their length-matched controls — meaning the truncation itself is downstream of the very effect under test, not an independent nuisance. If truncated repeated-arm items are excluded (rather than scored as failures) at a higher rate than truncated control-arm items, this could either inflate or deflate the reported exact-match gap depending on how truncation is scored, and the paper does not report the per-arm breakdown of these 133 exclusions.
   **What would address it** — Report the repeated-vs-control split of token-budget-truncated items, and show the headline gap is stable whether these are excluded, scored as failures, or scored using whatever partial count the truncated output contains.

4. **Issue** — Decoding hyperparameters ("shipped defaults") differ across architecture families and are not matched, which is a live confound for any cross-architecture claim (the partial-pooling model treating architecture as a random effect, and the "immune" vs. "not immune" architecture-level conclusions).
   **Where** — Method ("Code, data, settings": "Sampling uses each system's shipped defaults... they make Qwen3-TTS the most stochastic family"), Section 3.2 (penalty sweep on only 2 of the architectures).
   **Why it matters** — The within-model repeated-vs-control comparison is a legitimate paired design (same model, same decoding config, differing only in text), so the core exact-match gap is defensible per checkpoint. But cross-architecture comparisons — e.g., "Qwen3-TTS-1.7B... has an equally expansive map" contrasted with lower-scale checkpoints, or CosyVoice 2's partial immunity — are made across systems whose sampling temperature/top-p/penalty regimes were never equalized. A model that samples more conservatively by default will mechanically show a smaller exact-match gap regardless of any architectural property being invoked (alignment supervision, contraction, etc.). The repetition-penalty sweep addresses this for only 2 of 6+ checkpoints.
   **What would address it** — Either match decoding hyperparameters across all checkpoints for the cross-architecture claims, or restrict architecture-level claims (e.g., about CosyVoice 2's "repetition-aware sampling") to the within-model paired comparison and explicitly flag cross-architecture rank comparisons as confounded by decoding config.

5. **Issue** — The judge (CTC recogniser) is validated for accuracy against synthetic concatenative ground truth but never against human listeners, and the exclusion rules were authored by the same team that formed the hypothesis, with no blinded or independently-audited scoring pipeline.
   **Where** — Discussion ("the judge is validated against concatenative ground truth, not listeners"); Method (exclusion rules).
   **Why it matters** — The paper does an admirable job stress-testing judge choice against the specific artifact it worried about (autoregressive bias replicating the effect), but construct validity for the primary outcome — "did the speech actually render the correct count as a human would judge it" — is never established. Concatenative ground truth is a much easier, cleaner test case than actual TTS output with its own prosodic idiosyncrasies (e.g., co-articulation across repeated words, which could fool a CTC recogniser's blank-collapse mechanism in ways the audit doesn't probe, since the audit uses non-model-generated audio).
   **What would address it** — A small human-listener validation on a stratified sample of actual model outputs (the paper already ships "a stratified 165-clip sample" for spot-checking by ear — formalize this into an actual inter-rater reliability statistic against the CTC judge's automated calls, rather than leaving it as an unquantified "can be checked by ear" affordance).

### Minor concerns
- The claim "the manipulation here separates them by construction" (Introduction) is stronger than what Discussion later concedes; the two sections are in tension and a reader skimming only the Introduction/Abstract would come away with an unwarrantedly clean picture.
- Table 1's Qwen3-TTS-1.7B row shows 0.0% median count-error gap but a 60-point exact-match gap (38.9% vs 98.9%) — this is explained once in prose but is worth a footnote directly on the table, since it's a genuinely counterintuitive juxtaposition of two headline metrics disagreeing about the same checkpoint.
- The "666,000,666" example in the Introduction is vivid but is never explicitly confirmed as part of the 180-item ladder ("number-phrase families" is mentioned only in passing under Stimuli) — clarify whether this specific example was tested or is purely illustrative.
- "S9, S27" and similar supplement pointers for the Spanish replication are referenced but the Spanish arm's sample size (n) is never given in the main text (Discussion admits "a Spanish arm was unreportable" without saying what "unreportable" means quantitatively).
- The Lean-4 refutation section is compelling as a piece of intellectual honesty but its framing ("we keep the bound because it motivated the manipulation") reads as slightly self-congratulatory given the bound is reported as having zero explanatory value for Section 3 — a reader could reasonably ask why 1.5 pages are spent on a mechanism the authors themselves show never applied to any decoder tested, autoregressive or not, on either arm.
- Minor phrasing: "Not the recogniser, the objection this design most has to answer" — the section header logic (embedded declarative claims as headers, e.g. "3.2. Pre-specified: not the decoding rule, judge, or exclusions") is stylistically unusual for an ICASSP submission and occasionally reads as asserting the conclusion before presenting the evidence.

### Verdict
**Major revision** — the empirical robustness work (judge validation, decoding sweep, exclusion ablation, the mechanistic refutation) is unusually thorough, but the central "periodicity, not length" claim is not actually isolated by the reported design (it conflates periodicity with verbatim token identity/OOD frequency), the "pre-specified" claims rest on self-controlled commit history rather than independent registration, and the token-budget truncation asymmetry between arms is left unaudited — each of which the authors could address without new architectures, primarily by adding one missing control condition and reporting a few currently-omitted breakdowns.

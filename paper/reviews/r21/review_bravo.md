### Summary
The paper asks whether neural TTS models' well-known tendency to miscount, loop, or truncate repeated phrases is driven by input *length* or by input *periodicity*, and isolates the two with a "repetition ladder": 180 items where a target word recurs k times, each paired with a length- and word-count-matched control in which the k repeats are replaced by distinct fillers. Across six checkpoints spanning three architecture families (Llasa 1B/3B/8B, Qwen3-TTS 0.6B/1.7B, XTTS-v2), plus two non-autoregressive baselines (VITS, F5-TTS) and a held-out fourth architecture (CosyVoice 2), the authors report that repeated items are rendered exactly in 18.2% of generations versus 94.3% for length-matched controls at k≥6 — a gap they show survives greedy decoding, an order-of-magnitude repetition-penalty sweep, all exclusion rules turned off, and four different ASR judges. They further show the "obvious" judge (Whisper) is itself biased by the same autoregressive dynamics under study (scoring 0.19 of true count on ground-truth periodic audio vs. 1.00 for a CTC recognizer), and they formally state and then empirically refute a Lean-4-verified contraction-based mechanism for the failure, finding the relevant Jacobian expansive (q ≈ 21–348) on both repeated and control text alike.

### Major concerns

1. **Issue** — The paper's title and headline claim ("periodicity, not length") generalize far beyond what the sampled population of languages and architectures actually supports.
   **Where** — Abstract; Sec. 2 "Stimuli"/"Models"; Sec. 3.1 family/checkpoint statistics; Discussion, "What the design does not separate."
   **Why it matters** — The core panel is English-only; the one attempt at a second language is explicitly described as producing results that were "unreportable" (Discussion, referencing S27), so the periodicity-vs-length dissociation is essentially demonstrated in one language. On the architecture side, "six checkpoints" reduces to three genuinely distinct designs for the main statistical model, and the paper itself acknowledges this by modeling clustering rather than counting checkpoints. The posterior-predictive extrapolation to "an architecture outside the panel" (65.3 [5.9, 93.3], P=0.996) is reassuringly confirmed by CosyVoice 2 — a genuinely good practice — but the same hierarchical model, under "the widest prior on group-level scales," has its own interval touch zero (−1.6, P=0.956). A random-effects variance component estimated from three or four clusters is only weakly identified, so the confidently stated 76.4-point pooled gap and the prior-sensitivity caveat that nearly reverses it cannot both be given equal interpretive weight, yet the paper presents the point estimate as primary and the caveat as an aside. The mechanistic refutation in Sec. 4 (q never contracts, "in any checkpoint, lag or sub-stack") is measured on only five checkpoints from two families — a similarly thin base for a claim phrased as if it applies to TTS decoders in general.
   **What would address it** — Either (a) run the full ladder (not just the ASR-judge audit) on at least one more language and report it regardless of outcome, and add architecturally distinct TTS families beyond neural-codec LMs and the two NAR baselines (e.g., a diffusion/flow-based system beyond F5-TTS, an FastSpeech2-style duration-predictor system) to broaden the population the mechanistic claim is tested against; or (b) scope the title/abstract explicitly to "English, neural-codec-LM TTS" and present the CosyVoice 2 replication and prior-sensitivity result with equal prominence rather than folding the null result into a subordinate clause.

2. **Issue** — The design admits, in its own limitations paragraph, that it cannot separate "periodicity" from verbatim-token-identity repetition or from training-corpus frequency, yet the title asserts periodicity as the locus of the failure.
   **Where** — Discussion, "What the design does not separate, and other limits" ("Periodicity and verbatim token identity vary together here... an out-of-distribution account predicts the same dissociation").
   **Why it matters** — An equally consistent story is that k-fold exact-token repetition is simply rare in training data (an OOD/frequency account), which the control does not rule out, since the control's distinct fillers are individually common even when the sentence-level pattern is unusual. The paper's own contribution list claims "periodicity" as the isolated variable, which is stronger than the evidence licenses given this unresolved confound.
   **What would address it** — A further manipulation that holds periodicity fixed while varying verbatim identity (e.g., a period-2 or period-3 cycle of two or three distinct but still-patterned words) versus a matched non-cyclic-but-equally-rare sequence, to pull apart "periodic pattern" from "single token repeated" from "rare n-gram."

3. **Issue** — The CTC-judge validation and the informative exclusion of one template rest on small, narrow calibration samples.
   **Where** — Sec. 2, "Behavioural measurement" (Whisper audited on n=12 over 6 donors; CTC on n=24, concatenative audio); Sec. 2, "Of 2052 panel generations we exclude 342 in one template whose fillers the judge never transcribes."
   **Why it matters** — The entire behavioral pipeline, and hence every headline number in Sec. 3, is downstream of the claim that the CTC judge is (near-)unbiased. That claim is established on synthetically concatenated audio with known ground truth, not on genuine model outputs, which have different coarticulation and prosodic artifacts than spliced clips; a judge that is unbiased on clean splices could still behave differently on genuine model loops. Separately, excluding 342/2052 (~16.7%) generations because the judge cannot transcribe that template's fillers is a form of informative missingness whose relationship to the main effect (does that template disproportionately contain hard cases for either arm?) is not characterized, beyond the aggregate robustness check that all-exclusions-off still yields a positive gap.
   **What would address it** — Validate the CTC/Whisper contrast on a larger sample of genuine (non-concatenative) model generations against independent human transcription, and report the excluded template's per-arm effect size alongside the included ones rather than only an aggregate sensitivity bound.

4. **Issue** — The causal-patching result that anchors the paper's mechanistic conclusion ("the decoder ignores [the count coordinate]") is a single-checkpoint finding presented in the Discussion with more generality than it supports.
   **Where** — Sec. 5, "The rival the evidence favours" (rank-1 patch results, "The patch is a single-checkpoint result on Llasa-1B, where the Jacobian measurement now spans five").
   **Why it matters** — The paper is commendably explicit about this limitation in the same paragraph, but the surrounding prose ("On this checkpoint the count coordinate transplants cleanly and the decoder ignores it") reads as though it licenses a general claim about the class of decoders studied, when it is n=1 checkpoint out of six in the panel and out of five with Jacobian measurements.
   **What would address it** — Replicate the rank-1 patch on at least one more checkpoint (ideally from a different family, e.g. Qwen3-TTS-1.7B, which counts correctly and would be a strong contrast case) before treating "the decoder ignores it" as anything beyond a single-model observation.

### Minor concerns
- Reference [2] renders "VALL-E" as "V ALL-E" — likely a PDF-extraction artifact but should be checked in the camera-ready.
- Two of the paper's load-bearing citations — the companion spectral-hallucination paper [7] and Venkatesh [13], which the Discussion says "established the representational half" the current paper builds on — carry 2026 arXiv IDs (2604.xxxxx, 2605.xxxxx) indicating very recent, likely non-peer-reviewed preprints; leaning on these for core framing ("Closest is representational collapse... we add the causal half") without independent corroboration is a citation-stability risk.
- The prose is extremely dense with inline statistics (e.g., the paragraph beginning "Our six checkpoints are only three architecture families..." packs 7+ distinct interval estimates into a few sentences); a summary table analogous to Table 1 for the robustness/sensitivity results (420 specifications, prior-sensitivity, exclusion sweep) would substantially aid readability.
- Supplementary section references (S1–S27) are used extensively as load-bearing evidence (e.g., the Lean proof, the mixed-effects model, the causal patch) but the supplement itself is not part of the reviewed text, so several claims (e.g., "axiom audit," "the theorem's s_m has no single-layer realisation") cannot be checked here.
- Table 1: Qwen3-TTS-1.7B shows 0.0% median count error but only 38.9% exact-match rate; worth a one-line clarifying note that median-zero error is consistent with a wide, roughly symmetric error distribution rather than near-universal exactness, since a casual read of the abstract's "-12.5%... 0.0%" framing could suggest otherwise.
- No explicit conflict-of-interest or ethics statement (e.g., voice-cloning/dual-use considerations for the zero-shot TTS systems used), which is otherwise standard for this venue.

### Verdict
**Major revision** — the study is methodologically careful and unusually self-critical (held-out architecture prediction, judge-bias audit, mechanistic refutation with hedged bounds), but its universal-sounding claims about "periodicity" as an architecture-general TTS phenomenon are not yet earned given the near-total English-only, three-to-four-architecture evidentiary base and the admitted periodicity/verbatim-identity/frequency confound.

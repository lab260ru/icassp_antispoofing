# Review (reviewer: delta)

### Summary
The paper asks whether autoregressive TTS models mis-render repeated phrases (e.g., "say a word twelve times") because the input is *long* or because it is *periodic*, and tries to isolate the mechanism with a "repetition ladder": 180 stimuli where a target word recurs k times, each paired with a length-matched control in which the k repeats are replaced by k distinct fillers. Across six checkpoints spanning three architecture families (Llasa 1B/3B/8B, Qwen3-TTS 0.6B/1.7B, XTTS-v2), plus VITS, F5-TTS and later CosyVoice 2 as auxiliary conditions, models render the repeated condition exactly 18.2% of the time versus 94.3% for controls at k≥6, a gap the authors attribute to periodicity rather than length, sequence position, decoding rule, or judge artifact. They further formalize and then reject a "contraction" mechanism (a Lean-4-verified bound on state-map Lipschitz separability) via empirical Jacobian estimation, finding the boundary-to-boundary map is expansive (q ≫ 1) in every checkpoint, including ones that count correctly — so the proposed mechanism is falsified even as the behavioral phenomenon stands.

### Major concerns

1. **Issue** — The manipulation does not separate periodicity from training-distribution rarity (out-of-distribution frequency), so the title claim "periodicity, not length" is not actually established against the most obvious rival confound, which the authors themselves name but never test against.
   **Where** — Section 5, "What the design does not separate": *"neither [periodicity nor verbatim token identity] is separated from training-set frequency: k-fold repetition is far rarer in speech corpora than a matched distinct-word sentence, and an out-of-distribution account predicts the same dissociation."*
   **Why it matters** — This is not a minor caveat; it is a live alternative explanation that predicts the identical result pattern reported in Fig. 1 and Table 1. If a model simply renders text badly whenever the token sequence is far from its training distribution — irrespective of whether that atypicality takes a periodic form — then the entire headline dissociation (periodicity vs. length) reduces to a dissociation between in-distribution and out-of-distribution text, which is a much less interesting and much less novel claim. The paper's title and abstract assert the stronger, periodicity-specific reading without ruling out the weaker one. Under an adversarial reading, nothing in Section 3 falsifies the OOD-frequency account; it is only acknowledged, not tested.
   **What would address it** — A stimulus that is periodic but *not* rare in training (e.g., naturally occurring repeated function words, or repeated short high-frequency tokens like "no no no no…") crossed against one that is aperiodic but equally rare (e.g., a syntactically anomalous but non-repeating filler sequence) would let periodicity and corpus-frequency vary independently. Without that 2×2, the paper's central contrast is confounded, not isolated.

2. **Issue** — The "pre-specified" analyses throughout Section 3 are not independently verifiable pre-registrations; they rest on the authors' own git history (a mutable, self-hosted artifact), and the reported effect size is itself highly sensitive to researcher-set exclusion rules.
   **Where** — Section 3.1 header "Pre-specified"; the CosyVoice 2 prediction traced to "commit 6f34945" in the authors' own repository (S16); the exclusion-rule sensitivity analysis reporting 61.7 points with exclusions off vs. 76.1 with them on.
   **Why it matters** — A commit hash in a repository the authors control and could rewrite prior to submission is weak evidence of genuine pre-registration; there is no external timestamp (e.g., OSF, a public pre-registration registry, or a third-party attestation). Separately, the ~15-point (≈20%) swing in the headline gap depending on whether exclusion rules are applied shows the reported number is materially shaped by researcher-defined post hoc criteria (342 items dropped for a template the judge can't transcribe, 133 for token budget, 19 "degenerate"). The paper asserts these exclusions are "rule-based and blind to arm and score" (Section 5) but never describes the rule-authoring process, when the rules were fixed relative to seeing outcome data, or how "degenerate" was operationalized before results were inspected.
   **What would address it** — Either point to a genuinely external, timestamped registration (OSF/AsPredicted-style) made before any checkpoint was run, or drop the "pre-specified" framing and present the analysis honestly as exploratory-with-robustness-checks (the 420-specification curve already does useful work here and doesn't need the pre-registration language to be convincing).

3. **Issue** — The paper omits a foundational, closely related prior work on exactly the failure mode it studies: attention-alignment brittleness causing missing/repeated words in autoregressive TTS on long or out-of-distribution text.
   **Where** — Introduction, citations [1–4] for the "stability problem" and "attention supervision" fixes.
   **Why it matters** — Badlani et al., *"One TTS Alignment To Rule Them All"* [2108.10447], is squarely on-topic: it documents that autoregressive TTS attention alignments "tend to be brittle and often fail to generalize to long utterances and out-of-domain text, leading to missing or repeating words," and proposes an alignment mechanism (RAD-TTS-style) as the fix — precisely the class of remedy the introduction gestures at ("fixes proposed at the level of attention supervision") without citing its most direct exemplar. Given that the paper's own mechanism chapter (Section 4) is built around attention-flattening under repeated spans, failing to engage with the literature that already links attention brittleness to missing/repeated words on OOD text is a significant related-work gap; it also bears on the novelty claim, since the current paper's "isolation of periodicity from length" needs to be positioned against this alignment-robustness literature, not just against exposure-bias and neural-degeneration references.
   **What would address it** — Cite and discuss this work explicitly, and clarify whether the periodicity effect reported here is distinguishable from — or a special case of — the alignment-brittleness phenomenon Badlani et al. already characterize for long/OOD text.

4. **Issue** — The judge-validation samples that license trusting the CTC recogniser over Whisper are very small relative to the scale of claims built on top of them.
   **Where** — Section 2, "Behavioural measurement": Whisper audited on n=12 (six donors) for English, CTC audited on n=24; Spanish audit "mean 0.93, S9, S27" with no n given in the main text.
   **Why it matters** — Every headline number in Section 3 (75.4 points, 94.3% vs. 18.2%, the mixed-effects estimate of 76.4 points) depends on trusting the CTC judge's scoring function across 1558 retained generations. That trust is calibrated on an audit of only 12–24 items of synthetic concatenative audio constructed by the authors themselves, not organically produced TTS failures, and not judged by independent, blinded human listeners. A judge validated on a handful of self-constructed calibration items, then applied to score thousands of real generations that determine a paper's central claim, is a thin foundation; the audit sample should scale with claim strength, not sit at n≈12–24.
   **What would address it** — Report inter-rater reliability between the CTC judge and blinded human transcribers/counters on a substantially larger, randomly drawn (not authors'-choice) sample of actual model generations (not just synthetic ground-truth audio), stratified by k and arm.

5. **Issue** — The stratified 165-clip "checked by ear" sample is described only as available for inspection, not as a blinded, independently rated validation study, so there is no genuine human-perception check on the automated scoring pipeline at scale.
   **Where** — Section 2, "Code, data, settings": *"a stratified 165-clip sample spanning every model, k band and outcome ships with each clip's transcript and scoring decision, so a judgement can be checked by ear."*
   **Why it matters** — This is a reproducibility affordance (readers *can* check), not a validation result (no one *did* check, blind, and report agreement). Given that Concern 4 already shows the judge is calibrated on a tiny sample, and given that the paper's entire argument in Section 2 is built around "the obvious judge is wrong, choose the CTC judge instead," the absence of any blinded human-rater agreement statistic on model output (as opposed to synthetic ground-truth audio) leaves the core measurement instrument unvalidated against the actual criterion of interest — what a human listener would count.
   **What would address it** — Have 2–3 blinded raters independently count repetitions on a random subsample of real model outputs (not just synthetic ground truth) and report agreement (e.g., Cohen's κ or ICC) between raters and between raters and the CTC judge.

6. **Issue** — The paper's statistical philosophy is internally inconsistent: it explicitly disclaims multiple-comparison correction across 420 specifications ("our claims rest on effect sizes, not p-values"), yet elsewhere reports precise inferential quantities in p-value-like language (P=0.996, P=0.956) and confidence/credible intervals whose rhetorical force depends on exactly the frequentist/Bayesian discipline it disclaims elsewhere.
   **Where** — Section 3.1: "we correct across none of them: … our claims rest on effect sizes, not p-values" immediately followed a few sentences later, and earlier, by "P=0.996 — enough to place the effect" and "touches zero (−1.6, P=0.956)."
   **Why it matters** — Readers cannot have it both ways: either the numeric precision of P=0.996/0.956 matters for the argument (in which case the 420 unadjusted specification tests and multiple post hoc model comparisons elsewhere in the paper need multiplicity treatment, or at least an explicit justification for why they don't), or effect-size robustness is what's doing the work (in which case the specific posterior-probability figures should be de-emphasized rather than used as the crux of the "fourth architecture" prediction check in the very next paragraph). As written, the paper selectively invokes formal inferential rigor when convenient and disclaims it when convenient.
   **What would address it** — Pick one statistical register and apply it consistently; if effect-size robustness is the actual epistemic warrant, drop or clearly bracket the p-value-flavored numbers, or explain why they are exempt from the same "no correction" logic.

7. **Issue** — Despite grounding the headline in "three architecture families" (with CosyVoice 2 as a fourth, later addition), the paper generalizes in its title and abstract to "neural Text-to-Speech" broadly, and language coverage is essentially English-only, with the Spanish arm explicitly described as inadequate.
   **Where** — Title/Abstract ("Locating the Repetition-Counting Failure in Neural Text-to-Speech"); Section 5: *"a Spanish arm was unreportable, an exact-rate contrast needing a judge accurate enough to pin the control side near one (S27)."*
   **Why it matters** — A claim framed at the level of "neural TTS" architecture-agnostically is supported by 3(+1) families, one language with usable statistics, and a partial-pooling posterior whose 95% interval for an out-of-panel architecture spans [5.9, 93.3] — i.e., consistent with anywhere from a negligible to a near-total effect. The authors are commendably candid about this ("enough to place the effect, not to exclude a fourth architecture lacking it"), but the title's unqualified generality outruns what four architectures and one well-measured language license.
   **What would address it** — Either scope the title/abstract to the architectures and language actually tested, or add at least one more architecturally distinct family and a properly powered non-English arm before making the general claim.

### Minor concerns
- The paper reports a dense cluster of very similar but distinct headline numbers (75.4, 76.4, 76.7, 76.1, 69.6, 67.6, 65.1–76.7, 61.7…) without a summary table collecting "which number is under which condition" — this materially hurts readability and invites misquotation.
- The GitHub repo name ("icassp_antispoofing") is noted as predating the study; this is disclosed but still an odd artifact worth a one-line footnote explaining the repo's history, since a repo name mismatched to its content can raise reviewer suspicion about provenance/reuse.
- Section 4's Lean-4 verification is reported as rejected in every measured case; given that, its prominence (a full section, plus abstract real estate) versus its actual explanatory contribution ("it explains nothing in Section 3") could be trimmed or moved to supplement, letting Section 3's behavioral result carry more weight.
- No conflict-of-interest or funding statement is present in the provided text.
- "n=3" is used both for architecture families as a clustering unit and, confusingly, is invoked elsewhere for very different quantities (e.g., "n=12 over 6 donors"); tightening notation would help.
- Reference [7], the "companion" spectral-hallucination paper, is dated 2026 with an arXiv ID (2604.08591) that would need reader verification since it postdates the review window of many venues — worth double-checking before camera-ready.

### Verdict
**Major revision** — the core behavioral dissociation is interesting and the robustness battery is unusually thorough, but the headline "periodicity, not length" claim is undercut by an admitted-but-untested confound with training-distribution frequency, the "pre-specified" framing rests on unverifiable self-hosted provenance, and the judge/human-validation evidence base is too thin for the weight the paper places on it.

### Summary
The paper asks why autoregressive (AR) text-to-speech models miscount repeated words/phrases, and proposes a formal explanation: if softmax attention over k repeated spans dilutes to Θ(1/k) (a machine-checked lemma) and the resulting per-repetition decoder update contracts (an unverified empirical assumption), then no Lipschitz readout — including the model's own stop head — can distinguish repetition counts beyond a computable horizon N* (a machine-checked theorem, Lean 4/mathlib, sorry-free). Empirically, across six checkpoints from three TTS families (Llasa 1B/3B/8B, XTTS-v2, Qwen3-TTS 0.6B/1.7B), scored by a CTC recognizer chosen to avoid the AR bias of Whisper, repeated-word items are undercounted (median -8.3% at k≥6, exact only 18.2% of the time) while length-matched non-repeating controls are essentially always exact (94.3%). Within k≤32 the deficit is proportional rather than saturating (contradicting the theorem's constant-horizon prediction); a post-hoc extension to k=128 on four checkpoints finds saturation with the repeated-condition capacity 3.5× lower than controls. The authors report that two independent attempts to measure the theorem's key parameter (the contraction factor q) failed, and that the dilution lemma's dose-response prediction is sign-reversed in the data, while a single linear-probe experiment on one checkpoint supports the theorem's conclusion (though not its mechanism) below vs. above the horizon.

### Major concerns

1. **Issue** — The empirical population underwriting every generalizable claim ("in every checkpoint and every family," "autoregressive text-to-speech models") is extremely narrow: six checkpoints, effectively three independent architectures once within-family scale variants are collapsed, all English, all built from a single 180-item template pool.
   **Where** — §3 "Models"; abstract ("median count error of -8.3%… in every checkpoint and every family"); §4.3.
   **Why it matters** — Three of six checkpoints (Llasa 1B/3B/8B) are scale variants of one Llama-over-X-codec2 architecture, and two more (Qwen3-TTS 0.6B/1.7B) are scale variants of a second; the "three families" framing overstates architectural diversity. No non-English item is tested despite the field already treating multilingual coverage as central to TTS hallucination benchmarking (e.g., a comprehensive multilingual instruction-following TTS benchmark, [2604.17958]) — tokenization, phonotactics, and rhyme structure differ enough across languages that a periodicity effect measured only in English carrier sentences may not transfer. More fundamentally, Assumption 2 (contraction) is stated as "an empirical claim about a particular decoder, not a theorem about transformers," yet is never actually confirmed in any of the six systems (see concern 3): the paper extrapolates a categorical claim about AR TTS from an assumption that was tested and not verified anywhere in its own sample. Separately, there is direct evidence in the literature that transformers *can* be trained or architected to length-generalize on counting-like arithmetic tasks despite naive contraction expectations ([2410.15787]), which weakens the implicit assumption that contraction is a default property of AR decoders rather than a training/architecture-contingent one — the six checkpoints tested may simply not be representative of the space of AR TTS systems (e.g., ones trained with explicit index/position hints, monotonic-alignment losses [3,4] the paper itself cites as mitigations).
   **What would address it** — Test on non-English languages/scripts, on at least one more architecturally distinct AR-TTS family (e.g., a VALL-E-style or Bark-style system), and report whether the CosyVoice/RALL-E-style alignment-supervised models the introduction cites as "fixes" actually escape the contraction regime — which would be a direct, falsifiable test of whether contraction is architecture-general or training-contingent.

2. **Issue** — The formal theorem's distinctive quantitative content (a constant horizon N* computable from q, L, C, µ) is never actually confirmed: the ladder that was run to specification (k≤32) directly contradicts it, and the horizon eventually found at k=128 is fit from raw counts, not from the theorem's own parameters.
   **Where** — §4.1 ("not what Theorem 1(iv) predicts," proportional fit RSS 2.6 vs. 485.3); §4.2 (K̂ fit "not the theorem's N∗, a function of the unmeasured q").
   **Why it matters** — This is the central claim-vs-evidence gap in the paper: a saturating curve at k=128 is consistent with many mechanisms besides contracting state dynamics (e.g., a generic decoding-length ceiling, which the authors themselves invoke to explain why controls also saturate). Without an independently measured q feeding into equation (1), "the horizon" observed post hoc is a qualitative pattern-match to the theorem's shape, not a validation of its quantitative content. The paper's own framing acknowledges this ("though not one derived from a measured q") but the abstract and title nonetheless present the k=128 finding as if it corroborates the theorem.
   **What would address it** — Either obtain a working estimator for q (even a crude one, e.g., via a modified boundary-distance or perturbation probe with a validated linearity control) and show N* computed from it falls near the observed saturation point, or reframe the k=128 result explicitly as "consistent with, but not derived from" the theorem, throughout the abstract and discussion, not only in §4.2/§5.

3. **Issue** — Both direct attempts to measure the theorem's load-bearing premise (Assumption 2, contraction) failed, and the mechanism paper leans on to justify AR-specific applicability (Lemma 1's attention dilution) shows a sign-reversed dose-response relationship in the data.
   **Where** — §5 ("two attempts to measure q failed"); §4.4 ("its dose-response prediction fails outright: … flatter attention goes with better counting").
   **Why it matters** — The causal chain the paper argues for is dilution → approximate autonomy → contraction → horizon. If greater attention dilution correlates with *better* counting rather than worse (r=0.59 in the wrong direction), the proposed mechanism linking Lemma 1 to Theorem 1 is empirically contradicted within the paper's own data, not merely unconfirmed. Combined with the failure to measure q at all, the paper has formally proved an implication whose antecedent it cannot demonstrate holds for any of the tested systems, and whose motivating causal story is actively disconfirmed by one of its own tests. The paper is commendably explicit about this, but the abstract's framing ("no Lipschitz readout… can separate repetition counts beyond a horizon") reads as an established empirical fact about the tested models rather than an unverified conditional.
   **What would address it** — Either find a working, validated estimator of local contraction (the boundary-distance estimator's problem — needing a monotone read-head — suggests a state-space or Jacobian-based estimator might work better than a distance-based one) or substantially soften the framing throughout the paper (title, abstract, introduction) to make clear that the mechanism is currently a proved-but-empirically-unconfirmed conditional, and that one of its two empirical predictions failed in the direction opposite to what was predicted.

### Minor concerns
- Reference [1] is cited as motivation for the "stability problem" framing despite being noted as "Withdrawn 13 Feb 2026" — leaning on a withdrawn preprint, even only for its alignment analysis, should be flagged more prominently or replaced with a non-withdrawn source.
- The claim "to our knowledge the first hallucination result in speech synthesis carried out as a formal artifact" is plausible but unverified against the broader formal-verification-for-neural-networks literature; a brief search against that literature (rather than only the cited attention/self-attention dynamics papers [15–19]) would strengthen the novelty claim.
- Figure 1(a)/(b) axis labels as rendered in the extracted text (e.g., "22 25 / k / 100 0 count error") are difficult to parse — likely a text-extraction artifact, but worth double-checking in the camera-ready PDF that axis tick labels and the log-k transformation are legible.
- §4.2's exclusion accounting (342 of 2052 for one template, 132 for budget, 19 degenerate) removes ~24% of panel generations before the headline statistics are computed; the paper reports this transparently but a sensitivity check with the template-excluded item included (even scored as a lower bound) would help rule out that this specific exclusion drives part of the effect size.
- The "checkpoint, not the generation, is the unit of replication" framing with n=6 is honest about power, but the abstract's declarative language ("the limit tracks periodicity, not length, in every checkpoint and every family") should probably be hedged to match the "too small for significance testing" admission in §4.2.

### Verdict
**Major revision** — the periodicity-vs-length dissociation is a genuine, carefully controlled empirical finding, but the paper's title and abstract lean on a formal theorem whose premise was never confirmed and whose motivating mechanism (Lemma 1's dose-response) is contradicted by the paper's own data, while the generalizability of both is asserted over a narrow, mostly-English, architecturally-thin sample.

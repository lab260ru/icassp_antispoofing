I ran two arXiv checks: one to verify the Venkatesh (2026) rival-hypothesis citation and the self-cited companion paper actually exist (both do — [2605.09239] and [2604.08591]), and one to check whether the theoretical framing omits directly relevant prior art on transformers and counting. It does — see Novelty below.

---

### Summary

The paper makes three claims: (1) a Lean-4-verified conditional theorem — if a decoder's per-repetition state update contracts, no Lipschitz readout can recover repetition counts past a computable horizon; (2) an empirical demonstration, using a CTC judge chosen specifically to avoid an autoregressive scoring bias, that six TTS checkpoints undercount repeated phrases proportionally above k≈6, and that this failure tracks periodicity rather than sequence length (94.3% vs 18.2% exact-match on matched controls); (3) a set of direct attempts to test the theorem's own mechanism — measuring the contraction factor, the dilution lemma's dose-response, and post-horizon unreadability — most of which come back negative, null, or 1-for-2. The periodicity-vs-length dissociation is a genuinely well-designed, robustness-checked result and the paper's strongest asset. The theoretical apparatus is, by the authors' own repeated admission, unconnected to any verified mechanism in a real checkpoint. My overall stance: solid empirical core, honestly reported, wrapped around a theorem whose premise is never shown to hold and whose distinctive predictions are tested on samples (n=2, n=6) too small to adjudicate anything, with a narrative habit of explaining away the checkpoints that don't fit rather than letting the small sample speak for itself.

### Major concerns

1. **Issue** — The theorem's load-bearing assumption (contraction, q < 1) is never established for any checkpoint; two direct attempts to measure it failed outright. `[Evidence — distinguishing test]`
   **Where** — Discussion, "What is proved, and what the measurements do not show": "two attempts to measure q failed — one estimator needs a monotone read-head these decoders lack, and a perturbation probe found no difference between repeated and control items (7/18, p = 0.12)."
   **Why it matters** — Everything downstream of Theorem 1 is conditional on Assumption 2. Without evidence the assumption holds anywhere, the Lean proof certifies an if-then statement about a hypothetical dynamical system, not a property of any of the six checkpoints tested. The paper's own title concedes this ("a horizon it does not yet explain"), but a reader who stops at the intro's confident "past a computable horizon no Lipschitz function of the state... can recover the count (Theorem 1)" could easily walk away thinking the mechanism is established rather than untested.
   **What would address it** — Either a successful direct estimate of q on at least one checkpoint (even a rough one), or reframing the theorem throughout as an untested conjecture rather than a companion finding to the empirical results.

2. **Issue** — The related-work section omits the theoretical literature most directly on-topic for the paper's central claim (transformer counting expressivity), citing more tangential rank-collapse and state-tracking work instead. `[Novelty]`
   **Where** — Section 5, "Closest is representational collapse [14]... Related bounds [15–19] bound one pass; state tracking [20] probes many."
   **Why it matters** — arXiv search surfaces at least two papers squarely on "can transformers count repeated tokens" that go uncited: "The Counting Power of Transformers" [2505.11199] and "Language Models Need Inductive Biases to Count Inductively" [2405.20131]. Both bear directly on whether a Lipschitz/contraction argument is the right lens versus an inductive-bias or expressivity argument. Citing the more distant rank-collapse and SSM-state-illusion literature while missing the papers whose title is literally about counting understates how contested this specific question already is, and makes the novelty claim ("first hallucination result in speech synthesis carried out as a formal artifact") harder to situate — it may be a narrower claim than the framing implies once the counting-expressivity line is on the table.
   **What would address it** — Cite and position against the counting-power literature explicitly; state what the contraction-based account predicts that an inductive-bias account does not.

3. **Issue** — The theorem's single most distinctive prediction (unreadability past N*) is tested on 2 checkpoints, split 1-for-1, and the abstract reports this as the theorem "surviving" the test. `[Evidence — statistical rigor]`
   **Where** — Abstract: "Its conclusion survives one direct test... in 1 of the 2 checkpoints we could run it on." Discussion: "the prediction holds in 1 of 2 — Llasa-1B is what the theorem predicts and the rival does not, Llasa-8B the reverse."
   **Why it matters** — n=2 cannot discriminate between "the theorem is right" and "coin flip." The word "survives" in the abstract is doing real rhetorical work it hasn't earned — a 50% hit rate on a binary prediction with two trials is not survival, it's a null result with a story attached. The rival hypothesis this test was meant to adjudicate [13] is itself directly relevant prior work the authors cite and clearly take seriously, which makes the thinness of the discriminating evidence more consequential, not less.
   **What would address it** — Either run the probe on more checkpoints before claiming any directional conclusion, or explicitly state that n=2 is inconclusive rather than framing it as 1-for-2 in the theorem's favor.

4. **Issue** — The CTC judge is validated on spliced/concatenative periodic audio, not on naturally coarticulated repeated speech from the models under test, leaving a plausible confound (phonetic reduction/blending of consecutive repeats) untested. `[Evidence — alternative explanations]`
   **Where** — Section 3, "Behavioural measurement": "Audited against concatenative audio with known ground truth... ratio 1.00 on periodic ground truth against 0.19 for Whisper."
   **Why it matters** — CTC decoding collapses runs of repeated symbols before removing blanks; that's an appropriate design when the acoustic signal cleanly separates repeats, but spliced ground-truth audio has artificial boundaries that natural fluent repetition doesn't. If a TTS model produces genuinely reduced/blended prosody across consecutive repeats (a documented phenomenon in fluent speech), the CTC judge could systematically undercount for reasons that have nothing to do with the decoder's internal state dynamics — exactly the effect the paper is trying to isolate. The audit as described doesn't rule this out for the actual test items.
   **What would address it** — Audit the judge against a small set of naturally-produced (not spliced) ground-truth repeated speech with independently verified counts, ideally human-transcribed.

5. **Issue** — Roughly a dozen distinct supplement references (S5, S8, S10–S13, S15–S20, S22) carry load-bearing statistics that can't be checked from the text as given. `[Reproducibility]`
   **Where** — Throughout Sections 3–4 (e.g., "S12" cited for the probe correlation, the MAE/R² figures, and the dose-response statistic; "S16" for the post-hoc audit trail).
   **Why it matters** — This is a strong paper on code/data release (repo, pinned revisions, build.sh, released transcripts, a stratified spot-check sample), which makes it more notable that several of the specific numbers a reader needs to evaluate the paper's central claims — the effective-rank methodology, the exact bootstrap procedure behind repeated CIs, the linearity-control failure in S5 — are gestured at rather than shown. A 4-page ICASSP paper with a supplement is normal; a paper this dense with hedges and hidden-behind-a-letter statistics is harder to independently audit than the release practices otherwise promise.
   **What would address it** — Nothing needed if the supplement is public and equally rigorous (plausible given the repo link) — but the main text should say so explicitly rather than leaving the reader to guess whether S-numbers are in the released repo.

6. **Issue** — The paper contains zero qualitative examples of an actual "counting collapse" (no transcript, no waveform, no example generation) despite the abstract's vivid framing ("loop, truncate, and lose count"), and where individual checkpoints are discussed, disconfirming ones get bespoke post-hoc stories rather than being treated as evidence against generality. `[Evidence — cherry-picked qualitative examples]`
   **Where** — Abstract framing vs. Section 4.2: "The exception is not arbitrary: it is the weakest checkpoint on all 3 panel measures, its median-error gap zero and its exact-rate gap 60.0 points. This is where a small effect becomes none, not the theorem behaving conditionally." Also Discussion, S12: "Llasa-1B is what the theorem predicts and the rival does not, Llasa-8B the reverse."
   **Why it matters** — This is the inverse of the classic cherry-picking failure but functionally the same risk: rather than hand-picking flattering text excerpts, the paper hand-narrates individual checkpoints into "counts as support" or "doesn't count as disconfirmation" categories after the fact, with a plausible-sounding but unverified story each time (Qwen3-TTS-1.7B is "the weakest checkpoint," so its null doesn't threaten the theorem; Llasa-8B "keeps" a different prediction, so the theorem is "1 of 2" rather than "1 of 2, indistinguishable from chance"). With n=6 checkpoints and n=2 for the sharpest test, there isn't enough data for this kind of per-unit story-telling to be epistemically load-bearing — it reads as post-hoc rationalization dressed as case analysis. Separately, for a paper about an audibly perceptible phenomenon, the total absence of even one concrete illustrative example in the text itself (the released 165-clip sample is mentioned but never shown or characterized in-paper) means a reader has no way to sanity-check that "18.2% exact" corresponds to intuitively sensible degenerate behavior rather than a judge artifact.
   **What would address it** — Either report the per-checkpoint breakdown flatly, without narrative adjudication of which exceptions "count," and let a reader assess consistency themselves; and include at least one concrete example (transcript pair, repeated vs. control, at k above and below the reported horizon) in the main text.

### Minor concerns

- "Points" is used inconsistently to mean percentage-point gaps without being defined at first use (e.g., "76.7 [68.4, 84.4] points," "60.0 points") — a reader has to infer the unit from context.
- Confidence-interval methodology is stated for Figure 1(c) ("95% bootstrap intervals over stimulus templates") but not consistently stated for other bracketed intervals in the text (e.g., the -8.3% [-12.5, -6.2] pooled estimate).
- The extracted figure text for panel (b) ("26 27 k 25 26 27 rendered count") is hard to parse — likely a PDF-extraction artifact losing superscripts (probably 2^6, 2^7 on a log axis) rather than an authoring error, but worth double-checking in the camera-ready.
- Reference [2] renders as "V ALL-E" with a stray space — likely also an extraction artifact.
- "Capacity gain" (Table 1 / Fig. 1c) is introduced without units in the main text; a first-time reader has to infer it's an effective-rank slope from context in Section 4.4.

### Belief update

Modest, directional update: the periodicity-vs-length dissociation (94.3% vs 18.2% exact, replicated across decoding-rule and filler-pool confound checks) is a clean enough result that I now put more weight on "AR TTS repetition failures are specifically about periodicity, not generic length/exposure-bias effects" than I did before. I did not update toward the paper's preferred mechanism (state contraction erasing the count) — if anything, the balance of the paper's own evidence (Llasa-8B's probe result, the reversed dilution dose-response, the rival account [13] the authors engage honestly with) leaves me slightly more sympathetic to an output-policy account (the count is represented but not surfaced) than to a representation-erasure account. Nothing here shifted my view of the Lean verification's significance — a machine-checked proof of an unestablished premise doesn't transfer confidence to the empirical claim it's paired with.

### Verdict

**Major revision** — the empirical periodicity-vs-length result stands on its own, but the theorem and its companion tests are asked to carry more explanatory weight than n=2/n=6 samples and a checkpoint-by-checkpoint post-hoc narrative (concern 6) can support; the theoretical framing needs to be decoupled from, or explicitly subordinated to, the empirical result until the mechanism is tested on more than a coin flip's worth of checkpoints.

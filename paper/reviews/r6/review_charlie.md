### Summary

The paper makes three claims: (1) a Lean-4-verified theorem that *if* a decoder's per-repetition state update contracts (Lipschitz q<1), *then* no Lipschitz readout — including the model's own stop head — can distinguish repetition counts past a computable horizon N*; (2) an empirical panel of six AR-TTS checkpoints showing a periodicity-specific undercounting effect (median -8.3% error at k≥6, 18.2% exact-match vs. 94.3% for length-matched non-repetitive controls) accompanied by slower growth of internal-state effective rank (0.46× the control rate); and (3) that within the originally planned k≤32 range the effect looks proportional rather than saturating — the opposite of what the theorem predicts — until a post-hoc extension to k=128 on 4/6 checkpoints finds a saturation point after all, at roughly 3.5× lower scale for repeated vs. control text. The paper is unusually candid about its own weak points (post-hoc extension flagged as post hoc, n=6 significance floor stated explicitly, "the premise remains unestablished" stated outright), which is genuinely to its credit and makes this a harder paper to red-team than most. But candour about a gap is not the same as closing it: the theorem's load-bearing premise (contraction) is never measured to hold, the one experiment built to test the dilution lemma's causal *direction* comes back with the wrong sign and is explained away rather than treated as disconfirming, and the paper's self-declared "sharpest test" of periodicity-vs-length turns out, on inspection of its own methods paragraph, to not test periodicity against aperiodicity at all. My prior going in was that a title as strong as "Attractor Theory of Repetition Hallucination" is usually not earned by the body, and after reading, I think that's still roughly right here — the empirical periodicity effect is real and worth publishing, the mechanistic story wrapped around it is not yet distinguished from several deflationary alternatives the paper itself gestures at but doesn't rule out in the text provided.

### Major concerns

1. **Issue** — The title and abstract state the attractor mechanism in confident indicative voice, while the body concedes the mechanism's own premise was never confirmed. **[Narrative — overclaiming]**
   **Where** — Title ("Attractor Theory of Repetition Hallucination"); Abstract; Section 1 ("the decoder therefore meets approximately the same conditioning at every repetition boundary… If that map contracts, its record of how many repetitions it has produced is erased geometrically"); contradicted by Section 5 ("The premise remains unestablished").
   **Why it matters** — A reader who reads only the title and abstract (most readers, most of the time) comes away believing repetition hallucination in AR-TTS has been explained by a proven contraction mechanism. It hasn't — it's been modelled by one, conditionally, and the condition was never checked to hold in a real decoder.
   **What would address it** — Hedge the title and abstract to match the body: "a candidate contraction account," not "attractor theory," until Assumption 2 is measured rather than assumed.

2. **Issue** — No comparison against the alignment-supervised architectures the introduction itself names as the field's standard fix for exactly this failure mode. **[Evidence — baselines]**
   **Where** — Section 1 cites ELLA-V [2], RALL-E [3], CosyVoice 2 [4] as attention-supervision/decoding-rule fixes; the only mitigation actually run is an XTTS-v2 repetition-penalty sweep, disposed of in Discussion in one clause: "sweeping XTTS-v2's repetition penalty fourfold moves the deficit the wrong way (S7)."
   **Why it matters** — The theorem's own logic says a fix that raises q or the readout margin should raise N*. The one mitigation tested moved the *opposite* direction from what the theory predicts, and the paper doesn't reconcile this — it's the most theoretically informative negative result in the paper and it gets a subordinate clause pointing at an unreviewed supplement.
   **What would address it** — Run the ladder against at least one alignment-supervised checkpoint (ELLA-V/CosyVoice2-class) and report whether N*/the deficit moves in the predicted direction; explain the repetition-penalty inversion in the main text, not a footnote-supplement.

3. **Issue** — All checkpoint-level inferential statistics rest on n=6, at or below the test's own significance floor. **[Statistical rigour]**
   **Where** — Section 4.2: "the exactly-correct rate favours the control in all 6 (mean 76.7 points [68.4, 84.4], signed-rank p = 0.031, the exact floor at n = 6). The median-error gap is positive in 5 of 6 and zero in Qwen3-TTS-1.7B, so p = 0.062: on that statistic the panel misses even its own floor."
   **Why it matters** — p=0.031 here isn't evidence of a strong effect surviving a stringent test; it's the best p-value six binary outcomes can ever produce. On the more central metric (median error, not just exact-match rate), the panel doesn't even clear p<.05. This is a 6-checkpoint case series with confidence intervals bolted on, not a powered comparison — confirmatory-tier significance (p<.005) is nowhere close.
   **What would address it** — Either expand the panel (more checkpoints/families) or drop the framing that leans on p-values at n=6 and report this honestly as descriptive, checkpoint-by-checkpoint evidence, which is what it is.

4. **Issue** — The artifacts needed to independently check the paper's most novel empirical claim (effective-rank/capacity-gain, Section 4.4) are not actually released, only "available on request." **[Reproducibility]**
   **Where** — Section 3, "Code, data, settings": "Audio (2.5 GB) and activations (25 GB) are available on request and regenerable from the released seeds."
   **Why it matters** — The capacity-gain numbers in Table 1 (0.21–0.80 range across checkpoints) are the closest thing to direct mechanistic evidence in the paper, and they're derived from the one dataset (25 GB of activations) that isn't hosted. "Regenerable from released seeds" means a reader has to rerun the full generation+probing pipeline to check a single number, which is a materially higher bar than downloading a file.
   **What would address it** — Host the activations (or a representative subset sufficient to reproduce Table 1 and Fig. 1c/d) on a persistent store, not "on request."

5. **Issue** — The paper's self-declared "sharpest test" doesn't test periodicity against aperiodicity — it tests period-1 against period-8. **[Evidence — distinguishing test]**
   **Where** — Section 3, Stimuli: "same carrier and word count, the repeated token replaced by fillers from a per-template pool of eight, cycled above k = 8. The contrast tested is thus period-8 repetition at most against period-1 (S10)." Then Section 4.3 frames this as if it isolates periodicity cleanly: "Were the failure about length, attention span or exposure bias, the curves would coincide. They do not."
   **Why it matters** — Lemma 1's dilution effect scales as Θ(1/k) — it should produce *graded* degradation as period shrinks toward 1, including some (smaller) effect at period 8, not a clean step to exactly zero. The paper reports the control at "0.0% [0.0, 0.0]… at every k up to 32, for every model" — a step function, not the gradient the theory predicts. No intermediate periods (2, 3, 4) are tested, so the data can't distinguish "graded dilution effect, as Lemma 1 predicts" from "some unrelated threshold mechanism (e.g., a local n-gram/repeat detector) that only fires on exact adjacent repeats." Both hypotheses are consistent with what's shown; only one is tested.
   **What would address it** — Run the ladder at intermediate periods (2, 3, 4, 8) and check whether degradation is graded (supports the dilution mechanism) or a step at period 1 (supports a cheaper, unrelated explanation).

6. **Issue** — The one experiment built to test the causal direction of the paper's own mechanism returns the opposite sign, and both direct attempts to confirm the theorem's premise fail — yet the paper's framing survives both results via post-hoc reinterpretation rather than treating them as disconfirming. **[Evidence — distinguishing test]**
   **Where** — Section 4.4: Lemma 1 predicts flatter attention (more dilution) → worse counting. The measured relationship is the reverse: "within a (model, k) cell, items with flatter attention are counted better, not worse (r = 0.59 [0.35, 0.75] over 23 cells, all 3 flatness measures agreeing… That reverses the lemma's direction… peaked attention plausibly marks a decoder already stalled, which this design cannot separate from a cause." Compounded by Section 5: "two attempts to measure q failed — a boundary-distance estimator needs a monotone read-head these decoders lack, and a perturbation probe found no difference in local contraction between repeated items and their controls (7 of 18 pairs, p = 0.12), its own linearity control failing (S5). The premise remains unestablished."
   **Why it matters** — This is the crux. Take the paper's own evidence at face value: the theorem's antecedent (contraction) is never confirmed to hold in any real decoder (one measurement attempt is inapplicable, the other is a null result), and the one within-paper test of the dilution lemma's directional signature came back backwards. A "reverse-causation" story (stalled decoders happen to show peaked attention) is offered to save the mechanism, but it's introduced only after the data went the wrong way, and the design "cannot separate [it] from a cause" by the authors' own admission — i.e., it's unfalsifiable within this study. Taken together, nothing in the empirical section actually discriminates "counting fails because decoder state contracts to a fixed point" from "counting fails for some other reason correlated with, but not explained by, periodicity." The Lean proof is airtight; its connection to what these six models are actually doing is not established by anything reported here.
   **What would address it** — Either find an experiment that discriminates the contraction account from the "peaked attention = symptom of prior stall, not cause" alternative (e.g., an intervention that forces attention flatness/peakedness independent of position in the failure trajectory), or explicitly demote the theorem in the paper's framing to "a formally consistent candidate mechanism, not yet empirically discriminated from alternatives" — which is what Section 5 already says in effect, but the title and abstract don't.

### Minor concerns

- The "first hallucination result in speech synthesis carried out as a formal artifact" claim (Section 1) is a narrow triple conjunction (formal verification × speech synthesis × hallucination); no search methodology is described, and adjacent formal results already in the paper's own bibliography (state-tracking/circuit-class work, [15],[16]) aren't explicitly distinguished from this claim's scope.
- The "Limits:" paragraph that closes Section 5 lists external-validity limits (English-only, judge bias, softmax-only attention) but doesn't restate the internal-validity limit flagged two paragraphs earlier ("the premise remains unestablished") — a reader skimming for limitations lands on the weaker list.
- Reference [1] is cited for its alignment analysis despite being "Withdrawn 13 Feb 2026," with no sentence on why the withdrawal doesn't bear on the cited claim.
- Section 4.4: "rendering very sixteen times really does produce monotonous audio" reads like a dropped quotation mark or missing word around "very" (presumably the example repeated token).
- Figure 1's axis/caption text renders literal LaTeX escapes ("count error (\%)") rather than a percent sign as given in the paper text.
- No example transcript, spectrogram, or audio excerpt of an actual counting-collapse failure appears anywhere in the main text — for a paper whose hook is "say six hundred sixty-six thousand six hundred sixty-six," there isn't a single concrete rendered example, correct or failed, shown to the reader. (This forecloses cherry-picking risk, since nothing qualitative is shown at all — but it also leaves the phenomenon entirely unillustrated.)
- The abstract's horizon formula "N∗ ≤ log(µ/2LC)/log q" drops the parentheses present in numbered equation (1), "log(µ/(2LC))/log q" — ambiguous if read literally.
- "dNeff/d log k" (Table 1, Fig. 1d) never specifies the log base; the within-paper ratios are base-invariant, but the absolute magnitudes aren't interpretable without it.

### Belief update

Modest, directional update on one proposition: that repetition-induced failures in AR-TTS decoders are substantially about the *periodicity* of the conditioning text rather than sequence length per se (Fig. 1b's 94.3%-vs-18.2% exact-match gap is large enough to survive my skepticism about the period-8 confound in Concern 5 — some real periodicity-specific effect plausibly exists, even if the "sharpest test" framing overstates how cleanly it isolates periodicity). Essentially no update on the paper's title-level claim — that this is explained by a contracting fixed-point map in the decoder's state — because the paper's own reported attempts to test that premise directly returned a null result and a reversed correlation, and it says so itself. My stance-adjusted prior (assume artefactual until proven otherwise) is not overturned for the mechanistic story; it is mildly updated for the surface phenomenon. If I had to state it as a probability shift: P(TTS repetition failures track periodicity, not length) moves up meaningfully (from "plausible" to "probably true, pending a cleaner control"); P(the specific contraction/attractor mechanism in Theorem 1 is what's happening in these six checkpoints) barely moves, because no experiment in the paper was actually capable of moving it.

### Verdict

**Major revision** — the periodicity effect is a real, publishable empirical contribution, but the paper's title and abstract assert a mechanistic explanation (contraction/attractor dynamics) that its own reported experiments fail to establish: the premise is admitted unconfirmed, and the one directional test of the dilution lemma returns the wrong sign and is explained away rather than treated as disconfirming evidence (Concern 6) — until that gap between what's proved (an implication) and what's shown (a correlational, direction-reversed pattern) is closed or the framing is brought down to match it, the paper's central causal claim is not distinguished from the alternatives it gestures at.

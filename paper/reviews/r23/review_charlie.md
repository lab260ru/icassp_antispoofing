## Summary

The paper asks whether autoregressive TTS models' well-documented failure to render repeated phrases correctly (looping, truncating, miscounting) is driven by *input length* or by *periodicity* per se, and separately asks whether a Lean-4-verified "contraction" account of that failure actually describes the measured systems. Using a 180-item repetition ladder (target word repeated k times, each paired with a length- and word-count-matched control whose repeated token is replaced with distinct fillers) across six checkpoints from three (later four) TTS architectures, the authors report that repeated items are rendered exactly 18.2% of the time vs. 94.3% for controls at k≥6, that the deficit survives greedy decoding, repetition-penalty sweeps, exclusion-rule ablations, and four different ASR judges, and that a formally verified contraction bound the authors themselves derived to explain the effect is empirically false (measured Lipschitz expansion factors q ≫ 1 in every checkpoint and arm). The headline claim is that the failure tracks periodicity, not sequence length, and that it is a policy/readout failure rather than an erasure of the count from hidden state.

## Major concerns

1. **Issue** — The core manipulation confounds "periodicity" with "identical adjacent tokens triggering degenerate-loop sampling dynamics," a distinct and independently well-documented autoregressive pathology, and the design does not fully separate the two.
   **Where** — Section 1 ("the speech analogue of neural text degeneration [5,6]"); Section 2 (stimulus construction); Section 3.1 (period ladder, Fig. 1b).
   **Why it matters** — The paper's entire argument hinges on periodicity being the operative variable rather than length. But an alternative, equally plausible account is that *identical consecutive (or near-consecutive) tokens* push autoregressive samplers into self-reinforcing repetition loops — the very "neural text degeneration" phenomenon the introduction invokes as the field's existing explanation, now for a different mechanism than "length." The period-2 condition (no token adjacent to itself) is offered as the decisive control, and roughly half the deficit survives there — but ABAB alternation is itself a low-order, highly regular attractor structure that n-gram/loop-detection dynamics in a codec-token LM could latch onto without requiring any "counting" representation at all. The paper never tests whether the deficit is driven specifically by low-order periodic structure (p=2) that a simple loop-detector could produce, versus a genuine period-invariant "count" limitation, because the period ladder only samples p ∈ {1,2,4,8} — periods that all evenly divide the 8-word filler pool size. Odd or non-power-of-two periods (3, 5, 6, 7) are never tested, so "period" and "pool-cycling structure" are perfectly collinear in the design (this is acknowledged only obliquely in the Fig. 1(b) caption: "hollow markers past the rule are the p=k arm, matched to neither in vocabulary nor length"). A design that cannot distinguish "loop-attractor triggered by short-cycle repetition" from "general periodicity limit" cannot license the title's claim.
   **What would address it** — Add periods that do not divide the pool size (e.g., p=3, p=5) with independently constructed filler pools sized to those periods, and report whether the graded monotone relationship (Fig. 1b) survives with periods that are not artifacts of pool-cycling arithmetic. Additionally, report a metric of "loop entropy" (e.g., n-gram repetition rate in generated codec tokens) as a covariate, to test whether it, rather than abstract periodicity, mediates the deficit.

2. **Issue** — The k≥6 threshold that defines the headline comparison (18.2% vs 94.3%) is explicitly acquired post hoc.
   **Where** — Section 3.2: "The k≥6 threshold was chosen after seeing where the arms diverge, which is why the abstract gives the unconditioned figure too and the specification curve varies it."
   **Why it matters** — This is a textbook researcher-degree-of-freedom problem. The authors are commendably transparent about it, and they do report an unconditioned 46.8-point gap and a 420-specification robustness curve (34.4–86.4 points, never reversing sign) as compensation. But the *abstract's* leading contrast (18.2% vs 94.3%) is still the post-hoc-selected one, and the paper explicitly declines any multiple-comparison correction across the 420 specifications ("these are not independent tests and we correct across none of them"). Declining correction is defensible for an effect-size robustness envelope, but it should not be paired with reporting a single post-hoc-optimized headline number as the paper's primary quoted statistic without equally prominent framing of the pre-registered/unconditioned alternative.
   **What would address it** — Lead the abstract and Table 1 with the unconditioned (from k=1) or a pre-specified threshold's numbers, and demote the k≥6 contrast to "the largest gap observed, selected post hoc" framing throughout, not just in the fine print of 3.2.

3. **Issue** — The filler substitution used to build "length-matched controls" is not shown to be matched on lexical frequency, phonetic complexity, or duration to the target word — only on carrier and word count.
   **Where** — Section 2, "Stimuli"; discussion of never-cycled vs. cycled fillers in 3.1 ("most of that 7.1 points is vocabulary rather than periodicity, the substitution costing 1.7 within one unit").
   **Why it matters** — The paper's own numbers show that filler-vocabulary choice moves the gap by up to 7.1 points (never-cycled 67.6/69.6 vs. cycled 75.4/76.7), which the authors attribute to "vocabulary" effects distinct from periodicity — but this concedes that the control is not merely a periodicity-only manipulation; it also varies word-level acoustic/lexical predictability, articulatory difficulty, and per-word phoneme-decoding load relative to the fixed target word. Without matched controls for word frequency/length/phonetic complexity, part of the "periodicity" effect could be an artifact of the specific target words chosen being harder to render many times in a row for reasons unrelated to repetition per se (e.g., cumulative articulatory or prosodic drift specific to that word's phoneme sequence).
   **What would address it** — Report per-item control for filler-target lexical/phonetic similarity (frequency band, syllable count, phoneme overlap), or rerun a subset of the ladder with fillers drawn from a frequency- and length-matched distribution rather than a fixed 8-word pool, and show the periodicity gradient survives.

4. **Issue** — Blinding of the automatic exclusion pipeline (342 + 133 + 19 of 2052 generations dropped) is asserted but not independently verified.
   **Where** — Section 2 ("Behavioural measurement…"); Section 5 limitations ("Exclusions are blind to arm and score").
   **Why it matters** — The claim that exclusions are "blind to arm and score" is exactly the kind of claim that needs a check, not an assertion, in an adversarial reading: the exclusion rules are rule-based and applied post hoc by the same team that ran the main analysis, and one whole template (342 items) is dropped because "the judge never transcribes" its fillers — a template-level exclusion that could plausibly interact with which templates show the largest repeated/control gap. No independent audit (e.g., a held-out annotator blind to condition re-deriving the exclusion set, or a pre-registered exclusion protocol) is reported.
   **What would address it** — Report exclusion rates and effect sizes separately for the excluded vs. retained templates, and ideally have exclusion decisions made by an analyst blind to which arm (repeated/control) each item belongs to, verified against the reported rule rather than asserted.

5. **Issue** — The causal state-patching experiment supporting the "policy failure, not state erasure" conclusion has an unresolved, discordant fourth data point that is folded into "cannot tell" rather than treated as a disconfirming result.
   **Where** — Section 5, "The rival the evidence favours" (Llasa-8B: "a same-k donor moves the count as much as a cross-k one (0.25 vs. 0.22)").
   **Where it matters** — The zero-effect patching result on 3/4 checkpoints is the paper's positive evidence that "the count is not erased… but the policy reading it fails." But on the fourth checkpoint the patch *does* move the count, and the authors' explanation — "that arm measures foreign state" — is a post hoc rationalization for a result inconsistent with the favored account, not an independently verified diagnosis. Given n=36 total across all four checkpoints, and only three contributing to the headline "no dose-response" claim, the causal readout-vs-representation conclusion rests on a small and partially discordant sample.
   **What would address it** — Either provide independent verification of the "foreign state" explanation for Llasa-8B (e.g., a separate probe of positional/content mismatch under the patch) or report the causal conclusion as tentative and restricted to 3 of 4 checkpoints, rather than as a general "rival the evidence favours" claim in the discussion's framing.

## Minor concerns

- The specification-curve range (34.4–86.4 points) is reported without a distribution or histogram; readers cannot tell if most specifications cluster near the headline number or if the range is driven by a few outlier specifications.
- "Nor is it sampling: greedy decoding gives 72.2 points against 75.0 sampled" — no seed/variance information is given for the greedy condition (greedy is deterministic per input, so what does "72.2 points" average over — different items only, or repeated runs?).
- The partial-pooling posterior predictive interval for CosyVoice 2 is described as "committed before the checkpoint's first waveform existed," with a commit hash offered as evidence — this is a nice touch but the paper doesn't say whether the pre-registration document itself (not just the commit timestamp) is included in the public repository for independent verification.
- Table 1 reports Qwen3-TTS-1.7B with count-error gap "0.0%" but exact-match gap of 60 points — the juxtaposition is explained once in prose but risks being read as contradictory if a reader only skims the table.
- The paper cites Venkatesh [13] (arXiv:2605.09239) as establishing "the representational half" in text models — this citation checks out and the description matches the abstract (linear probes decode count correctly even where output is wrong), so the "we add the causal half" framing is fair, but the paper never discusses whether Venkatesh's finding was itself replicated/contested, leaving "the field's" prior finding resting on a single single-author preprint.
- "S9, S27" and similar supplement pointers are used heavily throughout the results section without the supplement included in the reviewed text — several load-bearing claims (Spanish results, per-condition rates, causal-patch details) are entirely deferred to unseen material.
- Minor wording: "94.3% for controls against 18.2% for their twins" and "18.2% of generations against 94.3%" (abstract vs. body) present the same numbers in reversed order in different places, which is easy to misread on a skim.

## Verdict

**Major revision** — the central periodicity-vs-length manipulation and its robustness checks are unusually careful and largely convincing, but the period-ladder's confound between "periodicity" and "pool-cycling/loop-attractor structure" (only powers-of-two periods tested, no odd-period conditions), the unverified blinding of the exclusion pipeline, and the post-hoc k≥6 threshold driving the headline statistic each need to be closed before the "periodicity, not length" claim can be accepted as demonstrated rather than strongly suggested.

### Summary
The paper asks whether autoregressive (and some non-autoregressive) TTS models' well-known tendency to miscount repeated words is driven by input *length* or by *periodicity* per se, and tries to locate the failure mechanistically. Using a 180-item "repetition ladder" (target word repeated k times, k=1…32) each paired with a length-matched, non-repetitive control, evaluated across six checkpoints from three architecture families (plus later a fourth, CosyVoice 2) and two non-autoregressive baselines (VITS, F5-TTS), the authors report a large, consistent exact-rendering gap between repeated and control conditions (94.3% vs 18.2% at k≥6), which persists under greedy decoding, penalty sweeps, exclusion-rule ablations, and four different ASR judges. A second "period ladder" shows exact-rendering rates rising monotonically with period p (10.4→93.1% for p=1→8), taken as evidence that periodicity rather than verbatim adjacency drives the deficit. Finally, the paper formally states (in Lean 4) a contraction-based bound on a decoder's ability to count, then measures the relevant Lipschitz constant q across checkpoints and finds q ≫ 1 everywhere, rejecting the mechanism that motivated the study.

### Major concerns

1. **Issue** — The paper's core cross-architecture generalization claim rests on a hierarchical/partial-pooling model with only 3 clusters (architecture families), and the reported inference is not robust to the choice of prior on the group-level variance — the text reports the estimate flips from clearly positive to touching zero depending on that choice.
   **Where** — Section 3.1: "the posterior predictive gap for an architecture outside the panel is 65.3 [5.9, 93.3], P=0.996 … and under the widest prior on the group-level scales it touches zero (−1.6, P=0.956)."
   **Why it matters** — With n=3 higher-level units, the between-group variance component of any mixed/hierarchical model is essentially unidentified from the data alone; the posterior is then driven largely by the prior. The paper reports this candidly, but the abstract and framing ("46.8 points… 76.4 points…") treat the point estimate as the operative conclusion while burying the prior-dependent null result in a subordinate clause. A reader who takes the stated P=0.956-under-a-reasonable-prior seriously would conclude the cross-architecture generalization is *not* established statistically, only demonstrated anecdotally by the single CosyVoice 2 follow-up.
   **What would address it** — Either drop the hierarchical generalization claim in favor of the descriptive per-checkpoint numbers, or obtain enough independent architecture families (≥6–8) to make the group-level variance identifiable, and report a prior sensitivity analysis rather than one contrasting pair of point estimates in-text.

2. **Issue** — The training-corpus-frequency confound — repeated k-fold phrases are far rarer in training data than length-matched non-repetitive sentences — is an alternative account that would produce the *exact same* repeated-vs-control dissociation reported as the headline result, and it is not experimentally separated from periodicity anywhere in the design.
   **Where** — Section 5, "What the design does not separate": "Periodicity is not separated from training-set frequency: k-fold repetition is far rarer in speech corpora than a matched distinct-word sentence, and an out-of-distribution account predicts the same dissociation."
   **Why it matters** — This is not a minor caveat; it is a complete rival explanation for every quantitative result in Section 3, including the period ladder (higher-period stimuli are presumably also rarer in training data, so the p=1→8 monotonicity is equally consistent with an OOD-frequency account). The abstract nonetheless states unconditionally that "the limit tracks periodicity, not length," without qualifying that a third candidate variable (corpus frequency) was never controlled.
   **What would address it** — A control that holds frequency-in-training-distribution roughly constant while varying periodicity — e.g., stimuli built from common repeated idioms/onomatopoeia (which do occur non-trivially in speech/text corpora, e.g. "ha ha ha," "no no no") versus matched rare non-periodic constructions — or an explicit corpus-frequency covariate/estimate reported alongside k and p.

3. **Issue** — The 420-specification "robustness envelope" explicitly applies no multiple-comparison correction, and the primary k≥6 threshold used throughout the abstract and tables was chosen post hoc after inspecting where the two curves diverge.
   **Where** — Section 3.1, end: "Across 420 specifications… the gap runs 34.4–86.4 points and none reverses its sign… we correct across none of them"; Section 3.2: "The k ≥ 6 threshold was chosen after seeing where the arms diverge."
   **Why it matters** — A specification curve where "no specification reverses sign" is weak evidence of robustness when the specifications are drawn from the same 1558–2052 generations, scored by the same judge pipeline, and share most of their variance (they are not independent tests, despite the caveat naming them as such). Recent methodological critiques of multiverse/specification-curve analysis note it is easily used to manufacture an appearance of robustness without controlling the actual degrees of freedom exercised in choosing the headline cut (Modrák, "Multiverse analysis, abdication of responsibility and manufacturing of doubt," [2607.14623]). Combined with the admitted post hoc threshold selection, the specific magnitude figures quoted in the abstract (67.6, 75.4, 46.8 points) are optimistically biased even if the qualitative periodicity-vs-length conclusion survives.
   **What would address it** — Pre-register (or clearly separate) a single primary threshold chosen independent of the divergence pattern, report the specification curve as exploratory/sensitivity analysis only, and quantify how correlated the 420 specifications actually are (e.g., effective number of independent tests) rather than treating "sign never reverses" as the relevant robustness statistic.

4. **Issue** — Several of the paper's strongest mechanistic and confirmatory claims are drawn from very small, uneven, and unpowered samples, with no power analysis anywhere in the paper.
   **Where** — Whisper ground-truth audit, n=12 (Section 2); rank-1 causal-patch mechanistic claim ("the count is not erased… it moves the count not at all") from n=36 across only 3 of 4 checkpoints (Section 5); CosyVoice 2 confirmatory test, n=90, compared against a predicted interval of [5.9, 93.3] points that is ~87 points wide (Section 3.1).
   **Why it matters** — A predicted interval spanning nearly the entire possible range of a percentage-point gap has almost no discriminating power: essentially any non-negative gap in a new architecture would have "confirmed" the model, which undercuts the rhetorical weight the paper gives to the CosyVoice 2 replication ("the agreement closer than it entitles us to expect" — the authors' own words concede this). Similarly, the n=36 causal-patching result is used to make a strong representational claim (count survives in the state but the policy fails to read it) that a sample of this size, on 3 checkpoints, cannot support with any stated confidence beyond the point estimate and its wide interval [−0.09, 0.09].
   **What would address it** — Report power/precision calculations for each key comparison, widen the causal-patching sample or explicitly flag it as pilot-scale, and replace "confirms"/"decisive" language around the CosyVoice 2 and patching results with hedged claims proportionate to the achieved precision.

5. **Issue** — The abstract's claim to have "ruled out the judge-side artifact" is broader than what was actually tested: only one specific artifact (CTC blank-collapse) and one specific mechanism (state contraction) were falsified; other plausible judge- or capacity-side accounts are acknowledged elsewhere in the paper (e.g., state-tracking/expressivity bounds cited in Section 5) but never measured against the data.
   **Where** — Abstract: "ruling out the judge-side artifact that would otherwise explain it"; Section 5: "counting-specific expressivity results agree by another route… state-tracking bounds [20] bracketing it" — cited but not tested against this paper's own checkpoints.
   **Why it matters** — The paper's rhetorical structure (propose mechanism → measure → refute) is compelling for the one mechanism it tests (contraction), but the abstract generalizes this single refutation to "the judge-side artifact" as though the space of alternative explanations were exhausted. Readers may take "ruled out" to mean more than one specific, falsified hypothesis.
   **What would address it** — Scope the abstract's claim to the specific tested artifact/mechanism, and either test or explicitly flag as open the state-tracking/expressivity-bound alternative that Section 5 itself raises as a live candidate.

### Minor concerns
- The Spanish replication arm is reported as producing directionally consistent but ultimately "unreportable" numbers (Section 5), yet Section 2 quotes specific Whisper/CTC contrast figures for it ("mean 0.93, S9, S27") — the inconsistency between "unreportable" and citing specific numbers should be clarified.
- Table 1's "Qwen3-TTS-1.7B" row (0.0% median error gap, described in text as "the one whose median-error gap is zero") is presented prominently as a counterexample the paper still explains away via exact-rate — worth flagging in the table caption itself rather than only in prose, since a reader skimming Table 1 could miss that this checkpoint nearly nulls the primary metric.
- The paper cites its own companion piece [7] (Viakhirev et al., arXiv:2604.08591, 2026) and a closely related concurrent preprint [13] (Venkatesh, arXiv:2605.09239, 2026) that the causal section leans on heavily ("Venkatesh established the representational half… we add the causal half") — both are very recent, same-cohort/same-year preprints; readers should be told explicitly whether [13] has been independently verified/reviewed, since the paper's causal framing depends on accepting its "representational half" result as given.
- "S6," "S8," "S9," "S10," etc. supplement references are used extensively without the supplement being reproduced or summarized in-line; several load-bearing methodological details (e.g., exact bootstrap/CI construction method, precise exclusion criteria for the 342/133/19 dropped generations) are deferred entirely to unseen supplementary material.
- Minor wording: "94.3% for their cycled controls at k ≥ 6" in the abstract vs. Table 1's per-checkpoint control rates (87.8–100.0%) — the aggregate figure obscures a fairly wide per-checkpoint spread that's only visible in the table.

### Verdict
**Major revision** — the experimental design (length-matched controls, period ladder, multi-judge cross-check, formally verified-then-refuted mechanism) is unusually careful and the transparency about exclusions and specification curves is commendable, but the paper's generalization and confirmation claims lean on small, prior-sensitive, and multiple-comparison-uncorrected statistics that materially overstate what the data support, and the training-frequency confound is acknowledged but left structurally unaddressed.

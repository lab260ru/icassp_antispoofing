STANCE: Adversarial (assume the headline result is false; what would falsify it?)

### Summary

The paper asks whether autoregressive (AR) text-to-speech models' well-documented tendency to mis-render a repeated phrase a requested number of times is a length/exposure-bias artifact or a distinct, periodicity-specific failure, and offers a Lean-4-verified theorem showing that if a decoder's per-repetition state update contracts, no Lipschitz readout (including its own stop head) can distinguish repetition counts beyond a computable horizon. Six AR checkpoints across three families are scored on a repetition ladder (k up to 32, extended post hoc to 128 on four checkpoints) using a CTC recognizer chosen specifically because the "obvious" judge, Whisper, is itself autoregressive and biased against repetition. The headline results are: a median relative count error of −8.3% at k ≥ 6 for repeated items versus near-perfect (94.3% exact) performance on length-matched non-repeating controls in every checkpoint and family; a proportional (non-saturating) deficit within k ≤ 32 that only becomes a saturating "horizon" once the ladder is extended to k = 128; and two non-AR baselines that disagree (VITS immune, F5-TTS not), leading the authors to withdraw their own architectural explanation. The authors report, and do not paper over, that direct attempts to measure the theorem's premise (the contraction factor q, and a dose-response test of the attention-dilution lemma) failed or came out sign-reversed, so what is empirically supported is a periodicity-specific deficit of roughly the predicted shape, not a validated instance of the mechanism the theorem describes.

### Major concerns

1. **Issue** — The CTC judge's validity was established on synthetic concatenative audio, not on the naturally-generated, plausibly-monotone audio the paper's own mechanism predicts at high k, leaving open a rival explanation where the "counting deficit" is a judge artifact that tracks k for reasons unrelated to any true generation failure.
   **Where** — Section 3, "Behavioural measurement, and why the obvious judge is wrong": the CTC recognizer is audited only against "concatenative audio with known ground truth" (ratio 1.00), then applied to naturally-generated repeated-phrase audio in Section 4.
   **Why it matters** — The paper's central argument for using CTC instead of Whisper is that Whisper's autoregressive decoder is "subject to the very dynamics under study" — i.e., an instrument can inherit the phenomenon it's measuring. That logic applies with equal force to CTC: the paper's own attention-dilution story (Lemma 1) predicts that the model's internal state — and plausibly its acoustic output (prosody, spectral variation) — becomes progressively less distinguishable across repetitions as k grows, which is exactly the acoustic regime (near-identical, low-variance frames) in which CTC recognizers are known to under-segment via blank-collapse. Concatenated ground-truth audio, built by splicing separately-recorded utterances, does not reproduce this within-utterance acoustic homogenization, so the audit does not establish that CTC counts correctly on the very stimuli it's used to score. If CTC systematically under-counts flatter/more monotone AR output as k rises, the periodicity-specific deficit reported in Fig. 1(a)/Table 1 could be partly or wholly a measurement artifact correlated with, rather than caused by, the model's true rendered count — which would falsify the headline result as stated. Notably, the authors do check for exactly this failure mode for VITS (S13, "blank-collapse would have depressed its repeated items too") but do not report the equivalent check for the six AR checkpoints, where the concern is at least as pressing given the paper's own dilution mechanism.
   **What would address it** — Validate the CTC judge against a stratified sample of naturally-generated high-k repeated-phrase audio with independent human ground-truth counts (not concatenative ground truth), specifically checking whether judge accuracy degrades with k in a way correlated with the reported deficit; report judge error rate as a function of k alongside the count-error curve.

2. **Issue** — The k = 128 extension, the saturating functional form fit to it, and most of the paper's pairwise contrasts (penalty sweep, pool-confound check, family- vs. checkpoint-level splits) are explicitly post hoc and run without any multiple-comparison correction or pre-registered analysis plan.
   **Where** — Section 4.2 ("we extended it, post hoc … S16 dates every analysis decision against the commit log") and the explicit admission that "no multiplicity correction clears 0.05 (S11)."
   **Why it matters** — The paper's strongest claim — that the count "levels off" and the theorem's predicted horizon shape appears — rests on a single post-hoc curve fit (ˆc(k) = K̂(1 − e^{−k/K̂})) chosen after the k ≤ 32 data had already been examined and found not to saturate. No alternative functional forms (power-law, logistic, piecewise-linear-then-flat) are reported as having been fit and rejected, so the reader cannot tell whether the exponential-saturation form was selected because it best captures a real mechanism or because it was the form that, post hoc, produced a headline-compatible "3.5-fold" contrast. Combined with the acknowledged absence of correction across the many additional contrasts reported (penalty sweep on 2 architectures, cycled vs. non-cycled control pool, family-level vs. checkpoint-level medians), the risk of a favorable analysis path being selected from many available ones is real and unaddressed.
   **What would address it** — Report at least one alternative saturating and one non-saturating functional form fit to the same k=128 data with model-comparison statistics (AIC/BIC or cross-validated held-out k values), and either apply a multiplicity correction across the family of reported contrasts or explicitly frame all post-hoc analyses as hypothesis-generating rather than confirmatory (which the current framing, with a numeric "3.5-fold" headline figure, does not fully do).

3. **Issue** — The theorem's mechanistic premise (Assumption 2: contraction) was directly tested twice and failed both times, yet the empirical section continues to present the periodicity-specific deficit as evidence "of the predicted shape."
   **Where** — Section 4.4 and Section 5 ("two attempts to measure q failed … a dose-response test … came out sign-reversed": flatter attention profiles are associated with better counting, r = 0.59, the opposite of what the dilution mechanism predicts).
   **Why it matters** — This is not a minor caveat; it is a disconfirmation of the causal chain the paper is built around (dilution → approximate autonomy → contraction → horizon). The paper's rhetorical move — decoupling "what is proved" (a conditional implication) from "what is measured" (a periodicity-specific effect with a horizon of "the predicted shape") — is honest, but it leaves the reader with a formally verified theorem whose antecedent is empirically unsupported, applied to a phenomenon whose only tested causal signature (dose-response of dilution) points the wrong way. Under an adversarial reading, a positive but mechanism-free correlation between periodicity and a saturating count curve is compatible with many alternative causes (e.g., a generic length/complexity ceiling, memory-limited codec representations, or the judge confound in point 1) that have nothing to do with fixed-point contraction.
   **What would address it** — Either drop the framing that ties the measured saturation to Theorem 1's specific mechanism (present it as a phenomenological horizon only), or invest in a more direct probe of contraction (e.g., a monotone read-head trained specifically to track repetition count, addressing the stated limitation of the boundary-distance estimator) before claiming the empirical horizon is "of the predicted shape."

4. **Issue** — The six-checkpoint panel is underpowered and only partially independent (three of the six checkpoints are same-family scale variants of one Llasa training pipeline), yet is treated as "the unit of replication" for a general claim about AR decoders.
   **Where** — Section 4.2 ("the checkpoint is the unit of replication … The panel is too small for significance testing at either level").
   **Why it matters** — With n = 6 (and effectively fewer independent draws once within-family correlation is accounted for), the signed-rank floor of p = 0.031 the authors cite cannot be reached with any correction, and the sole null result (Qwen3-TTS-1.7B) is a full 1/6 (or 1/3 at the family level) of the panel — a large fraction for a claim framed as holding "in every checkpoint and every family" in the abstract, which oversells what a panel this size and this correlated can support.
   **What would address it** — Either expand the panel with additional independently-trained AR TTS families/checkpoints to reach a pre-specified power target, or soften the abstract's "every checkpoint and every family" language to reflect the explicitly reported exception and the absence of any significance test.

5. **Issue** — The explanation for why VITS is immune and F5-TTS is not ("where the count must live") is a post-hoc hypothesis built from exactly two non-AR data points, but is presented with mechanistic language that risks being read as more than a speculative aside.
   **Where** — Section 4.3, closing paragraph ("What plausibly separates them is where the count must live — a hypothesis formed after seeing them, not a result").
   **Why it matters** — The authors are commendably explicit that this is post hoc, but a two-system comparison cannot discriminate between "duration-per-token vs. total-duration representation" and any number of other architectural differences between VITS and F5-TTS (training data, model scale, flow-matching vs. VAE-based generation, attention structure). Readers may nonetheless carry away a causal story that has n = 2 systems behind it, effectively zero statistical support, and no held-out test.
   **What would address it** — Test the "where the count must live" hypothesis on a third non-AR architecture with a duration mechanism that differs from both VITS and F5-TTS, or explicitly flag the claim as untested speculation in the abstract-level framing (it currently appears only qualified within the body).

6. **Issue** — The related-work discussion omits the existing literature on transformers' counting limitations, which bears directly on Theorem 1's novelty and on whether "periodicity, not length" is itself a new observation.
   **Where** — Section 5, "The closest prior result is representational collapse [14] … Related dynamical and expressivity bounds [15, 16, 17, 18, 19]."
   **Why it matters** — Work such as "The Counting Power of Transformers" [2505.11199], which formally studies which counting properties transformers can and cannot express, and "Counting and Algorithmic Generalization with Transformers" [2310.08661], which shows architectural reasons transformers fail at explicit and implicit counting tasks, are directly on-topic for a paper whose central theorem is a counting-impossibility result, yet neither is discussed. Likewise, [2605.09239] ("Repeated-Token Counting Reveals a Dissociation Between Representations and Outputs" — cited by the authors as reference [13] and confirmed to exist as described) is the paper's chosen "rival" to test against in Section 5, but the surrounding formal counting-expressivity literature that would let a reader judge how much of Theorem 1 is genuinely new versus a speech-domain instantiation of known transformer counting limits is absent. This affects the paper's novelty claim ("to our knowledge the first hallucination result in speech synthesis carried out as a formal artifact") — a claim about *formalization in speech* that may be defensible, but the paper does not clearly separate that narrower claim from the broader "counting collapse" phenomenology, which has closer theoretical antecedents than the ones cited.
   **What would address it** — Add a paragraph situating Theorem 1 against the transformer counting-expressivity literature, clarifying precisely which part of the contribution is new (the Lean formalization; the speech-domain application; the specific contraction-based horizon derivation) versus which part restates known counting limitations in different language.

### Minor concerns

- Reference [1] is cited for its alignment analysis despite being marked "Withdrawn 13 Feb 2026" — worth a sentence on whether the withdrawal affects the specific analysis being relied upon.
- Table 1 shows identical −12.5% count-error values for three different models (Llasa-1B, XTTS-v2, Qwen3-TTS-0.6B); given the stated ±1-unit sensitivity of the exact-match metric, this may reflect coarse discretization of the underlying error distribution rather than genuine convergence, and is worth a footnote.
- The reported confidence-type intervals (e.g., "-8.3% [−12.5, −6.2]", "ˆKctl = 105 [94, 114]") are never labeled with their resampling unit or method (bootstrap over items vs. templates vs. checkpoints) in the main text; this is deferred to unincluded supplementary sections (S10–S16), which the reviewer cannot check.
- Extensive reliance on supplementary sections (S5, S8, S10–S16) not included in the reviewed text means several load-bearing methodological details (statistical test specification in S11, the linear-probe protocol in Section 5, the perturbation-probe design behind the p = 0.12 result) cannot be independently assessed here.
- The abstract's claim that "the limit tracks periodicity, not length, in every checkpoint and every family" sits awkwardly next to the later, more careful statement that the median-error gap is "positive in 5 of 6, zero in Qwen3-TTS-1.7B" — the abstract should be reconciled with the one explicit exception.
- "Capacity gain" (effective-rank slope) in Section 4.4 varies fourfold across checkpoints (0.21–0.80 ratio in Table 1) with no discussion of what drives this range or whether it correlates with the count-error magnitude, which seems like a natural and currently-unexamined cross-check.

### Verdict

**Major revision** — the formal contribution and the length-vs-periodicity dissociation are genuine and unusually transparently caveated, but the central quantitative claims currently rest on an unvalidated judge (CTC accuracy checked only on concatenative, not naturally-monotone, audio), post-hoc curve fitting without correction, and a theorem whose premise the authors' own direct tests failed to confirm or actively contradicted.

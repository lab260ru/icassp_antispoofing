STANCE: Skeptical-but-fair

### Summary
The paper asks whether autoregressive TTS models' well-documented failure to correctly render text with a many-times-repeated word is caused by the repeated text being *long* or by it being *periodic*. Using a "repetition ladder" of 180 stimuli (a target word repeated k times, each paired with a length-matched control in which the repeated token is swapped for distinct fillers) and a companion "period ladder" that holds carrier, word count and requested count fixed while varying only the recurrence period, the authors find that six checkpoints across three TTS architecture families render repeated text exactly only 18.2% of the time against 94.3% for length-matched controls, that exact-rendering rate rises monotonically with period (10.4% → 93.1% for p = 1, 2, 4, 8), and that roughly half the deficit survives even at period 2 (no token adjacent to itself). They show this is not an artifact of the Whisper judge (which they demonstrate is itself autoregressive and biased against the phenomenon) by re-scoring with a CTC recognizer and three additional recognizers, and they formally derive (in Lean 4) a contraction-based bound on why counting should fail, then measure the relevant quantity (top singular value of a boundary-to-boundary Jacobian) and reject the bound's premise in every checkpoint measured (q = 21–348, always expansive, never contractive).

### Major concerns

1. **Issue** — The "length-matched, non-periodic" control itself contains periodic structure at high k, because its filler vocabulary is drawn from a pool of only eight items cycled once k > 8, and the construction of the "never-cycled" alternative control is not specified in enough detail to confirm it is actually periodicity-free.
   **Where** — Section 2, "Stimuli" ("replaced by fillers from a per-template pool of eight, cycled above k = 8") and Section 3.1 ("Never-cycled control fillers throughout put the gap at 69.6... against 76.7... cycled").
   **Why it matters** — The paper's central claim is that the deficit tracks *periodicity, not length*, and the period ladder (Fig. 1b) is presented as the decisive test of exactly this. But if the primary "control" condition at k = 16–32 recycles the same 8 fillers, it reintroduces a period-8 signal into the arm that is supposed to represent "length only, no periodicity." Given the paper's own finding that period-8 items already recover 93.1% correctness (i.e., period-8 periodicity is nearly as benign as no periodicity at all), this may not bias the comparison much in practice — but the paper never shows this directly for the *control* arm specifically, and it never explains how the never-cycled variant sources non-repeating fillers at k = 32 without either a much larger pool (unstated size) or reintroducing some other confound (e.g. drawing from a different lexical frequency distribution). A 7.1-point difference between cycled and never-cycled conditions is reported but not decomposed into a "periodicity introduced into the control" explanation versus the "vocabulary" explanation the authors offer.
   **What would address it** — Report the never-cycled filler pool's size and sourcing procedure explicitly, and report exact-match rates for the control arm alone, stratified by whether a given control item's fillers repeat within the item (period-8-in-control) versus not, to directly test whether the residual 7.1-point gap is attributable to this leakage.

2. **Issue** — The paper discloses, but does not fully neutralize, extensive post-hoc/researcher-degrees-of-freedom exposure: the k ≥ 6 threshold was chosen after seeing where the arms diverge, and the study reports "420 specifications... and we correct across none of them," resting instead on the specification curve never reversing sign.
   **Where** — Section 3.1, penultimate paragraph ("The k ≥ 6 threshold was chosen after seeing where the arms diverge...") and "Across 420 specifications spanning every forking choice we made... we correct across none of them."
   **Why it matters** — The honesty here is commendable, and effect-size robustness across a wide specification range is a reasonable substitute for correction when the tests are not independent. But the paper does not clearly separate a locked, pre-specified primary analysis from the exploratory specification search — the number that anchors the abstract (67.6/75.4/46.8 points) is itself one point on a curve whose endpoints were chosen with knowledge of the data. The one place the paper *does* practice genuine pre-registration (the CosyVoice 2 prediction, committed via a git commit hash before the checkpoint existed) is presented as unusually rigorous precisely because it is the exception rather than the rule in this study.
   **What would address it** — State explicitly which single specification (if any) is the pre-specified primary outcome and which are confirmatory sensitivity checks run afterward; if none was pre-registered, say so plainly rather than let the CosyVoice 2 example imply a pre-registration norm that the rest of the study does not follow.

3. **Issue** — External validation of the "periodicity, not length" claim beyond the original panel rests on a single additional checkpoint (CosyVoice 2), which was selected specifically because it was "the design most likely to be immune."
   **Where** — Section 3.1, "CosyVoice 2, whose alignment supervision makes it the design most likely to be immune..." (n = 90).
   **Why it matters** — Using a targeted extreme case to test a partial-pooling prediction interval is a legitimate falsification strategy (and it is commendable that the result disconfirmed immunity), but it is not a random draw from the population of untested architectures, so it cannot straightforwardly validate the posterior predictive interval [5.9, 93.3] the way an arbitrarily chosen held-out architecture would. The paper's own text acknowledges "three groups barely identify a group-level scale," so one additional, non-randomly-selected data point is thin support for generalizing the family-level gap beyond the three architectures in the core panel.
   **What would address it** — Either add a second, non-adversarially-selected held-out architecture, or reframe the CosyVoice 2 result explicitly as a falsification test of the "immunity via alignment supervision" hypothesis rather than as validation of the partial-pooling extrapolation.

4. **Issue** — The instrument used for nearly every quantitative claim (the CTC recognizer) is validated for count accuracy only on synthetic, concatenative audio with known ground truth (n = 12 trials, 6 donors), not on the genuinely pathological (looping, truncated, degenerate) audio the models actually produce when they fail.
   **Where** — Section 2, "Behavioural measurement" ("Audited against concatenative audio with known ground truth... n=12 over 6 donors... ratio 1.00 (n=24, mean 0.996)"); acknowledged as a limitation in Section 5 ("the judge is validated on concatenative audio, not on listeners").
   **Why it matters** — Concatenative audio (clean, individually-recorded repetitions spliced together) is acoustically much better-behaved than the audio a model produces when it loops, garbles or truncates a repeated phrase — exactly the audio whose transcription accuracy is most in question. A CTC recognizer's blank-collapse and greedy decoding could behave differently on genuinely garbled/looping speech than on clean concatenated repeats, and this domain gap is not tested. The n = 12/6-donor sample is also small for a validation that anchors the entire measurement pipeline.
   **What would address it** — Validate the CTC judge (and comparison recognizers) against a sample of actual model outputs with independently-obtained (e.g. crowd-sourced or expert) human transcription ground truth, not only against clean concatenative audio; the paper mentions a 165-clip stratified sample shipped "so a judgement can be checked by ear" — formalizing this as an inter-rater-agreement study against the automated judge would close this gap directly.

5. **Issue** — No blinding protocol is described for any manual verification step, including the 165-clip audit sample and the exclusion-rule decisions (e.g., "342 excluded in one template whose fillers the judge never transcribes," "19 degenerate").
   **Where** — Section 2, "Code, data, settings" (165-clip sample) and the exclusion tally in "Behavioural measurement."
   **Why it matters** — Exclusion of "degenerate" generations and template-level filler-transcription failures are judgment calls that could, even unintentionally, be applied more leniently to one arm (repeated vs. control) if the person making the call is not blind to condition. The paper states "exclusions are blind to the count" (Section 5) but this addresses blindness to the *count value*, not to arm identity, and does not describe a blinding protocol for the manual audit itself.
   **What would address it** — State explicitly whether whoever adjudicated exclusions and conducted the by-ear audit was blind to condition (repeated vs. control) and to model/checkpoint identity, or report inter-rater agreement between a blinded second annotator and the reported exclusion decisions.

### Minor concerns
- The p = k "hollow marker" arm in Fig. 1(b) is explicitly "matched to neither in vocabulary nor length" to the other rungs, yet is visually juxtaposed with them in the same plot in a way that invites over-interpretation; the text's odd-period tests (p=3, p=6 at k=24) don't state whether these share the p=2/4/8 rungs' 8-word filler pool.
- The abstract and Section 3.1 pack many closely related numeric quantities (67.6, 75.4, 46.8, 76.4, 76.7, 65.1–76.7, 61.7...) without a summary table cross-referencing which condition/exclusion regime each corresponds to; a reader without the supplement will struggle to track which of these is the "headline" number.
- "RSS 2.6 against 485.3" (Section 3.1, saturation discussion) is reported without units or a description of what RSS is computed over, making the claim unverifiable from the text alone.
- The Introduction cites [3,4] for attention-supervision/decoding-rule fixes to TTS repetition instability but omits closely related prior work explicitly testing attention robustness to repeating/skipping text via stepwise monotonic attention (e.g. [1906.00672]), which would strengthen the related-work framing of what "the field's standard mitigations" actually are.
- Minor prose inconsistency: the paper alternates between calling the audit sample "stratified 165-clip" and describing it only descriptively elsewhere; a single canonical description (with sampling method) would help.
- Reference [7] ("companion" spectral hallucination paper) and [13] (Venkatesh) are dated 2026 preprints; both were confirmed to exist via arXiv search, but readers should be aware the companion paper is very recent and its findings are only lightly integrated ("The work is a companion to...") rather than substantively cross-validated here.

### Verdict
**Major revision** — the core periodicity-vs-length manipulation and the multi-recognizer, multi-checkpoint robustness checks are a genuinely strong design, but the control arm's own potential periodicity leakage, thin/domain-mismatched judge validation, and reliance on a single non-random held-out architecture for generalization need to be resolved or explicitly bounded before the headline effect sizes can be taken at face value.

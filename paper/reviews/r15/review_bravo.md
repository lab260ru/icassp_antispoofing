# Review by bravo

## Summary

The paper makes three claims. (1) A formal one: if a decoder's per-repetition state update contracts (Lipschitz constant q<1) and attention over repeated spans dilutes as Θ(1/k), then no Lipschitz readout — including the stop-token logit — can distinguish repetition counts past a horizon N* that the paper derives in closed form; this is machine-checked in Lean 4/mathlib. (2) A behavioural one: six checkpoints across three TTS architectures undercount repeated phrases (median error ≈ -8 to -12% at k≥6, 18.2% exact) while matched non-repeating controls of identical length are essentially perfect (94.3% exact) — periodicity, not length, drives the failure. (3) A negative one, stated with unusual candour: the paper's own attempts to verify the theorem's premises mostly fail — two attempts to measure the contraction factor came up empty, the dilution lemma's dose-response prediction reverses sign, and the theorem's most distinctive prediction (no readout works past the horizon) replicates in only one of the two checkpoints tested.

This is a paper I want to like more than I trust. The periodicity-vs-length dissociation (§4.3) is a genuinely well-designed distinguishing experiment, the ablations against the decoding-rule and duration-model rivals are targeted rather than perfunctory, and the self-undermining honesty in §4.4/§5 is rare and should be rewarded, not penalized. But the paper's own reported statistics — n=6 checkpoints that reduce to n=3 independent architecture families, a sign-reversed dose-response test, and a decisive replication test run on n=2 — do not support the confidence with which the abstract states its behavioural numbers. The formal theorem is real but its premises remain, by the authors' own account, unmeasured; the behavioural phenomenon is real but its mechanism is not established by anything in this paper. Framework checks that produced nothing: I found no evidence of cherry-picked qualitative examples (the paper is entirely quantitative, releases a stratified 165-clip sample for independent audit, and doesn't lean on hand-picked transcripts); the baselines (VITS, F5-TTS, CTC vs. Whisper judge) are genuinely adversarial to the authors' own architectural hypothesis rather than strawmen, and the paper withdraws a claim (architectural immunity) when a baseline disagrees with it — a good sign, not a bad one.

## Major concerns

1. **Issue** — The abstract front-loads confirmatory-sounding language and defers the premise-failure admission to its final sentence, risking a misleading takeaway for a reader who skims. `[Narrative — overclaiming]`
   **Where** — Abstract: the theorem is stated in the first four sentences as established machinery ("we prove that... no Lipschitz readout... can separate repetition counts beyond a horizon"), followed by several confirmatory-sounding empirical numbers, with "We do not establish the theorem's premise" arriving only in the second-to-last sentence.
   **Why it matters** — A reader who stops at "models render repeated text with a median count error of -8.3%... the limit tracks periodicity, not length" (which is most of the abstract) would reasonably conclude the contraction/dilution mechanism is empirically confirmed. It isn't — by the authors' own account in §5, two premise measurements failed and the dose-response test reversed sign.
   **What would address it** — Move the premise-failure sentence earlier, or restructure the abstract to separate "what is proved" from "what is measured" from "what is not yet established," mirroring the paper's own (good) discipline in §2 and §5.

2. **Issue** — The repetition-penalty ablation cannot rule out "learned dispreference for verbatim repetition," the alternative explanation the paper itself names as the one it cannot exclude. `[Evidence — alternative explanations]`
   **Where** — §4.3, penalty sweep ("Swept on 2 architectures... the deficit stays put") and §S20 ("three we cannot exclude, chiefly a learned dispreference for verbatim repetition").
   **Why it matters** — Varying the decoding-time repetition-penalty hyperparameter tests whether a decoding-time knob explains the effect; it says nothing about whether the base logits already encode a trained-in aversion to verbatim repetition (e.g., from training data or objective) that would produce the same periodicity/length dissociation with no contraction dynamics at all. The paper flags this rival but never designs an experiment that touches it.
   **What would address it** — A test that manipulates training-time exposure to verbatim repetition (e.g., fine-tuning on synthetic repetition-heavy data, or comparing checkpoints with known differences in repetition-heavy pretraining data) rather than only decoding-time penalty.

3. **Issue** — The CTC judge's validity is established only against synthetic concatenative ground truth, not against genuinely model-generated degenerate audio — the exact case the judge is deployed to score. `[Limitations]`
   **Where** — §3, "Audited against concatenative audio with known ground truth its counted/true ratio falls to 0.27–0.65 for k≥4... We score instead with a CTC recogniser... put through the same audit: ratio 1.00 on periodic ground truth against 0.19 for Whisper."
   **Why it matters** — Concatenative audio is clean and artificially spliced; it doesn't reproduce the acoustic pathologies (prosodic monotony, degenerate looping, the "19 degenerate" generations excluded separately) that real model failures produce. A judge validated only on clean synthetic audio could still be systematically biased on the genuinely pathological generations that carry the paper's effect, and this is never tested. No sample size is given for the audit itself.
   **What would address it** — Validate the judge against a sample of model-generated audio with independently established (e.g., human-transcribed) ground truth, not only concatenative audio, and report the audit's n.

4. **Issue** — The paper's own attempt to distinguish "representational collapse" from the "output-policy-only fails" rival [13] is markedly weaker than the cited original result it's trying to extend, and doesn't actually adjudicate between the two hypotheses. `[Novelty — weak distinguishing test]`
   **Where** — §5: "A linear probe does not separate this from the rival on which the count survives and only the output policy fails [13]: it retains less on repeated text in 6 of 6 checkpoints, but the count stays decodable late (R2 0.48)."
   **Why it matters** — I verified [13] (Sohan Venkatesh, "Repeated-Token Counting Reveals a Dissociation Between Representations and Outputs," [2605.09239]) via arXiv search: its abstract reports linear probes decoding the correct count "with near-perfect accuracy at every post-embedding layer... even at the exact layers where the wrong answer crystallizes in the output." The paper under review's analogous probe gets R²=0.48 and, per its own §5, "holds in 1 of 2" checkpoints — a coin flip, not a replication. Citing [13] accurately as the rival hypothesis is fine; treating a noisy, non-replicating R²=0.48 probe as informed engagement with a "near-perfect" published result is not. The paper doesn't explain why its signal is so much weaker (different modality? decoder architecture? probe design?) — that gap is left unaddressed.
   **What would address it** — Either strengthen the probe (more layers, larger probe capacity, more checkpoints) to actually contest [13]'s near-perfect result, or downgrade the claim from "tests the theorem" to "a preliminary, inconclusive probe."

5. **Issue** — Headline effect sizes are computed and bootstrapped over n=6 checkpoints that the authors' own method section admits are really n=3 independent architecture families, and the paper still reports narrow-looking bootstrap CIs from this. `[Statistical rigour]`
   **Where** — §3 ("six checkpoints but only three designs, scale variants making up the difference"); §4.2 ("the exact rate favours the control in all 6 (mean 76.7 [68.4, 84.4] points)... By 3 families: 75.4 points, [71.1, 78.9]"); Fig. 1(c) caption ("95% bootstrap intervals over stimulus templates," template count unstated).
   **Why it matters** — Percentile bootstrap CIs computed over 3–6 points are not meaningfully informative about sampling variability — the resampling distribution of a statistic from n=3–6 draws can't approximate a population distribution, and the CI width mostly reflects arbitrary discreteness rather than genuine uncertainty. The paper is honest that "the panel is too small for significance testing" and that no multiplicity-corrected test clears 0.05, but it still reports bootstrap intervals as if they carry real information, and the abstract's headline "-8.3% [−12.5, −6.2]" pooled-item figure is explicitly the statistic the body says "ignores clustering" and that the authors "lean on the [per-checkpoint median] first" instead — yet it's the number in the abstract, not the one they trust more.
   **What would address it** — Either drop the small-n bootstrap CIs in favour of plainly stated ranges across the 3 families, or use a mixed-effects model with checkpoint/family as a random effect and report that instead of pooled item-level CIs alongside architecture-level means as if they were comparable evidence.

6. **Issue** — The theorem's single most distinctive, headline-quoted confirmatory test is run on n=2 checkpoints and succeeds in exactly one — statistically indistinguishable from chance, yet presented in the abstract as supporting evidence. `[Statistical rigour]`
   **Where** — Abstract: "Its conclusion survives one direct test... in 1 of the 2 checkpoints we could run it on." §5: "the prediction holds in 1 of 2, a failure to replicate (S12)."
   **Where** — §5, Llasa-1B vs. Llasa-8B probe comparison.
   **Why it matters** — With n=2, "1 of 2" carries zero evidential weight beyond a coin flip (p=0.5 under a null of no effect either way); calling this "surviving one direct test" in the abstract implies more than the data can bear. This compounds concern #5: the paper's most theory-relevant test — the one actually designed to falsify Theorem 1(iv) rather than just describe behaviour — is the least statistically powered thing in the paper, and it's foregrounded rather than caveated in the abstract. Combined with the sign-reversed dose-response test (r=0.59 in the wrong direction) and the two failed contraction-factor measurements, the paper has essentially zero surviving confirmatory evidence for its own causal mechanism, and the abstract's phrasing doesn't make that plain.
   **What would address it** — Either run the probe test on more checkpoints before reporting it as informative, or state explicitly in the abstract (not just §5) that n=2 makes this uninterpretable as replication evidence, and that the theorem's premises remain, on net, unconfirmed rather than "surviving one direct test."

## Minor concerns

- Reference [1] is cited as support for "its alignment analysis" without noting it was withdrawn (13 Feb 2026) — worth a sentence on why, since a withdrawal can mean the analysis itself was retracted.
- Repo name (`icassp_antispoofing`) is unrelated to the paper's topic ("name predates this study") — a minor discoverability/verifiability friction for reviewers trying to locate the artifact independently.
- Fig. 1's extracted axis labels ("22 25 k / 100 0 count error(%)") are hard to parse from the paper text alone — possibly a PDF-extraction artifact rather than an authorial issue, but as delivered the figure isn't independently checkable against its caption without the actual rendered plot.
- The abstract buries "(n=6, too small to test)" mid-sentence right after quoting the headline periodicity claim — easy to miss on a skim; deserves its own clause or footnote-style prominence.
- Table 1 reports per-checkpoint point estimates (e.g., capacity-gain ratio) with no per-row uncertainty, while Fig. 1(c) reports pooled bootstrap CIs — inconsistent granularity makes it hard to tell which numbers in the table are trustworthy point estimates vs. noise.
- The "two attempts to measure q" (§5) are described narratively rather than tabulated; a small table of estimator, sample, and result would make this auditable rather than prose-summarized.
- The paper's motivating "attractor regime" framing leans on a same-author companion preprint [7] (arXiv:2604.08591, 2026) that is itself unreviewed — fine as a self-citation, but worth flagging that the phenomenon's grounding outside this paper is not yet independently replicated.

## Belief update

I moved my belief in the narrow behavioural claim — that AR TTS repetition failures track periodicity specifically, not sequence length, attention span, or exposure bias generally — up moderately; the length-matched control design (§4.3) is the paper's strongest experiment and it's hard to explain the 94.3% vs. 18.2% gap any other way. I did not update on the proposed mechanism (contraction of a per-repetition state map plus attention dilution) being the actual cause in real transformer decoders: the paper's own premise tests failed twice, its dose-response test reversed sign, and its decisive replication test ran at n=2. The Lean formalization is a real artifact but formalizes an implication whose premises remain unmeasured in every checkpoint tested, so it doesn't move my belief about transformers — only about what such formalizations can and can't buy empirically. Net: real update on the phenomenon, no update on the theory that's supposed to explain it.

## Verdict

**Major revision** — the abstract states behavioural findings with a confidence the paper's own statistics (n=3 independent architecture families behind the "n=6" framing, and a decisive replication test run on n=2 that succeeds once) don't support, and the theorem's causal mechanism remains, by the authors' own admission, unconfirmed rather than merely "not yet explained."

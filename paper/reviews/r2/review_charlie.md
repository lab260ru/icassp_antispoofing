# Review by charlie

### Summary

The paper makes three claims: (1) repetition-induced counting failure in AR TTS is a *structural* consequence of a decoder's per-repetition update contracting to a fixed point, proved as an implication in Lean 4; (2) empirically, five (the paper actually enumerates six, and later analyses use seven — see below) TTS checkpoints show a finite counting horizon k* that tracks periodicity rather than sequence length, via a length-matched control; (3) the theorem's own predicted consequence — shrinking distinguishable-state count — is directly observable as a slower effective-rank growth under repetition than under controls. The periodicity-vs-length contrast (Fig. 1b) is the paper's best result and is hard to explain away. But the paper's evidentiary chain has real gaps: the empirical premise that would make the theorem *apply* (contraction, q<1) is explicitly reported as unmeasurable by the authors' own method (§4.4), so "formally verified" is certifying an if-then whose "if" is never confirmed; the biggest checkpoint in the one true within-architecture scale comparison (Llasa-8B) is silently missing from every accuracy result while still contributing to the headline capacity statistic; and the control experiment meant to rule out a "naturalness" confound scores text under an unnamed "general-purpose causal LM" that — I checked the released code — is literally the fine-tuned backbone of one of the models under test. None of this makes the core contrast false, but it means several of the paper's most quotable numbers are less independent, and less disclosed, than the text implies.

### Major concerns

1. **Issue** — The paper's title and formal-verification framing overclaim relative to what was actually established, because the one thing that would make Theorem 1 apply to real decoders (contraction, q<1) is exactly what the authors report they could not measure. `[Narrative — overclaiming]`
   **Where** — Title ("A Formally Verified Attractor Theory of Repetition Hallucination"); §4.4 ("That estimator is unsound here... the pooled fit returns q ≈ 1.00 with R² ≈ 0" per the released decision log, though the paper text itself only says the localisation "collapses onto near-duplicate positions").
   **Why it matters** — A reader skimming the title and abstract ("we prove that if F contracts... boundary states converge geometrically") could reasonably walk away believing the collapse phenomenon itself was formally verified in real models. What was verified is a piece of pure math (Banach fixed point ⇒ finite distinguishability horizon) that the paper itself says it cannot connect to any specific measured q for any specific checkpoint. The Discussion is honest about this in prose ("It does not prove that any transformer contracts"), but the title and abstract don't carry that hedge, and no table anywhere computes a predicted N* from measured constants (L, C, µ, q) and compares it to the observed k* — the formula in Eq. (1) is never actually instantiated on data.
   **What would address it** — Either report an estimated q (even a crude one, with wide error bars, flagged as unreliable) and show the resulting N* against observed k*, or retitle/re-abstract to make explicit that "formally verified" refers only to the implication, not to any claim that a given TTS decoder satisfies its premise.

2. **Issue** — Llasa-8B, the top rung of the paper's only true within-architecture scale ladder (1B/3B/8B), has no counting-accuracy data anywhere in the paper, despite having been generated and instrumented; the paper's own count of "how many checkpoints" is internally inconsistent across sections. `[Evidence — selective reporting / Reproducibility]`
   **Where** — §3 "Models" says "five checkpoints" but lists six (Llasa 1B/3B/8B + XTTS-v2 + Qwen3-TTS 0.6B/1.7B); Table 1 has five rows and omits Llasa-8B entirely; §4.3 and the Fig. 1(c,d) legend refer to "7 instrumented checkpoints" (the six plus the xtts2norp ablation). I inspected the released repository backing this submission: `logs/gen_llasa8b.log` and `logs/state_llasa8b.log` show Llasa-8B was fully generated and instrumented, its capacity-gain ratio appears in `data/results/capacity.json` (0.43, folding into the "6 of 7 disjoint intervals" claim), but it is absent from `data/results/behavioural.csv` and `summary.json` — no k*, no accuracy at any k, for any of the 276 stimulus items. The authors' own working notes (`findings.md`, "Open Questions") still list "Does q̂ vary monotonically with scale within the Llasa 1B/3B/8B ladder?" as unresolved.
   **Why it matters** — The paper explicitly motivates the Llasa ladder as testing whether "scale buys a longer horizon" and only reports that pattern for Qwen3-TTS (0.6B→1.7B doubles k*). Without knowing whether Llasa-8B's k* continues, plateaus, or reverses the 1B→3B trend (k*=1→3), a reader cannot tell whether this is an incomplete run or a result that didn't fit the "scale extends horizon" story and was quietly dropped from the accuracy tables while its (favorable) capacity number was kept. Either explanation should be stated, not left for a reader with repo access to reconstruct.
   **What would address it** — Report Llasa-8B's k* and accuracy curve (or explicitly state why it's missing — compute/ASR failure, time constraint, etc.), and reconcile "five checkpoints" with the six-to-seven models actually used across different analyses.

3. **Issue** — The paper's central theoretical mechanism (representational indistinguishability of distinct inputs under a Lipschitz readout, in a decoder-only transformer) closely overlaps prior work that is cited but under-engaged, and whose bibliography entry is broken. `[Novelty]`
   **Where** — §5 "Relation to prior results" cites [10, 11] only for "deep transformers lose rank and over-squash"; reference [11] in the bibliography reads "Federico Barbero, Andrea Banino, Steven Kaptur-owski, et al., ,” .” — blank title, blank venue, no arXiv ID.
   **Why it matters** — I located the paper via arXiv: Barbero, Banino, Kapturowski et al., "Transformers need glasses! Information over-squashing in language tasks" [2406.04267], which proves exactly a "representational collapse phenomenon" where distinct input sequences become indistinguishable in the final-layer representation used for next-token prediction in decoder-only Transformers — mechanistically very close to this paper's Theorem 1(ii)–(iv). A reader cannot even identify what [11] is from the reference list as printed, and the one sentence devoted to it undersells how directly related its result is. This doesn't sink the paper's novelty (the TTS/counting framing, the periodicity-specific control, and the Lean artifact are genuinely distinct contributions), but the "to our knowledge the first hallucination result in speech synthesis carried out as a formal artifact" framing would be more honest if it engaged with how much of the *underlying mechanism* is already established.
   **What would address it** — Fix the broken bibliography entry, and add a paragraph distinguishing what's new (periodicity-indexed horizon, counting-specific consequence, TTS domain, machine-checked artifact) from what over-squashing/representational-collapse work already shows (indistinguishability under repeated/near-duplicate conditioning in general).

4. **Issue** — The effective-rank (Neff) decline used as "the theorem's own consequence... directly observable" doesn't distinguish contraction-to-a-fixed-point from other dynamics that would produce the same signature. `[Evidence — distinguishing test]`
   **Where** — §4.3, Fig. 1(c,d).
   **Why it matters** — Theorem 1 specifically requires convergence to a *unique fixed point* (or at least Cauchy convergence). A stable non-converging limit cycle, or simple activation saturation/clipping under near-identical repeated input, would also produce a plateauing effective rank and a lower capacity-gain slope under repetition than under diverse control text — without any fixed point ever being approached. The paper doesn't propose or run a test that would separate these (e.g., checking whether late-trajectory states cluster near a single point vs. cycle among several). "Directly observable" oversells what a rank statistic alone can show about *why* the rank is low.
   **Why it matters (cont.)** — This weakens the claim that the measurement is a validation of contraction specifically, as opposed to any bounded-diversity dynamic under periodic conditioning.
   **What would address it** — A test for convergence-to-a-point vs. cycling (e.g., pairwise distances among the last several boundary states within a trajectory) would directly discriminate the two.

5. **Issue** — The conjunctive scoring rule (exact transcript count AND duration-consistency) shows clear signs of being shaped iteratively by looking at results, with no statement that the protocol was frozen before generation. `[Evidence — pre-registration]`
   **Where** — §3 "Behavioural measurement" (paper text only describes the final rule). The released decision log documents that control scoring was originally wrong ("Controls had expected_count=0... every control then looked like a runaway loop and scored 0.00... at high k," fixed by "scoring both item types with one rule") and that the duration model and boundary-unit handling were added/changed after an initial analysis pass produced a bug.
   **Why it matters** — None of these fixes look malicious — they read as legitimate bug fixes — but the paper presents the final conjunctive rule as if it were the a priori design, and the specific choice to fit the duration-consistency band "on the low-k regime" is exactly the kind of post-hoc threshold choice this framework flags. A protocol that changed shape after seeing how it scored the very comparison (repeated vs. control) that is the paper's sharpest test deserves an explicit pre-registration statement, or at minimum acknowledgment that it was iterated.
   **What would address it** — State whether the final scoring rule (including the low-k-fitted duration band) was frozen before any model's outputs were scored, or report accuracy under the scoring rule as originally specified alongside the corrected one.

6. **Issue** — The limitations paragraph is honest about several things (English-only, text attention unavailable for Qwen3-TTS, unpropagated residual term) but omits concrete, checkable gaps in the paper's own evidence base. `[Limitations]`
   **Where** — §5 "Implications and limits" (absent: no mention of missing Llasa-8B data, no mention of small per-(model, k) cell sizes at the extreme end of the ladder — several high-k cells are on the order of n≈18 per model, before any bootstrap or pooling).
   **Why it matters** — A limitations section that lists the safe, already-flagged caveats but not the two things that would most directly qualify the headline numbers (an incomplete scale-ladder model, and thin samples at the k values where the "collapse" is defined) reads as a partial accounting rather than the full one.
   **What would address it** — Add both, with the per-cell n reported directly in Table 1 or its caption.

7. **Issue** — The control comparisons meant to rule out competing explanations rest on weak or non-independent baselines: the "naturalness" check uses one of the tested models' own backbone as referee, and the headline pooled accuracy/capacity statistics quietly fold in a non-independent ablation variant as if it were an additional checkpoint. `[Evidence — baselines]`
   **Where** — §4.2 ("Scored under a general-purpose causal LM..."); Abstract ("11.1% vs. 48.9% at k ≥ 6", "a factor 0.57 of the control rate"); §4.3 ("6 of the 7 instrumented checkpoints").
   **Why it matters** — The paper never names the "general-purpose causal LM" used to score text naturalness. I checked the released code (`analysis/text_perplexity.py`): the `--lm` argument defaults to, and was run with, `HKUSTAudio/Llasa-1B` — the exact fine-tuned Llama backbone underlying one of the five/six/seven TTS checkpoints under test, justified in a code comment only as "already local and... a reasonable general-purpose text LM for this purpose." This is circular in a way the paper hides from readers: a model that is itself one of the systems whose behavior on repeated text is under investigation is being used as the independent arbiter of whether repeated text is "natural." It also isn't a strong test of the naturalness hypothesis on its own terms — causal LMs assign trivially low NLL to literal repetition via induction-head-style copying almost by construction, so "the repeated item is more probable than its control" is close to a tautology regardless of which LM scores it; finding the opposite direction (control less probable) doesn't require a genuine "naturalness" mechanism to be at work, and no comparison against a standard, independent LM (e.g., a base LM not derived from any tested TTS system) is offered to check whether the effect is an artifact of that specific backbone. Separately, the headline pooled numbers ("11.1% vs. 48.9%", the "0.57" capacity ratio, "6 of 7 disjoint intervals") are computed over a set that includes `xtts2norp` — XTTS-v2 with its repetition penalty disabled, an ablation of an already-included architecture, not an independent 6th/7th model — without the abstract disclosing that one architecture is effectively double-counted in its own headline statistics. I confirmed both directly against the released result files: `data/results/text_nll.json` reproduces the paper's 89%/16.1/14.9 numbers exactly under the Llasa-1B default, and the unweighted mean of `acc_rep_highk`/`acc_ctl_highk` over exactly {llasa1b, llasa3b, qwen06b, qwen17b, xtts2, xtts2norp} reproduces 11.1%/48.9% exactly — i.e., Llasa-8B is excluded and xtts2norp is included in the very statistic the abstract leads with.
   **What would address it** — Name the LM used for the naturalness check in the paper text, re-run it with a genuinely independent, non-TTS-derived LM (or several, to show robustness), and either exclude the ablation variant from headline pooled statistics or explicitly report it as a 6th data point with the architecture-duplication flagged.

### Minor concerns

- Reference [11]'s bibliography entry is blank (no title, venue, or arXiv ID) — uninterpretable from the paper alone; it is Barbero et al., "Transformers need glasses! Information over-squashing in language tasks" [2406.04267].
- Reference [1] is cited in the introduction to support "well documented as a stability problem," but its own entry notes it was "Withdrawn 13 Feb 2026" — leaning on a withdrawn preprint, even hedged as "cited for its alignment analysis," is worth a second citation to back it up.
- Decoding hyperparameters (temperature, top-p, repetition penalty settings other than XTTS-v2's) are not stated in the paper text itself, only recoverable from code.
- Fig. 1(c) y-axis label appears truncated ("eff" rather than "N_eff") in the panel as transcribed; worth double-checking the rendered figure.
- "Deep probe layers" (§4.3, Table 1 caption) is never pinned to specific layer indices in the paper text.
- No p-values or formal significance tests are reported anywhere; all claims rest on bootstrap CIs and raw percentages/fractions, which is fine, but some per-(model,k) cells underlying those percentages are quite small (~18) and this isn't surfaced.
- "µ", "L", "C" in Eq. (1) are never given numeric values for any real checkpoint, so the "explicit horizon" is asserted but never computed on data (ties to Major concern 1).

### Belief update

This paper does move me, modestly, toward believing that AR-TTS repetition failure is better explained by *periodicity* than by *length* per se — the matched-pair control contrast (11.1% vs. 48.9%) is a genuinely well-designed test and the direction is clean across a same-carrier, same-word-count comparison. I don't come away more confident in the specific *contraction-to-a-fixed-point* mechanistic story, since the empirical premise that would establish it (q<1) is explicitly unmeasured, the observable proxy (effective rank) doesn't distinguish it from other bounded dynamics, and the closest confirmatory experiment (naturalness ablation) turns out to use a non-independent referee. I also don't update toward "this is a novel mechanism" much beyond what over-squashing/representational-collapse work (Barbero et al. 2024, cited but under-engaged) already established at a more general level — this paper's contribution is better read as "that mechanism, instantiated for periodicity and counting, in speech." Net: real update on the descriptive finding (periodicity, not length), near-zero update on the causal/mechanistic story.

### Verdict

**Major revision** — the periodicity-vs-length result is worth publishing, but the paper needs to disclose that its naturalness-confound control is scored by one of the tested models' own backbone and that its headline pooled statistics quietly include a non-independent ablation variant as if it were an additional checkpoint, before its "no property of the decoding rule enters" and "directly observable" claims can be taken at face value.

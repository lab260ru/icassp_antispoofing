### Summary

The paper asks whether autoregressive TTS models' well-known tendency to mis-count, loop, or truncate repeated phrases is a "structural limit" rather than a sampling artifact. The authors model one repetition cycle as a state map $F$ on the decoder's hidden state, prove (in Lean 4/mathlib) that if $F$ is a contraction ($q<1$), any Lipschitz readout — including a stop head — loses the ability to distinguish repetition counts past a computable horizon $N^*$, and pair this with an attention-dilution lemma showing that softmax attention over $k$ identical spans flattens toward uniform. Empirically, across five checkpoints (Llasa-1B/3B/8B, XTTS-v2, Qwen3-TTS-0.6B/1.7B) on a 180-item repetition ladder with length-matched non-repetitive controls, counting accuracy collapses past a model-specific $k^*$ (11.1% vs. 48.9% for controls at $k \geq 6$), and the effective rank of generation trajectories grows more slowly under repetition (capacity gain ratio ≈0.57 of control). The headline claim is that repetition-induced hallucination is a provable consequence of state contraction under periodic conditioning, not exposure bias or length effects.

### Major concerns

1. **Issue** — The ground-truth measurement instrument (Whisper large-v3 transcription + a fitted duration model) is itself documented to hallucinate on atypical/repetitive audio, and this is never independently validated against human transcription.
   **Where** — Section 3, "Behavioural measurement"; Table 1 and Fig. 1(a,b) depend entirely on this pipeline.
   **Why it matters** — The paper explicitly acknowledges one failure direction ("the judge is itself an autoregressive model that de-duplicates repeated material, so a transcript count alone understates loops") but never bounds the opposite failure mode. Whisper is independently reported to hallucinate content on non-speech and atypical audio segments ([2501.11378]), and follow-up work studies exactly this failure surface for Whisper large-v3 ([2606.23060]). Since the stimuli under test are, by design, unusual acoustic sequences (identical word repeated up to 32 times), the judge is being asked to transcribe precisely the input class where it is least validated. If Whisper's own reliability degrades with $k$ for reasons unrelated to what the TTS model produced, the paper's central dependent variable — "counting accuracy collapses past $k^*$" — is confounded by the measurement instrument sharing the same repetition-sensitive failure mode as the system under test, and no human-verified subsample is reported to rule this out.
   **What would address it** — Report human transcription/agreement statistics on a stratified sample (across $k$, across predicted correct/incorrect labels), with a confusion matrix quantifying ASR-induced vs. TTS-induced errors.

2. **Issue** — Table 1's own numbers do not corroborate the paper's central causal narrative linking "capacity gain" to counting horizon.
   **Where** — Table 1 (columns k*, capacity gain ratio).
   **Why it matters** — The abstract and Section 4.3 assert that the reason for collapse is that "the number of distinguishable states... grows with $k$ at a factor 0.57 of the control rate" — i.e., models that "run out of states" faster should collapse sooner. But Llasa-3B has the *lowest* capacity-gain ratio in the table (0.28) yet a *higher* $k^*$ (3) than Llasa-1B, which has a much higher ratio (0.42) but the lowest $k^*$ (1). No correlation statistic (e.g., Spearman's $\rho$) between capacity-gain ratio and $k^*$ is reported across the panel, and the raw values are not monotonic. This directly undercuts the "the decoder... runs out of states in which the count could be represented" framing as the operative mechanism behind the observed $k^*$ values, rather than as a separately-true but only loosely associated phenomenon.
   **What would address it** — Report the actual across-model correlation between capacity-gain ratio (or absolute gain) and $k^*$, with a significance test, and temper causal language if it is not significant.

3. **Issue** — Assumption 2 (contraction, $q<1$), the load-bearing premise of Theorem 1, is never measured in any checkpoint; the one attempt is reported as methodologically unsound.
   **Where** — Section 4.4, "Two things we could not establish."
   **Why it matters** — Under an adversarial reading, the paper's design cannot currently falsify its own headline mechanism: Assumption 2 is stated as "empirical... where it fails, the theorem is silent," but no checkpoint is shown, or even tested, to be in the non-contracting regime, and the direct estimator (differencing boundary states) is abandoned as unsound. The two indirect proxies offered instead (effective-rank saturation, coarse probe decodability) are consistent with contraction but do not uniquely implicate it (see #4). As it stands, no observation described in the paper could have returned evidence against Assumption 2 for these five models — the theorem's applicability to real decoders is asserted from curve-shape agreement, not from a test capable of rejecting it.
   **What would address it** — Even a noisy, explicitly-caveated attempt at bounding $q$ (e.g., via known, engineered repetition boundaries rather than attention-localized ones, or synthetic inputs with ground-truth boundary timing) so that Assumption 2 is at least weakly testable rather than purely inferred.

4. **Issue** — The effective-rank/capacity-gain metric (Section 4.3) does not distinguish "states lack representational capacity for the count" from the much simpler explanation that repeated output is acoustically self-similar, which mechanically lowers spectral entropy regardless of any internal capacity limit.
   **Where** — Section 4.3, Fig. 1(c,d).
   **Why it matters** — If a model produces genuinely repetitive audio (which is definitionally what "counting collapse" looks like at the waveform level), its hidden-state trajectory will tend to revisit similar regions of state space for reasons that have nothing to do with an inability to represent count — it is a direct consequence of generating similar sounds. The paper offers this measurement as "the theorem's own consequence... directly observable" (Abstract), but without a control that holds acoustic content diversity constant while varying whether the underlying computation is "genuinely periodic" vs. "genuinely varied," the metric cannot separate the representational claim from a surface redundancy artifact.
   **What would address it** — Compare $N_{\text{eff}}$ between failed (looping/truncated) generations and teacher-forced or reference-correct renditions matched on acoustic content, to net out the redundancy confound.

5. **Issue** — No manipulation distinguishes the paper's structural/information-theoretic account from a simpler out-of-distribution training-data account, and the one causal intervention performed (XTTS-v2 repetition-penalty ablation) is a decoding-time knob, not a test of $q$ or of training exposure.
   **Where** — Discussion, "Implications and limits" (XTTS-v2 penalty ablation); absent elsewhere.
   **Why it matters** — Real speech/text corpora essentially never contain a word repeated verbatim 6–32 times; an equally parsimonious account of the collapse curve is that this is simply severe distribution shift at the input level, unrelated to any contraction dynamic. The two accounts make different predictions under exposure: a structural bound (Eq. 1) predicts $k^*$ shifts only via $q$ or the readout margin, while an OOD account predicts $k^*$ shifts substantially with fine-tuning exposure to repetitive text. The paper never runs this test, so "not a sampling accident... a structural limit" (Abstract) is stated more strongly than the observational design supports.
   **What would address it** — Fine-tune or adapt at least one checkpoint on repetition-heavy synthetic data and re-measure $k^*$; a horizon that moves far beyond what Eq. 1's $q$/margin levers would predict favors the OOD account over the contraction account.

6. **Issue** — Lemma 1's empirical verification (and Assumption 1 more broadly) covers only cross-attention over the $k$ repeated text spans, not the full autoregressive state update, which in codec-token AR TTS also depends heavily on causal self-attention over the model's own already-generated acoustic tokens.
   **Where** — Assumption 1; Lemma 1; Section 4.3 ("Lemma 1 is checkable in the same recordings, and it concerns the softmax restricted to the k repeated keys").
   **Why it matters** — Self-attention over recent generated output is typically recency-biased, not flat, in exactly the way text cross-attention is shown to flatten. If this channel retains boundary-distinguishing information even while text attention dilutes, $F$ need not be well-approximated as autonomous overall, even though Lemma 1 holds for its text-attention component — breaking the chain from Lemma 1 to Assumption 1 to Theorem 1's premise for the actual decoder.
   **What would address it** — Report entropy/locality statistics for self-attention over the acoustic history at repetition boundaries, alongside the text-attention statistics already given.

7. **Issue** — The duration model used in the conjunctive correctness label is fit on the low-$k$ regime and then applied to judge high-$k$ items (the regime that matters most), and per-cell sample sizes (model × unit-type × $k$ × seed) are never reported, with only 3 seeds per item.
   **Where** — Section 3, "Behavioural measurement"; Section 4.2's "54 matched pairs" and "6 models" sub-analyses.
   **Why it matters** — Extrapolating a duration-consistency check fit at low $k$ to $k$ up to 32 risks systematic bias in precisely the collapse zone being studied, and small per-cell counts (e.g., 54 pairs, or a 6-model sentence-vs-word contrast) are reported as point percentages without confidence intervals or significance tests, making it hard to judge whether contrasts like "1.7 vs. 1.34×" (Section 4.2) are robust or noise.
   **What would address it** — Validate the duration model's high-$k$ extrapolation against held-out human-verified items, and report per-cell Ns with CIs/tests for the smaller comparisons.

### Minor concerns

- Reference [11] (Barbero, Banino, Kapturowski et al.) is malformed — empty title and venue fields — and needs to be fixed before publication.
- Reference [1] is cited as "Withdrawn 13 Feb 2026" for a substantive framing claim ("well documented as a stability problem"); the paper should note why a withdrawn analysis remains an appropriate citation for that claim.
- Several load-bearing citations, including the authors' own companion paper [5] and [9], [13], are same-year (2026), non-peer-reviewed preprints; [9]'s claim of "near-perfect decodability" for text LMs is used as a contrast point and does check out against its abstract, but the broader reliance on contemporaneous self-citation for framing is worth flagging.
- The released repository name (`lab260ru/icassp_antispoofing`) does not match this paper's subject matter (repetition/counting collapse in TTS) — worth double-checking the artifact link before camera-ready.
- The $k^*$ definition ("largest requested repetition count at which counting accuracy still exceeds 0.5") is an arbitrary threshold with no sensitivity analysis; claims like "doubles from 0.6B to 1.7B" may be threshold-dependent.
- Eq. 1's constants ($L$, $C$, $\mu$, $q$) are never jointly estimated to produce a predicted $N^*$ compared against an observed $k^*$ for any checkpoint — the "explicit horizon" remains qualitative in the results.
- The intro's number-phrase example ("six hundred sixty-six thousand...") is vivid but not explicitly tied back to a specific quantitative result in Section 4.

### Verdict

**Major revision** — the formal contraction-mapping result and the periodicity-vs-length control design are genuine strengths, but the causal chain from theorem to observed collapse rests on an unvalidated measurement pipeline, an untested core premise (Assumption 2), and a capacity-gain metric whose own reported numbers (Table 1) don't monotonically track the phenomenon it's meant to explain.

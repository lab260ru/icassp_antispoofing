### Summary

The paper asks whether neural TTS models' well-documented tendency to miscount repeated words is caused by input *length* or by input *periodicity*, and separates the two by constructing 180 parametric stimuli in which a word repeated k times is paired with a length- and word-count-matched control whose repeated token is replaced by distinct fillers. Across six checkpoints spanning three nominal architecture families (Llasa 1B/3B/8B, Qwen3-TTS 0.6B/1.7B, XTTS-v2), plus two non-autoregressive baselines (VITS, F5-TTS), the paper reports exact-count rendering in 18.2% of repeated-text generations against 94.3% for length-matched controls, a gap that survives decoding-parameter sweeps, judge-choice changes (replacing autoregressive Whisper with a CTC recognizer), and exclusion-rule ablations. It further states and Lean-4-formalizes a conditional "counting collapse" theorem (contraction implies a finite counting horizon) but explicitly reports that the theorem's premise (contraction factor q < 1) could not be measured, so the formal result is offered as an untested conjecture rather than an explanation.

### Major concerns

1. **Issue** — The "three architectures" claim overstates the independence of the architecture-level random effect used for every generalization inference in Sec. 3.1.
   **Where** — Sec. 2 "Models"; Sec. 3.1 (mixed-effects gap of 76.4 points, posterior-predictive gap of 65.3 [5.9, 93.3] for "an architecture outside the panel").
   **Why it matters** — Two of the three "families" (Llasa, Qwen3-TTS) are both autoregressive discrete-codec-token language models scaled from the same fundamental design (next-token prediction over quantized audio tokens, Llama-style backbone in Llasa's case); the panel's genuine architectural contrast is really XTTS-v2 versus a codec-LM axis, i.e., closer to n≈2 independent designs than n=3. The paper leans on "n=3" to justify treating architecture as a random effect and to extrapolate a posterior-predictive gap to unseen architectures — but that extrapolation's own reported interval already touches zero under a wide prior, which is the expected symptom of inflating apparent replication from what is mostly within-design scale variation. The abstract's "across six checkpoints from three architectures" reads as broader coverage than what is structurally tested.
   **What would address it** — Report an explicit architecture-genealogy/similarity table (shared backbone, tokenizer, training recipe) and either nest Llasa/Qwen3-TTS under a shared "codec-LM" design factor in the mixed-effects model, or add a fourth architecturally distinct system (e.g., a diffusion-based or non-codec NAR model) before making claims that generalize "across architectures."

2. **Issue** — The finding is validated on English only, yet the mechanism it is used to motivate (a language-independent state-contraction bound) and the headline framing ("the limit tracks periodicity, not length") are stated without linguistic qualification.
   **Where** — Limitations paragraph acknowledges "English the only language tested"; Abstract, Sec. 1, and Sec. 3.1 state the periodicity/length dissociation in unqualified terms.
   **Why it matters** — The paper's own cited prior art on transformer counting explicitly ties counting limits to tokenization [17, Zhang, Cao, You, arXiv:2410.19730]. Because the stimuli are English carrier sentences with repeated *words*, an all-English test cannot separate "periodicity of the acoustic/state signal" from "periodicity as expressed through English BPE/subword segmentation of a repeated wordform." If the effect is partly a tokenization artifact, it would not transfer to languages with different morphology or orthography (e.g., agglutinative or logographic languages), which would undercut both the claimed generality of the empirical result and the motivation for treating it as evidence relevant to a language-agnostic formal bound.
   **What would address it** — Replicate the repetition ladder (or a reduced version) in at least one typologically distinct language using an equivalent CTC-based judge, or explicitly scope every claim in the Abstract/Introduction to "English text" rather than presenting it as a property of "neural text-to-speech" in general.

3. **Issue** — The mechanistic conclusion favoring the "output-policy" account over "state-contraction" (Sec. 5) rests on only 3 checkpoints able to run past the extension horizon, with the predicted pattern holding in just 1 of 3, and imports a rival hypothesis from a different modality without testing whether it transfers.
   **Where** — Sec. 5, "Where the count lives"; citation of [13] Venkatesh, arXiv:2605.09239.
   **Why it matters** — [2605.09239] establishes the representations-vs-outputs dissociation for *text* LLMs counting repeated tokens in a discrete text stream, not for TTS codec-LMs synthesizing audio from repeated text. The present paper treats this as the established "live rival" and reports its own probe data (R² 0.48, retention >1 in 2 of 6) as "consistent" with it, then concludes "the output-policy account is the better supported" — a directional claim built on n=3 checkpoints, of which the authors themselves note only 1/3 matches the bound's distinctive prediction, one plainly contradicts it (Llasa-8B), and one is ambiguous. That is a thin basis for adjudicating between two mechanistic accounts, and the cross-modality import (text-token counting → audio-codec-token counting) is asserted rather than justified.
   **What would address it** — Either qualify the Sec. 5 conclusion as inconclusive (the paper already does this for the n=3 result "at n=3 this is inconclusive," but then still asserts "the output-policy account is the better supported" one sentence later — these two statements are in tension) or expand the extension-horizon-probe analysis to more checkpoints, and explicitly argue why a text-domain dissociation result should be expected to hold for discrete-audio-token generation.

4. **Issue** — The filler-word pool used to build controls is not characterized for lexical difficulty (frequency, length, phonotactics) relative to the repeated target words, leaving open a difficulty confound underneath the central 94.3%-vs-18.2% contrast.
   **Where** — Sec. 2 "Stimuli" ("fillers from a per-template pool of eight, cycled above k = 8 (S10)"); the never-cycled 146-word pool used for the de-confounding check in Sec. 3.1.
   **Why it matters** — The entire dissociation argument pivots on controls sitting near ceiling (94.3% exact, control medians at 0.0% error). If fillers are systematically shorter, higher-frequency, or more distinguishable phonetically than the repeated target word, some of the gap could reflect lexical ease rather than the presence/absence of periodicity per se. The paper controls for word count and carrier but not for per-item lexical difficulty of the varying element.
   **What would address it** — Report frequency, length, and phonetic-distance statistics comparing target words to their filler sets, and ideally re-run a subset with difficulty-matched fillers.

5. **Issue** — The stimulus population (a synthetic repetition ladder embedding a word up to 32 times in a fixed carrier) is narrow relative to the paper's framing as a general "benchmark for repetition miscounting in neural TTS," and no evidence is given that the periodicity/length dissociation observed here predicts behavior on the repetition patterns TTS systems actually encounter in deployment (repeated phrases in dialogue turns, numerals, long-form narration, multi-speaker or noisy prompts).
   **Where** — Sec. 2 "Stimuli"; Title and Sec. 1 framing ("a controlled benchmark for repetition miscounting in neural TTS").
   **Why it matters** — A controlled, parametric ladder is the right design for isolating periodicity from length as *causal factors* in this restricted population, and the paper is honest that this is what it does. But the title and framing suggest a general-purpose benchmark for a real-world failure mode, while the tested population is a single constructed stimulus family in one language with a fixed carrier structure. Nothing in the paper establishes that this construct-population effect size (75.4 points) predicts the rate or severity of repetition failures on naturalistic TTS inputs.
   **What would address it** — Either narrow the title/framing to "a controlled causal-isolation paradigm for periodicity vs. length" (which is what was actually built and is a legitimate, well-executed contribution), or add a validation pass on a sample of naturalistic repetition-containing text (e.g., real numerals, real dialogue with repeated phrases) to show the isolated effect predicts real-world failure rates.

### Minor concerns

- The GitHub repo hosting the code/data is named `icassp_antispoofing`, unrelated to TTS repetition; the paper notes "(name predates this study)" but this is worth a cleaner explanation in a camera-ready version, since it raises an easily-avoidable provenance question for reproducibility auditors.
- The Abstract leads with the tightest interval (75.4 [71.1, 78.9]) without noting, as the body does, that the widest-prior posterior-predictive interval for an unseen architecture touches zero (-1.6, P = 0.956) — this asymmetry between what is foregrounded and what is disclosed inside the paper should be balanced in the Abstract.
- Sec. 5's own language contains a direct tension: "At n = 3 this is inconclusive, and we report it for transparency rather than as evidence either way" immediately followed by "the output-policy account is the better supported" — pick one framing.
- The novelty claim "to our knowledge the first hallucination result in speech synthesis carried out as a formal artifact" is asserted without a lookup analogue in the paper itself; worth either softening ("to our knowledge") is already there, but a brief scoping sentence (formal *hallucination* results vs. formal verification of TTS systems generally) would preempt an obvious reviewer objection.
- Table 1's "cap. ratio" column is introduced without inline definition at first appearance in the table caption beyond "at deep layers"; a one-clause pointer to Sec. 3.3/Fig. 1(c) would help readability.
- Self-citations [7] (arXiv:2604.08591) and [13] (arXiv:2605.09239) are very recent (2026); confirmed [13] exists and is on-topic, but its applicability to the TTS/codec-LM setting (rather than text LLMs) is asserted rather than argued (see Major concern 3).

### Verdict

**Major revision** — the core causal-isolation result (periodicity vs. length) is a genuine, carefully-controlled contribution with commendable robustness checks and honest hedging on the formal bound, but the paper's generalization claims outrun its population: the "three architectures" framing overstates independent architectural coverage, the English-only scope is left unqualified in the headline claims, and the mechanistic adjudication in Sec. 5 draws a confident conclusion from n=3 with a cross-modality import that is not justified.

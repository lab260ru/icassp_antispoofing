### Summary

The paper asks whether a decoder's failure to render a text-repeated phrase the correct number of times is a consequence of a simple, formalizable dynamical mechanism: if softmax attention over k repeated spans dilutes to weight Θ(1/k) (Lemma 1) and the resulting per-repetition state update contracts (Assumption 2), then no Lipschitz readout of the decoder's state — including its own stop head — can distinguish repetition counts past a horizon N* (Theorem 1), both proved and machine-checked in Lean 4. The authors then test the empirical footprint of this claim across six TTS checkpoints (three architectures: Llasa, Qwen3-TTS, XTTS-v2) plus two non-autoregressive baselines (VITS, F5-TTS), scoring generations with a CTC recognizer chosen because the more obvious Whisper judge is itself autoregressive and biased. The headline empirical result is that repeated-phrase renderings undercount by a median of -12.5% per checkpoint at k≥6 while length-matched non-repetitive controls are essentially exact (94.3% vs 18.2% exact-match), establishing that the deficit tracks periodicity rather than length. However, the count error is proportional (not saturating) up to k=32 — the opposite of what Theorem 1 predicts — and only a post-hoc extension to k=128 on 4 of 6 checkpoints finds saturation-like behavior, with the theorem's actual premise (a measured contraction factor q) never established and several planned confirmatory tests (a dilution dose-response check, a linear probe past the horizon) failing or succeeding in only a minority of checkpoints.

### Major concerns

1. **Issue** — The paper's own confirmatory tests of Theorem 1 mostly fail or are ambiguous, yet the framing throughout treats a curve-fit "saturation scale" from the post-hoc k=128 extension as if it corroborates the theorem's specific, q-derived horizon N*.
   **Where** — Abstract; §4.2 ("Fitting ĉ(k) = K̂(1 − e^{-k/K̂})... K̂ a saturation scale read off counts, not the theorem's N∗, a function of the unmeasured q"); §5 ("The theorem's distinctive prediction is a constant N∗, invisible where our ladder stopped; k = 128 found saturation of that kind, though not from a measured q").
   **Why it matters** — The theorem's entire predictive content is a *specific* horizon determined by q, L, C, and µ. A model-free exponential fit to observed counts that merely *looks* saturating is consistent with many mechanisms unrelated to state contraction (e.g., a hard length ceiling, exposure-bias accumulation, or attention entropy collapse with no dynamical-systems content at all — indeed the paper's own control curves saturate too, §4.2, "these decoders have a general length ceiling"). Calling this "saturation of that kind" invites readers to credit the theorem with an empirical confirmation it has not received. Compounding this, the two experiments explicitly designed to test the theorem's distinguishing claim (dilution dose-response in §4.4, and the post-horizon linear probe in §5) go against the predicted direction in the *majority* of cases: the dose-response correlation is sign-reversed (r=0.59, flatter attention → *better* counting, the opposite of Lemma 1's implication), and the probe recovers count past N* — contradicting the theorem — in 2 of the 3 checkpoints tested, succeeding (i.e., failing to recover) in only 1 of 3. A theorem whose premise is unmeasured and whose two most direct empirical fingerprints are absent or reversed in a majority of tested cases cannot be described as the operative explanation of the collapse, but the paper's rhetorical structure (title notwithstanding — "a horizon it does not yet explain" is honest) keeps nudging toward that reading in the body text.
   **What would address it** — Either measure q directly for at least a subset of checkpoints (the paper reports two failed attempts, §5, but doesn't propose a third), or reframe the k=128 result plainly as "consistent with *a* saturating process, not diagnostic of *this* one," and downweight language in §5 that pairs "saturation of that kind" with the theorem's name.

2. **Issue** — Generality claims ("the limit tracks periodicity, not length, in every checkpoint and family," Abstract) are not supported uniformly by the n=6 panel, and the paper's own reported nulls/reversals in Qwen3-TTS-1.7B are folded into effect-size averages rather than surfaced in the headline claim.
   **Where** — Abstract; Table 1; §4.2 ("Qwen3-TTS-1.7B shows no repeated-side saturation within k≤128 and inverts the ratio"; "median-error gap is positive in 5 of 6, zero in Qwen3-TTS-1.7B").
   **Why it matters** — "In every checkpoint and family" is a strong universal claim built on 6 data points, one of which (by the paper's own account) shows a zero median-error gap and an inverted saturation ratio. The paper appropriately notes it lacks power for significance testing (n=6, signed-rank floor 0.031, no correction clears 0.05, §4.2) and pivots to effect sizes — a defensible move — but the abstract's "in every checkpoint" wording overstates what "effect sizes, held loosely, mostly point one way" can support.
   **What would address it** — Qualify the abstract claim (e.g., "in 5 of 6 checkpoints, and in all 3 families by pooled comparison") to match the qualified language already used correctly in §4.2.

3. **Issue** — The k=128 extension is explicitly post hoc and exploratory, run on only 4 of 6 checkpoints, and the saturation effect itself is absent in one of those four — yet it is the analysis that supplies the paper's only quantitative "horizon" evidence.
   **Where** — §4.2 ("So we extended it, post hoc... on the 4 checkpoints admitting utterances that long... though none moves a checkpoint across 1 (S17)").
   **Why it matters** — Deciding to extend the ladder *because* the pre-specified k≤32 range showed no horizon (§4.1: "not what Theorem 1(iv) predicts... no horizon lies inside this ladder") is a textbook garden-of-forking-paths move: the analysis that "found" the predicted phenomenon was chosen after the phenomenon failed to appear where it was supposed to. The paper is commendably transparent about this (dating decisions against the commit log, S16), which mitigates but does not eliminate the concern, since transparency about post-hoc-ness doesn't restore the statistical guarantees a pre-registered test would have had.
   **What would address it** — A held-out replication of the k=48–128 ladder on a fresh stimulus set (not just re-running the same items), ideally pre-registered, before treating the saturation-scale gap (2.7–4.3-fold) as a confirmed effect rather than a hypothesis-generating one.

4. **Issue** — The CTC judge is validated on artificially concatenated ("concatenative") ground-truth audio, not on genuinely degenerate model output (loops, blended repeats), leaving the accuracy of the core measurement instrument unverified on the actual failure mode under study.
   **Where** — §3, "Behavioural measurement" ("Audited against concatenative audio with known ground truth its counted/true ratio falls to 0.27–0.65... We score instead with a CTC recogniser... put through the same audit: ratio 1.00 on periodic ground truth against 0.19 for Whisper").
   **Why it matters** — Concatenative ground-truth audio (spliced clean repeats) has clean phrase boundaries by construction; real degenerate TTS output — loops with prosodic drift, partial repeats, or acoustic blending across repetition boundaries — is exactly the regime where a CTC recognizer's segmentation-free decoding could plausibly under- or over-count relative to a human listener, and this is untested. Since the -12.5% headline number and the exact-match rates are both direct functions of the judge's counting behavior on real (not synthetic) model output, an unvalidated instrument on the target distribution is a first-order threat to the headline claim, not a peripheral one.
   **What would address it** — Report agreement between the CTC judge and the "165-clip stratified sample... shipped with each clip's transcript and scoring decision" (§3) as a human-vs-judge concordance statistic on real (non-concatenative) model outputs, not only on synthetic ground truth.

5. **Issue** — The paper's own late-stage evidence (R²=0.48 count-decodability late in generation; 2 of 3 checkpoints where a linear probe recovers count past the claimed horizon) is more consistent with the "output-policy-only failure" rival hypothesis than with its own contraction account, but the framing treats this as an open question rather than a result that currently favors the rival.
   **Where** — §5 ("A linear probe does not separate this from the rival on which the count survives and only the output policy fails [13]... The other two keep it (0.99 and 0.86 of the constant predictor's error), so the prediction holds in 1 of 3").
   **Why it matters** — Reference [13] (Venkatesh, *Repeated-Token Counting Reveals a Dissociation Between Representations and Outputs*, [2605.09239]) reports for LLMs that linear probes on the residual stream decode repeated-token counts with near-perfect accuracy at every layer, including the exact layers where the output crystallizes incorrectly — i.e., representational information survives while the output policy fails. This paper's own probe result (majority of tested checkpoints keep the count decodable past the theorem's predicted horizon, plus general late-generation decodability at R²=0.48) sits closer to that account than to a genuine information-erasure/contraction account, yet the paper stops short of stating that its strongest currently available evidence favors the rival mechanism over its own theorem's premise.
   **What would address it** — State explicitly, as a limitation, that the balance of the probe evidence (2/3 checkpoints, R²=0.48) currently favors the output-policy account over the state-contraction account, and describe what a decisive discriminating experiment between the two would look like.

6. **Issue** — Several confound-ruling-out analyses (repetition-penalty sweep, cycled-vs-non-cycled filler pool, greedy-vs-sampled, duration-conditioning test on F5-TTS) run on small, non-overlapping subsamples (n=30, n=60, n=90, n=240) without multiple-comparison correction, but are reported with point-estimate precision ("worth 13.4 points") that implies more resolution than the samples support.
   **Where** — §4.3 (filler-pool re-generation, n=240; penalty sweep, S15; greedy vs. sampled, S22; F5-TTS duration test, n=30 and n=60).
   **Why it matters** — Each of these is a legitimate alternative-explanation check, and individually reasonable, but collectively they constitute a garden of post-hoc comparisons run on the same panel; none carries a correction, and several (n=30, n=60) are underpowered to support the specific point differences quoted (e.g., "43.3% to 76.7%, n=30" — a 33-point swing on 30 items has a wide binomial CI that isn't reported).
   **What would address it** — Report confidence intervals (not just point differences) for each confound-check comparison, and note where n is too small to distinguish the claimed effect from sampling noise.

7. **Issue — reproducibility.** The operationalization of ĉ (repetitions "delivered") from a raw CTC transcript is not described in the reviewed text.
   **Where** — §3, "Behavioural measurement" (absent — the text describes the judge's architecture and its audit ratios but not the string-matching/counting procedure that turns a transcript into ĉ).
   **Why it matters** — Whether ĉ is computed by exact substring counts, fuzzy/edit-distance matching, or forced alignment materially affects the count-error numbers that anchor every subsequent result; without this description in the main text, an independent reader cannot judge how counting errors (off-by-one boundary effects, partial-word transcription) might bias ĉ systematically low or high.
   **What would address it** — A short explicit description (even one sentence) of the ĉ-extraction procedure in §3, with the fuller algorithm in the supplement.

### Minor concerns

- The abstract's two Whisper accuracy figures (0.27–0.65 "counted/true ratio" for k≥4 model output vs. 1.00 for distinct-word sequences, then later "ratio 1.00 on periodic ground truth against 0.19 for Whisper" for the CTC audit) refer to two different tests (model output vs. concatenative ground truth) but are easy to conflate on a first read; a clarifying phrase would help.
- Lemma 1's inequality "e^{−δ}/k ≤ softmax(z)_i ≤ e^{δ}/k" is potentially ambiguous as typeset/extracted (exponent scope unclear); worth double-checking in the camera-ready.
- "worth 13.4 points" and similar single-decimal effect-size phrasings throughout §4 read as more precise than the underlying n justifies (see Major #6); consider rounding or pairing with intervals.
- The claim "to our knowledge the first hallucination result in speech synthesis carried out as a formal artifact" (Introduction) is appropriately hedged with "to our knowledge," but given how specific formal-verification-of-hallucination novelty claims can be litigated, an explicit scoping sentence (formal ≠ empirical prior art, e.g. distinguishing from the cited companion ASR spectral study [7]) would preempt confusion.
- Table 1's "capacity gain" column and Figure 1(c) both report the same effective-rank-slope quantity; consider whether both are needed or whether one could be moved to supplement.
- The exclusion accounting in §3 (2052 → 1559) and in §4.2 (240 → 21–26/cell) is arithmetically consistent but the per-cell denominator structure (what exactly constitutes a "cell") is not spelled out in-text, requiring the reader to infer it.

### Verdict

**Major revision** — the empirical periodicity-vs-length dissociation is a genuine and carefully instrumented finding, but the paper's central theoretical apparatus (contraction-driven counting collapse) has an unmeasured premise and its two most direct confirmatory tests fail or succeed in only a minority of checkpoints, so the connection between the Lean-verified theorem and the reported horizon needs to be reframed rather than merely gestured at as unresolved.

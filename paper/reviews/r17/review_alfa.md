### Summary

The paper makes three claims: (1) a Lean-4-verified theorem that *if* an autoregressive decoder's per-repetition state update contracts, *then* no Lipschitz readout — including the model's own stop head — can recover the repetition count past a computable horizon N*; (2) an empirical finding that six AR TTS checkpoints across three architectures undercount long literal repetitions, and that this failure tracks periodicity rather than sequence length (the cleanest experiment in the paper: 94.3% exact renderings for length-matched non-repeating controls vs 18.2% for their repeated twins); and (3) a much shakier claim that the theorem's predicted saturating horizon actually shows up if you push the repetition count far enough — visible only in a post-hoc extension to k=128, confirmed by a direct probe test in 1 of the 3 checkpoints on which that test could even be run. The authors are unusually honest about the gaps: they say outright that Assumption 2 (contraction) was never established, that two attempts to measure it failed, and that the dilution lemma's own dose-response prediction came out sign-reversed. That honesty is real and should be credited. But it also means the theorem is decorative for most of the paper: its headline formula (Eq. 1, N*) is never once instantiated with a number for any checkpoint, and the one place the theorem makes a sharp, falsifiable prediction (probe recovers count below horizon, fails above it), the paper's own data disconfirms it in 2 of 3 checkpoints tested — yet the prose gives the confirming checkpoint a full, clean, cross-checked walkthrough and buries the other two in an ambiguous parenthetical. The empirical periodicity-vs-length result stands on its own and doesn't need the Lean machinery to be interesting; bolting an unverified contraction theorem onto it, and then narrating the evidence asymmetrically toward the cases that support it, is the paper's central weakness.

### Major concerns

1. **Issue** — The theorem's own headline quantity, N* (Eq. 1), is never computed for any checkpoint anywhere in the paper. `[Narrative — overclaiming]`
   **Where** — Abstract ("we prove that... no Lipschitz readout... can separate repetition counts beyond a horizon N*"); §4.2, where the fitted saturation scale K̂ is explicitly said to be "not the theorem's N*, a function of the unmeasured q."
   **Why it matters** — A reader who stops at the abstract, or even skims §4, comes away believing the paper demonstrated a *predicted, computed* horizon operating in real decoders. It didn't: q was never measured (two attempts failed), so N* was never evaluated for a single checkpoint, and the number that is reported (K̂, a free curve-fit parameter over a heuristic saturating function) is explicitly disowned as a stand-in. The entire empirical section is therefore consistent with the theorem but does not test its distinctive numeric content — only its qualitative shape (a curve levels off eventually), which a dozen other mechanisms (length ceiling, exposure bias, learned repetition-dispreference) would also produce.
   **What would address it** — Either measure q by a method that works on these architectures and report N* alongside K̂, or drop the pretense that Eq. 1 is doing empirical work and reframe the theorem as a purely conditional, untested formal result (which the discussion section already half-admits it is).

2. **Issue** — The one experiment that directly tests the paper's proposed causal mechanism (attention dilution → contraction → collapse) returns the opposite sign from what the theory predicts, and the paper doesn't revise its framing in response. `[Evidence — alternative explanations]`
   **Where** — §4.4: "Lemma 1's premise holds only approximately... and its dose-response prediction fails outright: within a (model, k) cell, flatter attention goes with better counting (r=0.59 [0.35, 0.75])."
   **Why it matters** — This is the paper's own attempt at a distinguishing test between "dilution drives collapse" and "dilution is a correlate, not a cause," and the data picks the second option, decisively for this sub-experiment (CI excludes zero, wrong direction). Yet the mechanistic story in the introduction and theorem statement is never walked back — it's presented in the abstract and Theorem 1 with full confidence, and the sign-reversal is relegated to one clause with the shrug "whatever selects which generations fail is not the degree of dilution." If the mechanism proposed to *cause* the collapse doesn't have the predicted dose-response, that's evidence against the specific causal chain (dilution → contraction → collapse) the paper is built around, not a minor footnote.
   **What would address it** — Either find a dilution measure whose dose-response matches prediction, or explicitly state in the abstract/intro that the mechanistic story (Lemma 1 → Assumption 2 → Theorem 1) is falsified in its one direct test and that the paper's real contribution is the phenomenological periodicity effect, independent of the proposed cause.

3. **Issue** — A non-autoregressive, non-recurrent model (F5-TTS, flow-matching) reproduces the target signature, which the theorem — stated entirely in terms of an autoregressive per-step state map — has no mechanism to explain, and the paper doesn't resolve the tension. `[Evidence — distinguishing test]`
   **Where** — §4.3: "F5-TTS... does show it: 25.6% against 85.6%, +60.0, growing with k as the panel's does... Absence of an autoregressive decoder confers no immunity, and we withdraw the architectural claim."
   **Why it matters** — The theorem's premises (Assumption 1: single map F between repetition boundaries; Assumption 2: F contracts) are stated for a decoder that updates a hidden state step by step. F5-TTS does not do this — it estimates one total duration and denoises in parallel. If a system with no analogous per-repetition-boundary state map at all shows the same undercounting-with-periodicity signature, the most parsimonious reading is that the true cause lives upstream of autoregressive decoding (e.g., in how repeated spans are encoded by the text/duration conditioning, shared by both architectures), which would make the entire "autoregressive decoder" framing of the paper's title and theorem too narrow — or simply mistargeted. The authors' own honesty here ("we withdraw the architectural claim") is commendable, but the paper still ships with a title and theorem that are exclusively about AR decoding, without revising the framing to match what F5-TTS actually shows.
   **What would address it** — Either retitle/reframe around "periodicity collapse in TTS decoders" generically (dropping "autoregressive" from the theoretical claim, since it demonstrably isn't required), or restrict the theorem's claimed scope explicitly to the subset of results where AR-specific mechanisms were shown necessary (which, per the current data, is none).

4. **Issue** — Cross-checkpoint claims rest on an effective n of 3 independent architecture families, one of which (Qwen3-TTS) inverts on the extended ladder, and no claim is significance-tested. `[Statistical rigour]`
   **Where** — §4.2 ("Qwen3-TTS-1.7B shows no repeated-side saturation within k≤128 and inverts the ratio"); §4.2 ("the panel is too small for significance testing... so we rest on effect sizes and their consistency").
   **Why it matters** — Six checkpoints collapse to three architecture families for any claim about generality across designs. One of those three families gives a null-to-inverted result on the paper's own extended-ladder saturation test — the single place the theorem's distinctive prediction is meant to show up. The paper explains this away as "the weakest checkpoint on all 3 panel measures," which may be true, but is also exactly the kind of post-hoc rationalization that should raise a reviewer's eyebrows on a panel this small: with n=3 families, one disagreement is not "the exception that proves the rule," it's a third of the evidence pointing the other way. The authors are appropriately honest that they can't run inferential statistics here — but that honesty should translate into weaker claims in the abstract, not into "the limit tracks periodicity, not length, in every checkpoint and family (n=6, too small to test)" being stated as if it settles the matter for the harder, extended-ladder question.
   **What would address it** — Either add more checkpoints/families before claiming generality of the saturation effect specifically (as opposed to the k≤32 undercounting effect, which is better replicated), or explicitly caveat the k=128 saturation claim in the abstract as "2 of 3 testable families" rather than folding it into an omnibus statement.

5. **Issue** — The paper's two most quotable results are narrated with an asymmetry that consistently gives the confirming case full, clean, cross-checked detail while disconfirming or ambiguous cases are compressed into unnamed, hard-to-audit fragments — the same failure mode as cherry-picked qualitative examples, applied to which quantitative result gets the paragraph. `[Evidence — cherry-picking]`
   **Where** — (a) Discussion, the probe-past-horizon test: "On Llasa-1B it reaches MAE 0.50... against 0.50 for a predictor that ignores the states... Range is not why: over the same k it still reads the count off control states (0.43). The other two keep it (0.99 and 0.86 of the constant predictor's error), so the prediction holds in 1 of 3 — Llasa-1B and Llasa-3B both within 2%..." (b) Table 1 vs. §4.3: the paper's single most-quoted contrast — 18.2% vs 94.3% exact-match rate — is never broken out per checkpoint anywhere in the paper, even though Table 1 breaks out every other headline metric (count error, capacity gain) by all six checkpoints.
   **Why it matters** — For (a): Llasa-1B gets a full three-number story (below-horizon comparison, above-horizon comparison, a control check ruling out a range confound) that reads as a small, self-contained confirmatory case study. The other two checkpoints — which is genuinely the majority of the n=3 sample this test could even be run on — get two bare numbers with no names, no below/above-horizon breakdown, and no control check, and the passage is dense enough that even a careful read leaves it ambiguous whether Llasa-3B (named in the next clause) is one of "the other two" that "keep it" (disconfirm) or one of the two "within 2%" (confirm) — these two readings of the same sentence are not compatible, and the checkpoint identity of the 0.86-ratio outlier (a 14% edge over the constant predictor, i.e., a readout that plausibly *does* separate counts past N*, contradicting the theorem) is never given. For (b): given that the exact-match dissociation is described in the paper's own words as "the paper's sharpest test," omitting its per-checkpoint breakdown — while providing it for every other metric — makes it impossible to check whether that 94.3% control figure is uniform across checkpoints or, as with every other headline number in this paper, driven asymmetrically by the weaker/stronger checkpoints (Qwen3-TTS-1.7B in particular, which is the outlier on essentially every other measure reported).
   **What would address it** — Name all three checkpoints in the probe-test passage and report the same three numbers (below-horizon MAE, above-horizon MAE, control MAE) for each, not just the confirming one. Add a per-checkpoint exact-match-rate column to Table 1 so the 18.2%/94.3% headline can be audited the same way every other number in the paper already is.

### Minor concerns

- Abstract density: the single-paragraph abstract packs upward of fifteen distinct statistics and qualifiers into ~200 words (e.g., "-12.5% per checkpoint at k≥6," "18.2%... against 94.3%," "2.7–4.3-fold," "1 of the 3 checkpoints"). It's admirably precise but a genuinely difficult first read; consider moving some of the hedging to the body and letting the abstract state the two or three load-bearing numbers.
- The pooled item-level estimate (-8.3% [-12.5, -6.2], n=457) and the checkpoint-median estimate (-12.5%) disagree in magnitude, and the paper correctly notes the pooled interval "ignores clustering" — good practice, but then both numbers are used somewhat interchangeably elsewhere in the text; pick one primary number and stick to it.
- Reproducibility: code lives on `research/ar-tts-counting-collapse`, a mutable branch rather than a tagged release or archived DOI — fine for now but a real risk for a paper whose entire reproducibility pitch is "build.sh regenerates every number here from pinned revisions." Consider tagging a release at camera-ready time.
- The 165-clip human-audit sample is described as available for the reader to check "by ear," but the paper never reports what fraction of it the authors themselves checked or what the agreement with the CTC judge's scoring decisions was. If this check was actually run, report the number; if it wasn't, say so rather than implying availability substitutes for having done it.
- The CTC judge's audit uses "concatenative audio with known ground truth," which is acoustically cleaner/more regular than a model's own genuinely degraded, monotonous repeated output (acknowledged elsewhere: "It is not merely the monotonous audio a correct rendering produces (S8)"). The judge's 1.00 audit ratio may not transfer to the noisier real generations it's actually scoring — worth a sentence of caution.
- S20's "three [alternatives] we cannot exclude, chiefly a learned dispreference for verbatim repetition" is exactly the standard exposure-bias/repetition-penalty explanation the introduction claims to move past — it's flagged but never tested in the main text, and it's the most natural rival to the whole contraction story.
- Table 1's "capacity gain" numbers (e.g., Llasa-1B: 18.2 rep / 56.7 ctl) sit uncomfortably close in value to the abstract's unrelated "18.2%" exact-match figure; worth double-checking no metrics got transposed during table assembly, and worth giving the capacity-gain column explicit units/definition in the caption rather than relying on the body text.
- R² = 0.48 for "the count stays decodable late" (Discussion) is characterized as "decodable" when it explains under half the variance — a fair reading but a generous one; a skeptical reader could just as easily call that "partially decodable at best."
- Baselines/ablations check: the repetition-penalty sweep and greedy-vs-sampled check are reasonable and adequately powered for what they test — this sub-check produced no further concern beyond what's flagged above.

### Belief update

Moderate, narrow update: I now put more weight on "AR (and apparently some non-AR) TTS repetition failures are specifically triggered by literal periodicity in the conditioning text, dissociable from sequence length, decoding parameters, and duration allocation" — the length-matched control in §4.3 is a genuinely well-designed distinguishing test and the cleanest thing in the paper. No update, or if anything a slight downward update, on "this is caused by contraction of a per-repetition decoder-state map" as formalized in Theorem 1 — the paper's own dose-response test for the proposed mechanism came back sign-reversed, both direct attempts to measure the theorem's premise failed, and the theorem's specific numeric prediction (N*) is never computed for any checkpoint. The Lean formalization is a real, if narrow, engineering contribution to the "formal artifacts for ML failure modes" space, but it doesn't move my credence on anything about actual TTS decoders, since its premise was never shown to hold for one.

### Verdict

**Major revision** — the periodicity-vs-length result is solid and worth publishing, but the paper oversells the connection between that result and its Lean-verified theorem (whose headline formula N* is never instantiated), and the selective, asymmetric detail given to the one confirming checkpoint in the probe-past-horizon test versus the two others (§Discussion; concern 5) is precisely the kind of favorable-example emphasis that should be fixed before this goes out as a claim that the theorem's "conclusion survives one direct test."

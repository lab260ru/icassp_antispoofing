### Summary

The paper's steelmanned version is genuinely strong: it proves a Banach-fixed-point-style theorem (contraction ⇒ finite readout-separable horizon) about repetition counting in AR-TTS decoders, machine-checks it in Lean 4 with a clean axiom audit, and — unusually for this literature — is honest that the empirical premise (does the decoder's per-repetition map actually contract?) is *not* established: two direct attempts to measure the contraction factor both come back null or unmeasurable. In its place, the paper offers indirect behavioural evidence: a periodicity-vs-length contrast (Fig. 1b) that is genuinely well controlled and shows a real, large effect (94.3% vs 18.2% exact-match), plus a state-space effective-rank analysis (Fig. 1d) that moves the same direction. The paper's most theorem-specific prediction — a finite, saturating horizon N* — fails inside the pre-registered k ≤ 32 ladder (the deficit stays proportional, not saturating) and is only found after the authors extend the design, post-hoc and by their own account, out to k = 128 on 2 of 6 checkpoints. Pressed on that steelmanned version: the paper's single decisive number (N*rep = 23 vs N*ctl = 94, "4.1-fold") is the least reproducible and least pre-specified result in the paper, and the abstract states it without the hedges the body immediately attaches to it. Net: solid descriptive finding (periodicity ≠ length), unconfirmed mechanistic story (contraction), and a headline quantitative claim that a reader cannot currently check.

### Major concerns

1. **Issue** — The theorem's distinguishing qualitative signature (a saturating, finite horizon) is contradicted within the paper's own pre-registered k ≤ 32 range and is recovered only by an admittedly post-hoc extension of the design. `[Evidence — distinguishing test]`
   **Where** — §4.1 ("Within k ≤ 32, though, the deficit is proportional, not saturating — not what Theorem 1(iv) predicts") vs §4.2.
   **Why it matters** — A conditional theorem is only informative if its distinctive prediction (saturation, not proportionality) is what the data show under the conditions the authors themselves chose to test first. Here the first, larger, better-powered dataset (6 checkpoints, k up to 32) falsifies exactly that signature, and the confirming result comes from a second, much smaller dataset (2 checkpoints) added afterward. That ordering is disclosed candidly, which is commendable, but it means the paper's rescue of its own theorem is evidentially the weakest part of the paper, not the strongest — yet the abstract and title lean on it.
   **What would address it** — Report the extension result as what it is (a separate, exploratory follow-up with its own pre-registration going forward), and run it on all 6 checkpoints with a stated sample size per cell before treating N*=23 vs 94 as a headline number.

2. **Issue** — The k ∈ {48, 64, 96, 128} extension was chosen *because* the original ladder failed to show saturation, i.e., it is a data-driven range extension, not a pre-specified design. `[Evidence — pre-registered vs post-hoc]`
   **Where** — §4.2, first sentence: "So we extended it to k ∈ {48, 64, 96, 128}... (S10)."
   **Why it matters** — This is the textbook shape of post-hoc data collection: test a hypothesis, see it fail, extend the design until it succeeds, then report the extended design's result as confirmation. The paper is unusually transparent about the sequence, but transparency about a post-hoc design doesn't neutralize the statistical logic — with only 2 checkpoints and "few items per cell," a fit that eventually separates two exponential-saturation curves is not strong evidence for a specific N*, especially when (per the paper's own admission) "a pure horizon predicts a plateau whereas the repeated median declines (27→15)" — the observed curve doesn't even match the model being fit to it.
   **What would address it** — Treat N*=23/94 as a pilot estimate motivating a pre-registered replication (fixed k-grid, fixed checkpoint set, fixed n-per-cell, specified before data collection) rather than as a confirmed instance of the theorem.

3. **Issue** — The abstract reports "N*rep = 23 repetitions against 94 for length-matched controls — repetition lowers the counting horizon 4.1-fold" with none of the qualifications the body attaches to the same number two pages later. `[Narrative — overclaiming]`
   **Where** — Abstract, final sentence, vs. §4.2: "Two things keep this short of clean confirmation... We read this as locating these decoders just below the collapse regime... rather than as confirming the bound."
   **Why it matters** — A reader who reads only the abstract (the majority of a conference paper's audience) walks away believing 4.1-fold is an established, confirmed measurement. The body says the opposite: it is an upper bound, from 2 checkpoints, with a non-monotonic curve shape that only loosely matches the fitted model, and the judge's own bias "points the same way (S9), making 4.1 an upper bound." That's a real, substantive difference between what the abstract claims and what the body supports.
   **What would address it** — Hedge the abstract sentence to match the body's own epistemic status ("consistent with," "upper bound," "exploratory") rather than stating the ratio as a flat finding.

4. **Issue** — The paper's own two direct tests of Assumption 2 (does F actually contract?) both fail to support it, yet the rest of the results section is framed as evidence *for* the contraction account rather than being weighed against the simpler alternative that Lemma 1 (attention dilution, which *is* proven and *is* measured) suffices on its own. `[Evidence — alternative explanation]`
   **Where** — §5: "two attempts to measure q failed... found no difference in local contraction between repeated items and their controls (7 of 18 pairs in the predicted direction, p = 0.12), with its own linearity control failing."
   **Why it matters** — Attention dilution alone (Lemma 1, which the paper does verify empirically — entropy grows with log k, most-attended occurrence gets ~1.7/k to 3.4/k of mass) already predicts that a decoder loses positional information about "which repetition am I on" as k grows, which could produce exactly the observed proportional undercount and reduced effective rank *without* requiring a contracting fixed point in the Banach sense. The paper never runs a model or analysis that would separate "dilution-only" from "dilution-plus-contraction" — the contraction-specific tests it does run come back null. The formal theorem's contribution over the (proven) lemma is therefore currently unsupported by the paper's own data.
   **What would address it** — Either find a successful q-measurement (the paper is honest that its two attempts failed — a third, better-designed probe would help) or explicitly present the results as consistent with attention-dilution-alone and downgrade the contraction/attractor framing accordingly.

5. **Issue** — The theorem itself is a direct specialization of mathlib's Banach fixed-point machinery (stated as such in the paper: "part (i) specialises its Banach fixed-point machinery"); the novelty is the *application* to AR-TTS repetition, not the mathematics, but the framing ("we prove," "the theorem," heavy emphasis on formal verification) risks a reader crediting the formal-verification badge with more epistemic weight than it carries given that the theorem's premise is unverified. `[Novelty]`
   **Where** — §2, "Formal verification" paragraph; abstract's lead sentence structure.
   **Why it matters** — Formal verification in Lean certifies that the *implication* is correctly derived from its premises — it says nothing about whether the premises hold of real transformers. The paper is explicit about this ("Formalisation also forced the separation between what is proved... and what is measured"), which is good practice, but the rhetorical weight given to "machine-checked," "no sorry," "no nonstandard axioms" throughout the abstract and intro is disproportionate to what a reader should actually update on, which is closer to "IF a decoder's per-repetition map contracts THEN a horizon exists" — a fact about Banach spaces the field already knew, now applied to a new domain.
   **What would address it** — Keep the formal-verification framing but calibrate its prominence in the abstract/intro to match its actual epistemic contribution (rigor of derivation, not confirmation of mechanism).

6. **Issue** — The single most load-bearing experiment in the paper (§4.2's extension ladder, producing N*=23 vs 94) is documented at a level that a competent reader cannot reproduce or audit from the paper: it does not name which 2 of the 6 checkpoints were extended, does not give per-cell sample sizes beyond "few items per cell," does not specify the fitting procedure/weights for the exponential-saturation model, and the underlying audio (2.5 GB) and activations (25 GB) needed to check the effective-rank and CTC-judge numbers directly are "available on request" rather than hosted for direct download; meanwhile stated seed counts are inconsistent across model families (only Qwen3-TTS is explicitly given "three seeds each" — Llasa and XTTS-v2's seed counts, if any, are never stated), so it's not even clear whether Table 1's per-checkpoint medians reflect single generations or averages. `[Reproducibility gap]`
   **Where** — §3 "Code, data, settings"; §4.2 ("the extension covers 2 checkpoints with few items per cell").
   **Why it matters** — This is exactly the experiment the abstract's headline number comes from, and it is simultaneously (a) statistically thinnest, (b) least specified, and (c) hardest to independently check, since the raw materials are gated behind an ad hoc request process rather than a permanent archival release (Zenodo/HF/institutional mirror), and stochastic generation is only partially seed-documented. A paper whose central quantitative claim cannot be reproduced from the text plus a request email is not meeting the bar the rest of the paper's rigor (Lean proof, released code/build script, released transcripts) otherwise sets for itself.
   **What would address it** — Name the 2 checkpoints and n-per-cell in the main text (not just "(S10)"); state the fitting method; archive audio/activations on a permanent host (even a subsampled, size-capped release) rather than "on request"; report seed counts uniformly across all three model families.

### Belief update

I updated meaningfully on one narrow, well-controlled claim: **periodicity, not raw sequence length, is what degrades AR-TTS repetition counting.** Figure 1(b)'s length-matched-control design is the paper's best experiment — same carrier, same word count, only the periodicity changes, and the contrast (94.3% vs 18.2% exact match) is large and consistent across k. That's a real, useful, falsifiable finding independent of the theorem.

I did **not** update much on the paper's title claim — that this is caused by a *contracting fixed-point attractor* in the Banach sense. The paper's own two direct attempts to measure contraction failed (one unmeasurable, one null at p = 0.12), and the paper does not test whether attention dilution alone (which it does verify) already explains the data without positing contraction. The Lean proof certifies a conditional that was arguably true before the paper (Banach fixed points imply bounded readout separation) — the hard empirical claim, that AR-TTS decoders actually live in the contracting regime this argument requires, remains open by the paper's own admission.

### Verdict

**Major revision** — the paper's honesty about its own theorem's premise being unestablished is a real strength, but the headline number the abstract leads with (N*=23 vs 94, "4.1-fold") comes from the least reproducible experiment in the paper (2 unnamed checkpoints, unspecified per-cell n, data gated behind "available on request," inconsistent seed reporting), and a reader currently cannot check it.

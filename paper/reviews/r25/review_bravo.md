LENS: Experimental design (controls, confounds, blinding, pre-registration)
STANCE: Adversarial (assume the headline result is false; what would falsify it?)

### Summary
The paper asks whether autoregressive TTS models' well-documented failure to correctly render repeated phrases ("say a word six times") is driven by input *length* or by input *periodicity*, and argues for the latter using a 180-item parametric repetition ladder (target word repeated k times, 1–32) paired with length-matched non-repeating controls, tested across six checkpoints from three TTS architecture families (Llasa, Qwen3-TTS, XTTS-v2) plus two non-autoregressive baselines (VITS, F5-TTS). The headline result is a large gap in exact-count accuracy between repeated and control items at k≥6 (18.2% vs 94.3%), a graded period-ladder showing that varying periodicity alone (holding word count and carrier fixed) reproduces the gradient, and a mechanistic account — a Lean-4-verified bound predicting counting failure if per-repetition hidden-state dynamics contract — that the authors measure directly and reject (the dynamics are expansive, not contractive, in every checkpoint they tested).

### Major concerns

1. **Issue** — The k≥6 threshold that anchors the paper's central effect-size numbers (18.2% vs 94.3%, Table 1, Fig. 1) was selected after inspecting where the two curves diverge, i.e., post hoc on the same data used to report the effect.
   **Where** — Section 3.1, final paragraph ("The k ≥ 6 threshold was chosen after seeing where the arms diverge...").
   **Why it matters** — This is a textbook researcher degree of freedom: choosing a cutpoint after seeing the data can manufacture or inflate an apparent threshold effect even when the underlying relationship is a smooth (and much smaller) trend. The paper is commendably transparent about this, and it reports the unconditioned effect (46.8 points from k=1) and a 420-specification curve (34.4–86.4 points, median 70.5) as a partial defense — but the fact that the *headline* abstract and Table 1 numbers, the ones that will be quoted, cited and repeated, are the post-hoc-thresholded ones rather than the pre-registered or unconditioned ones is a design choice that should have been reversed, or at minimum flagged prominently in the abstract itself rather than only in a footnote-style aside deep in the results.
   **What would address it** — Report the unconditioned or a pre-specified threshold as the headline statistic, with the k≥6 cut relegated to a sensitivity analysis; or explicitly pre-register the threshold for a follow-up replication and report that instead.

2. **Issue** — The control condition is not periodicity-free at the k values where the effect is largest: fillers are drawn from an 8-item pool "cycled above k=8" (Section 2, Stimuli), so at k=16, 24, 32 the "control" is itself period-8 text, i.e., it shares the very structural property (periodicity) that the manipulation is supposed to remove.
   **Where** — Section 2 (Stimuli) and Section 3.1 ("Above k=8 the cycled control is itself period-8...").
   **Why it matters** — At exactly the k values that drive the largest raw gaps, the comparison is not "periodic vs aperiodic" but "period-1 vs period-8," which the paper's own period ladder shows is *already* a large-effect contrast (10.4% vs 93.1% exact-match). This means a nontrivial share of the reported "periodicity, not length" gap at high k could be explained by a periodicity gradient *within* the control arm rather than a length confound being ruled out. The authors partially address this with a "never-cycled" control variant (67.6 vs 75.4 points), which is the right instinct, but the paper never states how the never-cycled filler set was constructed (vocabulary size, phonological/semantic matching to the cycled pool) or shows the never-cycled curve broken out by k in the way Fig. 1(a) does for the cycled version — so a reader cannot verify that the residual 67.6-point gap is not itself contaminated by a smaller, less-visible periodicity signature (e.g., syntactic or prosodic recurrence introduced by reusing a small filler pool at all).
   **What would address it** — Report the never-cycled analogue of Figure 1(a) (exact rate vs k, repeated vs never-cycled control) with per-k breakdown, and describe the never-cycled filler construction procedure in the main text.

3. **Issue** — The panel is only three architecture families (n=3), yet the paper makes and headlines a claim about a "gap by architecture family" as a generalizable property of autoregressive TTS.
   **Where** — Section 3.1 ("Our six checkpoints are only three architecture families, so we model the clustering rather than count checkpoints"; partial-pooling paragraph).
   **Why it matters** — The authors are unusually candid that "three groups barely identify a group-level scale" and that under a wide prior the extrapolated between-family effect touches zero (−1.6). That is the correct statistical read of n=3, and it directly undercuts the abstract's implicit framing of this as a cross-architecture, near-universal phenomenon ("across six checkpoints from three architectures"). The single CosyVoice2 "held-out" check (Section 3.1) is a nice pre-committed test (interval nailed down before the checkpoint existed) but it is one additional architecture drawn from a design lineage adjacent to the panel (alignment-supervised AR TTS); it cannot establish generalization to architecturally distant systems (e.g., discrete-diffusion, VALL-E-style multi-codebook decoders, purely non-neural-codec pipelines).
   **Why it matters (cont.)** — Note that the two non-autoregressive baselines *disagree with each other* (VITS immune, F5-TTS not; Section 3.2), which is itself evidence that architecture family predicts almost nothing about susceptibility on its own — undermining rather than supporting a clean "family effect" narrative, and the paper doesn't reconcile this tension with the family-level statistical framing in 3.1.
   **What would address it** — Either drop or heavily caveat the "gap by architecture family" statistic in the abstract, and add at least 2–3 more genuinely distinct architectures (not scale variants) before making a between-family generalization claim.

4. **Issue** — The CTC judge's accuracy is validated ("audited") only on *concatenative* audio built from known ground truth, not on actual model-generated periodic speech, which is the population the judge is then used to score.
   **Where** — Section 2 (Behavioural measurement paragraph): "Audited against concatenative audio with known ground truth... CTC recogniser... ratio 1.00 (n=24, mean 0.996)."
   **Why it matters** — Concatenative audio (clips spliced together) is acoustically clean and lacks the artifacts that a genuinely looping/degenerate TTS decoder can produce — coarticulation smearing across repeated boundaries, prosodic drift, breath/energy collapse, or partial-word truncation at loop boundaries. If the CTC recognizer's error pattern on *actual* repeated generations differs systematically from its behavior on concatenative ground truth (e.g., if it also under- or over-counts on genuinely degenerate loops), the "ratio 1.00" validation does not license treating CTC output as ground truth for the very items whose degeneracy is the phenomenon under study. This is exactly the kind of judge-side confound the paper is careful to rule out for Whisper but does not equivalently rule out for its own preferred instrument on the actual test distribution (as opposed to a held-out 165-clip sample checked "by ear," which is described as enabling spot checks, not a systematic recount).
   **What would address it** — Report inter-rater agreement (CTC vs. trained human counters, not just spot-checks) computed specifically on the model-generated stimuli that produce the reported gap, not only on synthetic concatenative audio.

5. **Issue** — The paper reports extensively on supplementary sections (S1–S28) that are not included in the reviewed text, and the main text does not state how many *independent* carrier/word templates underlie each k or p cell versus how much of the quoted n is seed-replication of a small template set.
   **Where** — Throughout (e.g., "180 stimuli," Section 2; Fig. 1(b) caption: "Rungs p=2,4,8 share one 8-word pool"; n counts scattered across Section 3 without template counts).
   **Why it matters** — If, say, the p=2/4/8 rungs and much of the k-ladder rest on only one or two carrier sentences per condition (replicated across three seeds and six checkpoints to generate the quoted n's), the effective degrees of freedom for generalizing beyond those specific lexical items is far smaller than the reported sample sizes suggest, and item-level idiosyncrasies (a particular word's phonotactics, a particular carrier's prosody) could be driving results attributed to periodicity per se. This is a classic pseudo-replication risk that a n=1539-style number can mask.
   **What would address it** — State, per condition, the number of distinct carrier×word templates versus seeds, and report a template-level (not just checkpoint-level) random effect in the mixed model.

6. **Issue** — The paper explicitly declines any multiple-comparison correction across its 420-specification robustness curve and across the many secondary tests (penalty sweep, exclusion-rule sweep, four-recognizer sweep, duration-injection test, rank-1 patch test), justifying this by framing the work as effect-size estimation rather than hypothesis testing.
   **Where** — Section 3.1 ("Across 420 specifications... we correct across none of them... our claims rest on effect sizes, not p-values").
   **Why it matters** — This is a defensible position for the primary comparison (which is large and consistent by sign across every specification, mitigating the concern), but it is applied uniformly to much smaller, more fragile downstream analyses too — e.g., the rank-1 patch causal-probing result in Section 5 (n=18, qualitative thresholds like "16× its magnitude," "p ≥ 0.38" reported without correction or pre-specified effect thresholds) and the r=0.59 dose-response correlation in Section 4. For these smaller, noisier sub-analyses, the same "we don't correct, we trust effect sizes" stance is considerably less safe, since small-n exploratory comparisons are exactly where uncorrected multiplicity inflates false discovery.
   **What would address it** — Distinguish explicitly between the primary, well-powered periodicity-vs-length comparison (where the no-correction stance is reasonable) and the secondary mechanistic probes (where it is not), and treat the latter as exploratory/hypothesis-generating in the text.

7. **Issue** — No ethics, consent, or conflict-of-interest statement is present, despite the use of human "donors" for concatenative ground-truth audio and human listeners implied by the "checked by ear" validation sample.
   **Where** — Absent throughout; closest mention is Section 2 ("n=12 over 6 donors," "165-clip sample... so a judgement can be checked by ear").
   **Why it matters** — ICASSP and comparable venues expect a statement on data/voice consent when human speech recordings (donor voices) are collected and used, and on annotator/rater conditions when human judgment is part of the evaluation pipeline. Its absence is a reporting gap.
   **What would address it** — Add a brief consent/IRB or ethics statement covering the donor recordings and any human raters used for the 165-clip check.

### Minor concerns
- Figure 1(b) caption labels a model "Qwen-0.6B" while Section 2/Table 1 consistently use "Qwen3-TTS-0.6B" — inconsistent naming.
- CosyVoice2 is introduced mid-Results (Section 3.1) as a test case without ever being listed among the panel/baseline models in the Section 2 "Models" paragraph; a reader has to infer it's an additional, separately-run architecture.
- The abstract's clause "a gap of 67.6 points by architecture family (n=3) with never-cycled control fillers, 75.4 with the cycled pool the ladder ships" is dense to the point of being nearly unparseable on first read; the paper would benefit from moving this level of qualification out of the abstract's single run-on sentence.
- Table 1's "Qwen3-TTS-1.7B" row (0.0% median error, 38.9% exact match) is the one checkpoint whose median-error statistic reads as "no deficit," and the text has to explain away this discrepancy ("still renders repeated items exactly 60.0 points less often"); presenting median error as a headline metric alongside exact-match without flagging upfront that they can dissociate this sharply invites misreading of Table 1 at a glance.
- The duration-injection ablation for F5-TTS (Section 3.2) uses an exclusion rule ("any gain above 8.3 points excluded," n=60) that is stated without justification for the specific threshold chosen.
- Self-citation [7] (companion paper, arXiv:2604.08591) and citation [13] (Venkatesh, arXiv:2605.09239) both carry very recent 2026 arXiv identifiers; worth double-checking at camera-ready that both are indexed and stable before publication.
- The Lean 4 formalization is asserted "free of sorry and with an axiom audit" (Section 4) but no link to the specific proof file or line count is given in the main text (only a general repository pointer), making this claim unverifiable without digging through the supplement/repo.

### Verdict
**Major revision** — the central periodicity-vs-length dissociation is a genuinely interesting and reasonably well-triangulated result, but the post-hoc threshold selection, the residual periodicity leakage into the cycled control at high k, the n=3 basis for cross-architecture claims, and the judge validation on concatenative rather than model-generated audio each need to be fixed or substantially caveated before the headline numbers can be taken at face value.

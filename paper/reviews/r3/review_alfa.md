### Summary

The paper argues that repetition-induced counting failures in autoregressive TTS (stopping early, running on, looping) are not a training or decoding artifact but a structural consequence of periodic conditioning: if the per-repetition state update contracts (Assumption 2), then no Lipschitz readout of the decoder's hidden state — including the stop-token logit — can distinguish repetition counts beyond a horizon N* that the authors derive in closed form and machine-check in Lean 4. They test the theorem's *consequence*, not its premise, across six TTS checkpoints (Llasa 1B/3B/8B, XTTS-v2, Qwen3-TTS 0.6B/1.7B), scoring output with a CTC recognizer (rather than the autoregressive Whisper, shown to be itself repetition-biased) and measuring effective rank of hidden-state trajectories. The headline results are: (1) a median relative count error of −8.3% at k ≥ 6 for repeated content versus 0.0% for length-matched non-repeating controls, and (2) a ~0.50× reduction in the trajectory's effective-rank growth rate under repetition relative to control, robust to output-diversity covariates.

### Major concerns

1. **Issue** — The paper's central novelty claim — that its contraction/fixed-point mechanism is distinct from prior "representational collapse" accounts — is asserted rather than demonstrated, and the one citation load-bearing this distinction is textually broken.
   **Where** — Section 5 ("The closest prior result is representational collapse [9]…") and Reference [9], which in the given text reads: "Federico Barbero, Andrea Banino, Steven Kapturowski, et al., ,” .” with no title, venue, or identifier.
   **Why it matters** — This is the single most important related-work comparison in the paper — it is the sentence that draws the line between "our mechanism" and "the nearest existing mechanism." I traced the reference to Barbero, Banino, Kapturowski et al., "Transformers need glasses! Information over-squashing in language tasks" [2406.04267], which frames representational collapse via a signal-propagation/over-squashing analysis in which sensitivity between tokens depends on the structure of attention paths, not merely on sequence *length*. The paper sets up "collapse-by-length" as a straw alternative ("a 32-word control and a 32-word repetition fail alike, since both are equally long") and then refutes it with the length-matched control. But an over-squashing account is not purely length-indexed: repeated, near-identical spans are exactly the case where attention paths become maximally correlated (which is what Lemma 1 shows), so an over-squashing theorist could predict the same periodicity-sensitive gap the authors observe, via squashing that is aggravated by content redundancy rather than by length alone. The paper never engages this version of the alternative, so the empirical dissociation (Fig. 1b) does not actually rule out [2406.04267]'s framework — it rules out only a naive length-only reading of it. Given that the supporting citation is also mechanically malformed in the text, a reader cannot verify this comparison was made carefully rather than as a rhetorical foil.
   **What would address it** — Fix the citation; then engage the actual over-squashing sensitivity bound (e.g., show it does or does not predict a periodicity-specific gap when instantiated on repeated versus distinct token sequences) rather than only its length-only strawman.

2. **Issue** — Adjacent formal/theoretical literature on transformer state-tracking limits is absent, weakening the "first formal hallucination result" and "quantitative impossibility result" novelty claims.
   **Where** — Section 1 (contribution list) and Section 5 (related-work paragraph, citations [9–13]).
   **Why it matters** — The paper's core theoretical object — a discrete count that a transformer-based decoder must carry as internal state and that can provably become unrecoverable — sits squarely in the literature on state tracking and expressivity limits of transformer LMs, e.g. Li, Guo & Andreas, "(How) Do Language Models Track State?" [2503.02854], which studies exactly whether/how transformer LMs track discrete state (there, permutation composition) through generation. None of this line is cited or discussed, so the reader cannot assess how the Banach-fixed-point framing here relates to, extends, or is subsumed by existing formal characterizations of what transformers can and cannot track internally. This matters directly for the claim of being "the first hallucination result in speech synthesis carried out as a formal artifact" and for calling Theorem 1 "a quantitative impossibility result for repetition counting in AR decoders" — both are claims about priority and formal novelty that need to be checked against exactly this kind of work, not only against dynamical-systems papers on attention rank/clustering ([10–12]).
   **What would address it** — Add a paragraph relating Theorem 1 to the state-tracking/expressivity literature, specifying what is new (the Lipschitz-readout/decision-margin horizon, the Lean formalization) versus what is a re-derivation of known limits in a new application domain.

3. **Issue** — The theorem's premise (Assumption 2, contraction) is never verified for any of the six systems studied, yet the paper's causal language throughout treats the theorem as explaining the observed behavior.
   **Where** — Section 4.4 ("We could not measure q itself… the distances measure localisation error instead") versus the abstract/conclusion language ("The decoder does not merely err on repeated text; it runs out of states in which the count could be represented").
   **Why it matters** — What is actually measured is a *consequence* consistent with contraction (effective-rank saturation, undercounting) but this consequence is also consistent with other mechanisms that are not the specific contraction map F posited in Assumption 2 (e.g., token-level exposure bias compounding, learned repetition-suppression heuristics, or the over-squashing account in concern #1). Because the antecedent is admittedly unmeasured, the paper has shown correlational support for a prediction of the theorem, not an instance of the theorem. The rhetorical force of "runs out of states" borrows the certainty of the Lean-checked implication for a claim that is actually empirical and unconfirmed at the premise level.
   **What would address it** — Consistently phrase results as "consistent with Assumption 2" rather than as demonstrating the mechanism, or attempt an indirect test of contraction beyond the failed boundary-distance approach (e.g., an intervention that manipulates q, such as varying repetition-penalty strength, and checking whether N* moves as predicted by Eq. 1).

4. **Issue** — The one model reported as being in the theorem's "silent," non-contracting regime (Qwen3-TTS-1.7B) still shows a statistically significant capacity-gain deficit under repetition, undercutting the clean two-regime story used to explain the one panel exception.
   **Where** — Table 1 (Qwen3-TTS-1.7B: capacity-gain ratio 0.78, described in Section 4.1/4.3 as having "disjoint 95% intervals for all six panel checkpoints") versus Section 4.1's framing ("The theorem is silent about such a model — if its per-repetition map does not contract, no horizon follows").
   **Why it matters** — If Qwen3-TTS-1.7B genuinely does not contract (0.0% count error at every k up to 32), Theorem 1 predicts nothing distinguishing it from control on the capacity measure either, yet its rep/control ratio (0.78) is still well below 1 with a disjoint interval — i.e., it shows the theorem's signature attenuated but present. The paper's discussion only notes in passing ("the capacity ratio does not order k* across models, and we do not claim it does") without reconciling why a model in the "no contraction" regime nonetheless exhibits the qualitative effect the theorem is meant to explain in the contracting regime. This graded pattern is arguably more consistent with a single continuous mechanism (weak contraction everywhere, with model-dependent strength) than with the categorical "collapse vs. no collapse" story the paper uses to turn its one non-conforming checkpoint into confirming evidence ("the outcome a conditional theorem should produce").
   **What would address it** — Report q (or a capacity-gain-ratio proxy for it) as a continuous variable across all six models and test whether N* (Eq. 1) or count error scales with it continuously, rather than only using the categorical regime split to explain away the exception.

5. **Issue** — A substantial part of the paper's conceptual grounding (the "attractor" framing itself) is imported from an unpublished, very recent companion preprint by the same three authors, with only a one-sentence differentiation.
   **Where** — Section 1 ("The work is the autoregressive-decoder companion to a spectral study of hallucination in encoder–decoder ASR [5], which identified an attractor regime descriptively; here the attractor is what the theorem is about") and Reference [5] (arXiv:2604.08591, 2026, same author list).
   **Why it matters** — For a paper whose primary contribution is claimed to be theoretical/formal novelty, leaning on a same-author, same-year, not-yet-independently-vetted companion paper for the foundational "attractor" concept makes it hard for a reviewer to assess how much of the present paper's conceptual contribution is genuinely new versus a formalization of the authors' own prior informal claim. The differentiation offered ("descriptively" vs. "the theorem is about") is thin.
   **What would address it** — State explicitly which specific claims in [5] are assumed as given here, and which are independently established in this paper; if [5] is still under review, note that transparently, since it affects how independently the current paper's claims can be evaluated.

### Minor concerns

- Reference [1] is cited in the Introduction as documenting the stability problem, but the bibliography notes it was "Withdrawn 13 Feb 2026" — this caveat appears only in the reference list, not where the claim is used in Section 1, so a reader skimming the motivation wouldn't know the citation was retracted.
- The pooled statistic "median error of -8.3% [−12.5, −8.3] (n = 586)" has its point estimate sitting exactly on the upper edge of its own reported confidence interval, which is numerically unusual for a median CI and should be double-checked.
- The pooled -8.3% figure happens to exactly equal the per-model Table 1 value for Qwen3-TTS-0.6B; likely coincidental, but worth a sanity check given the CI oddity above.
- The released repository is named `icassp_antispoofing`, which does not describe this paper's content (repetition/counting collapse in TTS); this is a presentational inconsistency that could confuse readers trying to locate or trust the artifact as dedicated to this work.
- Reference [12] (arXiv:2608.08922) is dated the same month as the current review, i.e. essentially contemporaneous with this submission — worth double-checking that the differentiation from this paper ("ours indexes it by generation step") was written against the final version of that work rather than an early draft.
- Table 1's "k*" is defined in the caption but not actually populated as a column in the reproduced table — only capacity-gain and count-error columns appear, which is either a transcription omission or a mismatch between caption and table.
- The XTTS-v2 ablation (repetition penalty disabled) is reported as "uninformative about the dissociation" once its output degenerates, but the degenerate-output rate itself is never quantified, so the reader cannot judge whether "almost entirely degenerate" is 60% or 99% of items, or whether a partial-degeneracy subset could still have been analyzed.

### Verdict

**Major revision** — the empirical design (length-matched periodicity controls, CTC re-scoring to avoid a biased judge) is genuinely careful, but the paper's novelty claims relative to closely related theoretical accounts of representational/attention collapse are asserted through a one-line dismissal and a broken citation rather than argued, and the theorem's own premise is admittedly unverified, so the causal story presented in the abstract outruns what Sections 4–5 actually establish.

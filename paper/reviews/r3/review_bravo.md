### Summary

The paper makes three linked claims: (1) a formal argument — attention over repeated spans dilutes to near-uniform (Lemma 1), and if the resulting per-repetition state map contracts, no Lipschitz readout can recover the repetition count past a computable horizon (Theorem 1); (2) this is machine-checked in Lean 4/mathlib; (3) six AR-TTS checkpoints show a periodicity-specific undercount (median −8.3% at k≥6) that vanishes on length-matched non-periodic controls (0.0%), and a matching effective-rank ("capacity") saturation on generation trajectories, scored via a CTC recognizer chosen specifically because the more obvious judge (Whisper) was shown to be periodicity-biased. The formal result is real and the periodicity-vs-length control is a genuinely well-built experiment. My core problem is that the paper swaps out one biased judge (Whisper) for a second judge (CTC) whose own susceptibility to periodicity is never audited by the same protocol used to disqualify the first — so the headline metric and the paper's "sharpest test" both run through an instrument of unestablished validity on exactly the dimension being measured. Combine that with a theorem whose premise (contraction, Assumption 2) is explicitly never measured, and an abstract that asserts representational erasure ("runs out of states") while the paper's own most direct test of that claim (the ridge probe, §4.4) reports mere degradation — and the paper is a solid piece of engineering wrapped around empirical claims that are less nailed-down than the framing suggests.

### Major concerns

1. **Issue** — The replacement judge (CTC) is never audited for periodicity bias with the same protocol used to disqualify the judge it replaced. `[Evidence — distinguishing test]`
   **Where** — §3, "Behavioural measurement, and why the obvious judge is wrong."
   **Why it matters** — The paper's central move is: Whisper is shown biased (counted/true ratio 0.27–0.65 at k≥4 on concatenative audio with known ground truth, vs. 1.00 on distinct-word controls), so they swap to a CTC recognizer, asserting it's unbiased because "output length [is] governed by the acoustics." But CTC decoding is *defined* by a collapsing operator over consecutive identical/near-identical frame predictions — it is not obviously immune to exactly the periodicity-driven degeneracy that sank Whisper, and forced acoustic-frame alignment is well known in ASR to under-segment repetitive or low-energy stretches. The paper never runs the concatenative-ground-truth audit on the CTC judge that it ran on Whisper. Since every number in the paper — the −8.3% headline, the "sharpest test" (periodicity vs. length, Fig. 1b), the 0.50 capacity ratio — is measured through this unaudited instrument, and since a periodicity-specific bias would move exactly the two things the paper reports as its central discovery (repeated items score lower than length-matched controls), this is not a peripheral gap. It sits directly under the paper's strongest claim.
   **What would address it** — Run the identical concatenative-audio-with-known-ground-truth audit used for Whisper on the CTC recognizer, split by k and by periodic vs. control condition, and report the counted/true ratio the same way.

2. **Issue** — The abstract and §4.3 assert representational erasure ("it runs out of states in which the count could be represented"), but the paper's own most direct test of that claim (§4.4, ridge probe) reports only mild degradation, and a paper the authors cite themselves reports the opposite result in a closely analogous setting. `[Narrative — overclaiming]`
   **Where** — Abstract; §4.3 closing sentence; §4.4.
   **Why it matters** — §4.4 states plainly: a ridge probe recovers log₂k "only coarsely" (median R²=0.48), and using the first-third/last-third contrast (which cancels probe capacity), the count is "retained at 0.97 of its early level on repeated text against 1.11 on controls... degradation rather than erasure." That is a ~13% relative gap, described in the authors' own words as "degradation rather than erasure" — a materially weaker claim than "runs out of states in which the count could be represented." Worse, the paper cites [13] Venkatesh, "Repeated-Token Counting Reveals a Dissociation Between Representations and Outputs" [2605.09239], which reports that in text LLMs, "linear probes on the residual stream decode the correct count with near-perfect accuracy at every post-embedding layer... even at the exact layers where the wrong answer crystallizes in the output" — i.e., the opposite conclusion (representation intact, output policy at fault) using a stronger version of exactly the test this paper runs weakly. The Discussion dismisses this with "our probes favour representational degradation, but the two are not exclusive" — that sentence resolves nothing; it just juxtaposes a weak result with a contradicting strong one and moves on. A reader who reads only the abstract walks away believing the count is provably erased from the state; the body's own strongest direct test doesn't support that.
   **What would address it** — Downgrade the abstract/§4.3 language to match §4.4's own hedge, or run a probe with power comparable to [13]'s (e.g., matched layer/window granularity) and report whether the TTS result genuinely diverges from the text-LLM finding rather than asserting non-exclusivity as a substitute for a test.

3. **Issue** — No existing repetition/hallucination mitigation method is actually run or compared against; they are cited only as claims. `[Evidence — baselines]`
   **Where** — §1 ("attribut[ion]... to exposure bias or the absence of a monotonic alignment constraint, with fixes proposed at the level of attention supervision or decoding rules [3,4]"); Discussion ("Existing mitigations act late [1,3]").
   **Why it matters** — RALL-E, CosyVoice 2, and the attention-guidance method of [1] are cited as competing accounts/fixes but never evaluated on the repetition ladder. "Existing mitigations act late" is an empirical claim about what those methods do to the measured error/capacity curves; nothing in the paper measures it. This isn't a strawman baseline problem so much as an absent-baseline problem: the reader can't tell whether the paper's framing (contraction/margin levers) explains why prior fixes underperform, or whether those fixes actually work fine on this exact benchmark and the comparison was simply never run.
   **What would address it** — Run at least one competing mitigation (e.g., a monotonic-alignment-constrained checkpoint, or CosyVoice 2, already cited and presumably accessible) through the identical ladder and CTC-judge pipeline.

4. **Issue** — Several headline statistics pool over a panel the paper itself describes as heterogeneous, and multiple percentage-based claims carry no CI or significance test. `[Statistical rigour]`
   **Where** — §4.1 ("median error of −8.3% [−12.5, −8.3] (n=586)"); §4.2 ("the control is the less probable member in 87% of the 54 matched pairs"; "a positive gap in 78% of pairs," §4.3).
   **Why it matters** — The panel explicitly splits 5-of-6 vs. 1-of-6 (Qwen3-TTS-1.7B at 0.0% error), yet the pooled median (−8.3%) is reported as a single headline number in the abstract without qualifying that one-sixth of the panel contributes nothing to it — a reader skimming the abstract has no way to know the effect is absent in one of six models until they reach §4.1. Separately, 87% and 78% are reported as point estimates over 54 and an unstated-but-presumably-similar number of matched pairs, with no binomial CI or exact test, in a paper that otherwise uses bootstrap CIs everywhere else — the omission specifically on these two numbers, both of which are load-bearing for ruling out the naturalness and acoustic-attractor alternatives, is inconsistent methodological rigor.
   **What would address it** — Report per-model (not just pooled) medians in the abstract/§4.1 prose, and attach exact binomial CIs to the 87%/78% figures.

5. **Issue** — The effective-rank "capacity gain" measure, presented as a direct readout of the theorem's mechanism, cannot distinguish genuine loss of count-representable capacity from the trivial fact that acoustically self-similar output has a lower-rank hidden-state trajectory for reasons unrelated to counting — and the paper's own "correctly-rendered items" control arguably argues against its own story. `[Evidence — distinguishing test / alternative explanation]`
   **Where** — §4.3.
   **Why it matters** — Any generative decoder producing near-identical acoustic frames repeatedly will show a lower-rank hidden-state trajectory than one producing diverse content — this is close to a tautology about periodic signals, independent of any claim about whether "count" is represented. The paper controls for output monotony via type/token ratio, adjacent-repeat rate, and length as covariates, and by restricting to items each model rendered *correctly*. But the restricted-to-correct analysis is the sharper test and it cuts the wrong way for the paper's thesis: on items where the model got the count right — where, on the paper's own theory, the state should *not* have run out of room to represent the count — capacity growth is still only 0.52× the control rate. That is at least as consistent with "Neff tracks acoustic self-similarity, full stop, and has nothing specifically to do with count-representation capacity" as it is with the paper's contraction/erasure story, and the paper doesn't attempt the test that would separate them: showing the same Neff-saturation pattern on periodic acoustic content that carries no count semantics at all (e.g., a droning or musically periodic generation task), which would falsify the "this is about counting" interpretation if it also saturates at a similar ratio.
   **What would address it** — Run the identical Neff/capacity-gain measurement on periodic-but-uncounted generation targets (no discrete "how many" semantics) to check whether the 0.50 ratio is specific to count-bearing repetition or a generic property of periodic decoder output.

### Belief update

Moderate update, narrowly scoped: I'm more willing to believe that AR-TTS repetition-count failures have a component specific to *periodicity* rather than *length* — the length-matched control design in §4.2 is genuinely good (matched carrier, matched word count, categorical 0.0% vs. non-zero result, plus the naturalness and token-cost cross-checks), and that basic dissociation is hard to explain away even granting the judge concerns above, since a fixed periodicity-only bias would need to be fairly specific to fully account for it. I did **not** update toward the mechanistic claim the paper is actually named after — "counting collapse" as state-space contraction erasing the count. Assumption 2 (contraction, q<1) is explicitly never measured (§4.4, Discussion: "our attempt to measure that premise directly failed"), and the paper's own most direct empirical proxy for erasure (the ridge probe) reports degradation, not erasure, consistent with the competing account it cites but doesn't rule out. The Lean formalization is real and well-scoped, but it certifies an implication whose antecedent nobody has shown holds for these six models — it's rigorous math orbiting an unconfirmed premise, not a mechanistic explanation validated by the experiments.

### Verdict

**Major revision** — concern 5 (and its companion, concern 1) means the paper's two central empirical instruments — the CTC judge and the effective-rank "capacity" measure — are each consistent with an unexamined alternative (residual judge bias; trivial acoustic self-similarity) that the paper doesn't rule out, so the headline numbers and the "runs out of states" framing aren't yet earned by the evidence presented, even though the underlying periodicity-vs-length dissociation and the formal apparatus are worth keeping.

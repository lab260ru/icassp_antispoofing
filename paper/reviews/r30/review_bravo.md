### Steelman

The strongest reading of this paper is that it is unusually statistically self-aware for a systems/TTS venue. It builds a length-matched control into the design so the headline contrast (repeated vs. control) is a within-item comparison rather than a between-condition one; it flags its own post-hoc threshold (k ≥ 6) and reports the unconditioned effect anyway; it runs what is effectively a specification-curve/multiverse analysis (420 specifications, six analysis axes) and reports the full range and median rather than a single cherry-picked number; it fits a partial-pooling (mixed-effects) model to avoid treating six checkpoints as six independent architectures; and it explicitly probes the model's own inferential honesty by testing (not just asserting) the extrapolation on a held-out architecture (CosyVoice 2). That is a genuinely more careful evidentiary posture than most papers making a claim this strong. Pressing on it from that baseline is where the real problems surface.

### Summary
The paper asks whether neural TTS models mis-render repeated words because the input is long or because it is periodic/repetitive, and isolates the two with a length-matched repetition ladder (180 stimuli, k = 1–32) across six checkpoints spanning three architectures plus non-autoregressive baselines. It reports a large, consistent repeated-vs-control gap (46.8 points overall, 75.4 at k ≥ 6), argues the effect tracks periodicity rather than raw length via a separate period ladder, rules out decoding rule, judge artifact, and exclusion criteria as confounds via ablations and multiple recognisers, and then formally states and tests a "contraction" mechanism (via a Lean-4-verified bound) for why counting should fail — measuring the relevant Lipschitz constant q across checkpoints and finding it empirically refutes the mechanism (q ≫ 1 everywhere).

### Major concerns

1. **Issue** — The paper's stated evidentiary framework ("effect sizes, not hypothesis tests, and we correct across none") is not applied consistently: it invokes one bare p-value and treats interval-exclusion-of-zero across "six interval constructions" as a significance criterion, without ever specifying how any interval was constructed.
   **Where** — Section 3.1, robustness-envelope paragraph ("Across 420 specifications... we correct across none: the curve is a robustness envelope over one comparison, not a family of hypotheses, and our claims rest on effect sizes"); Section 3.1, periodicity paragraph ("Shuffling each item's own multiset recovers 0.6% of a 17.7-point deficit, missing the pre-committed band (P=0.010)"); repeated phrases like "all six interval constructions exclude zero" and "76.4 points... [63.2, 89.2]" with no stated CI method or coverage level anywhere in the main text.
   **Why it matters** — If the paper's epistemology is "we report effect sizes and do not correct for multiplicity because these are not independent hypotheses," then introducing a single p-value at the one point where the result is inconvenient (the shuffle experiment "missing the pre-committed band") reads as an opportunistic switch to significance-testing language exactly when it helps the argument. Without knowing whether the reported intervals are bootstrap, Wilson, normal-approximation, or Bayesian credible intervals, and over what resampling unit (item? checkpoint? seed?), a reader cannot judge whether "excludes zero in all six constructions" reflects genuinely independent lines of evidence or six re-parameterizations of the same noisy statistic. This directly undercuts the paper's central defense of its own robustness-checking philosophy. Relatedly, unconstrained "garden of forking paths" multiverse analyses of this kind — impressive-looking on the surface — have been argued to function as a way to manufacture the appearance of robustness without actually pre-registering or exhaustively enumerating the specification space [2607.14623]; the paper gives no supplement excerpt or table here that lets a reader verify the 420 specifications are an exhaustive, principled grid rather than a curated subset.
   **What would address it** — State the CI construction method and coverage level for every interval reported in the main text (ideally in one sentence near first use); either drop the P=0.010 or justify explicitly why that one comparison merits a frequentist test while ~420 others are treated as an unadjusted envelope; publish the specification-curve axis/level table (which combinations of exclusions × k-threshold × cycled/never-cycled × seed subset × aggregation × outcome definition make up the 420) so the multiverse's exhaustiveness, rather than its selectivity, can be checked.

2. **Issue** — Several key "n" values that anchor strong claims are clustered/non-independent samples reported as flat sample sizes, which likely overstates precision.
   **Where** — Section 2 ("...it transcribes the repeated word exactly once... (0.19 of the true count, n=12 over 6 donors)" and "ratio 1.00 (n=24, mean 0.996)"); Section 2 ("Every arm renders every item at three seeds").
   **Why it matters** — n=12 over 6 donors is 2 samples per donor; if within-donor samples are correlated (same voice, same recording chain), the effective sample size for the Whisper-vs-CTC audit is well below 12, yet the 0.19-vs-1.00 contrast is used as the load-bearing justification for switching the entire panel's scoring instrument (2052 generations) to the CTC judge. Likewise, the mixed-effects model treats architecture and checkpoint as random effects but the text gives no indication that seed (nested within item within checkpoint) is modeled as a variance component, despite the authors themselves noting seed-driven stochasticity differs sharply by family ("Qwen3-TTS the most stochastic family"). If seed variance is pooled into residual rather than modeled, the reported 76.4-point gap's interval could be too narrow.
   **What would address it** — Report effective sample size (or an ICC/donor-level random effect) for the judge-audit comparisons; add seed as an explicit random effect (or report a seed-level variance decomposition) in the mixed-effects model, or show that seed variance is negligible relative to checkpoint variance.

3. **Issue** — Two specific analyses use exclusion/threshold rules that are acknowledged as chosen after seeing the data, with one case unclear on direction of bias.
   **Where** — Section 3.2 ("The k ≥ 6 threshold was chosen after seeing where the arms diverge"); Section 3.2, F5-TTS duration-supply result ("lifts exact renderings 43.3% to 76.7% below k=12 and 0.0 at or above it (n=60, gains above 8.3 points excluded)").
   **Why it matters** — The k ≥ 6 admission is handled reasonably (unconditioned 46.8 figure is also given, and the threshold is varied in the specification curve). The F5-TTS exclusion is not: "gains above 8.3 points excluded" is stated with no rationale, and it is impossible to tell from the text whether this rule was pre-specified or discovered after seeing which items looked anomalous, nor whether excluding large gains inflates or deflates the reported 43.3-to-76.7 improvement. Given that this number supports a specific mechanistic claim (that supplying per-token duration is what lets F5-TTS count), an unexplained post-hoc filter on the outcome variable is exactly the kind of researcher degree of freedom the paper's own robustness-curve philosophy is trying to guard against elsewhere.
   **What would address it** — State whether the 8.3-point exclusion rule was pre-registered; report the F5-TTS duration result both with and without the exclusion so readers can see its effect on the estimate.

4. **Issue** — The CosyVoice 2 "test" of the partial-pooling extrapolation is a single new architecture (n=90) compared against a wide, prior-sensitive predictive interval, but is narrated with the confidence of a confirmatory test.
   **Where** — Section 3.1, CosyVoice 2 paragraph ("...three groups barely identify a group-level scale and under the widest prior on it that gap touches zero (−1.6), so we did not rest on the extrapolation: we tested it... inside the predicted interval and nearer its 65.3 mean than an interval that wide entitles us to expect").
   **Why it matters** — The authors' own admission that the interval "touches zero" under the widest prior on the group-level scale means the predictive distribution is barely informative with only three architecture groups. A single data point landing inside a wide interval is weak confirmatory evidence (a null result — the model failing to predict CosyVoice 2 — would also often land "inside" such a wide interval by construction). Describing this as having "tested" the extrapolation, rather than "failed to falsify it," overstates what one architecture-level observation can establish, especially since the phrase "nearer its 65.3 mean than an interval that wide entitles us to expect" gestures at a probability judgment without giving the actual predictive density or a formal surprise statistic.
   **What would address it** — Report the predictive density (or a posterior predictive p-value) of the CosyVoice 2 observation under at least two priors on the group-level scale, and soften the "we tested it" framing to reflect that n=1 new architecture is a weak confirmatory test, not a powered one.

5. **Issue** — Several small-sample null results are stated as strong absence-of-effect claims without power or interval reporting.
   **Where** — Section 5, rank-1 patch paragraph ("on Llasa-8B nothing is detectable at all (n=36; S26)"); Section 3.2, VITS paragraph ("VITS shows no dissociation... (65.6% against 58.9%, n=90, a gap of -6.7... and not for want of competence (S13)").
   **Why it matters** — "Nothing is detectable" and "no dissociation" from n=36 and n=90 samples respectively are absence-of-evidence claims that would need an explicit minimum-detectable-effect or CI to be interpreted as evidence of absence rather than merely underpowered null results. This matters most for the rank-1 patch claim, since it feeds directly into the paper's most novel mechanistic conclusion (Section 5): that the count is "registered but carries nothing" in the readout.
   **What would address it** — Report CIs (or a minimum detectable effect given the observed variance) for both null claims; rephrase to "no detectable effect at this sample size" rather than categorical absence language.

6. **Issue** (claims vs. evidence, secondary to lens) — The paper's headline "exactly right" percentages (e.g., 18.2% vs. 94.3%, Table 1) depend entirely on the CTC judge's transcript-matching procedure, which is validated only against concatenative audio with known ground truth (Section 2), never against human listener judgments — a limitation the Discussion itself flags ("the judge is validated on audio, not listeners") but that is easy to miss given how central "exactly right" is to the abstract and Table 1.
   **Where** — Section 2 (judge audit), Table 1, Section 5 limitations.
   **Why it matters** — The instrument-validity claim ("Nor is it the recogniser...") rests on the CTC judge agreeing with itself and with a second automatic instrument, not with a human ground truth for what "correctly rendered repetition count" perceptually means (e.g., a model that renders 5 of 6 repetitions with a barely audible extra syllable might be scored identically to one that drops 2 words outright). The paper is honest about this gap but its placement (one clause in the Discussion) undersells how much weight the exact-match statistic carries in the abstract and Table 1.
   **What would address it** — Either report inter-rater agreement between the CTC judge and human transcribers on a subsample (the 165-clip audit sample already shipped could support this), or move the "not validated against listeners" caveat next to the first use of "exactly right" rather than only in Discussion.

### Minor concerns
- Fig. 1 caption's "hollow markers are the p = k arm, matched to neither in vocabulary nor length" is opaque without cross-referencing the main text; consider a one-clause gloss in the caption itself.
- "It favours the control in all 6 checkpoints, where models render repeated text exactly in 18.2% of generations against 94.3%" (Abstract) has an ambiguous pronoun antecedent for "It."
- Inconsistent units: "points" (percentage-point differences) and raw percentages are interleaved without a style convention, occasionally requiring the reader to infer which is meant (e.g., "60.0 points less often").
- OCR/typesetting artifact: "V ALL-E 2" appears broken across multiple instances (Abstract, Introduction, Section 3.2, references) — should render as "VALL-E 2".
- Self-citation [8] (arXiv:2604.08591, "2026") is a companion paper; confirm it is publicly resolvable at review time since several claims (e.g., "companion to a spectral study of hallucination") depend on it.
- The supplement is referenced extensively (S1–S29) but none of its content is reproduced or summarized in the reviewed text, making several statistical claims (CI method, bootstrap procedure for q, the mixed-effects model specification) unverifiable from the main paper alone.

### Verdict
**Major revision** — the experimental design and mechanism-refutation are genuinely strong, but the statistical reporting is internally inconsistent (selective use of significance testing inside an otherwise no-correction framework), several small/clustered samples are treated as more informative than their structure supports, and at least one post-hoc exclusion rule (F5-TTS "gains above 8.3 points excluded") needs a stated rationale before the headline effect sizes can be fully trusted.

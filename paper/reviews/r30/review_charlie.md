### Summary

The paper claims three things: (1) a documented TTS failure mode — miscounting/looping on repeated phrases — is driven by repetition per se and not by input length, shown via a repetition ladder with length-matched controls across six checkpoints (46.8-point gap overall, 75.4 at k≥6); (2) the standard instrument for measuring this, Whisper, is itself biased in the direction of the effect and must be replaced with a CTC recognizer, a methodological point the paper treats as a result in its own right; and (3) a Lean-4-verified contraction bound that the authors themselves proposed as the mechanism is measured and refuted — the boundary-to-boundary Jacobian is expansive (q = 21–348), not contractive, on every checkpoint and on both arms. The controlled-ladder design is a genuinely good idea and, on the numbers given, internally consistent (the per-checkpoint and per-family aggregates in Section 3.1 reduce correctly from Table 1). But the entire case is built on numbers alone: not one transcript, spectrogram, or worked example of the actual failure — the thing the abstract opens on ("loop, truncate and lose count") — appears anywhere in the manuscript. Every load-bearing claim about "420 specifications," Lean proofs, and per-item audits is deferred to an unincluded supplement (S1–S29), so the reviewer is asked to trust aggregate statistics for a phenomenon that, by the authors' own admission, needs "to be checked by ear."

### Major concerns

1. **Issue** — Non-autoregressive baselines are single-checkpoint per architecture, so the "not the decoding rule" claim rests on n=1 per condition for the two comparison classes that matter most. `[Evidence — weak baselines]`
   **Where** — Section 3.2, VITS and F5-TTS results; the authors themselves concede "at one model per strategy that is suggestive rather than isolating" for the AR-vs-non-AR split.
   **Why it matters** — The paper's strongest architectural claim (dissociation traced to "where the count must live," duration-per-token vs one total) is drawn from exactly two data points, one on each side. A single VITS checkpoint being immune and a single F5-TTS checkpoint failing is compatible with dozens of other checkpoint-idiosyncratic explanations (training data, duration predictor quality, model scale) that have nothing to do with the proposed duration-representation distinction.
   **What would address it** — At least 2-3 checkpoints per non-AR family (e.g., different VITS/F5-TTS scales or public checkpoints) before asserting the duration-representation explanation as anything beyond a hypothesis.

2. **Issue** — The core "does ordering carry the periodicity effect" question is explicitly left unresolved, with results that point in opposite directions on different checkpoints, yet the abstract's headline framing ("half the deficit surviving at period two") reads as settled. `[Evidence — missing ablations]`
   **Where** — Section 3.1, "Does the ordering carry it?" — shuffling gains 17.8 points on two Qwen checkpoints, is null on a third, and *loses* 30.0 points on XTTS-v2, with the authors stating "intervals excluding zero both ways."
   **Why it matters** — This is not a minor loose end; it's the ablation that would tell you *what* about repetition causes the deficit (adjacency/ordering vs. multiset composition). Without it, "repetition, not length" (the title's claim) is under-specified — repetition of *what*, exactly, is still open, and the paper ships that ambiguity as a footnote to a monotone-looking Figure 1(b).
   **What would address it** — A dedicated experiment isolating ordering from composition at a period where both arrangements are well-defined (the paper notes p=2 and p=8 can't distinguish them; p=4 is the only usable rung and it's underpowered at one pool per architecture).

3. **Issue** — Every quantitative claim beyond Table 1 and Figure 1 — the "420 specifications" robustness sweep, the Lean 4 proof and axiom audit, the mixed-effects model, the partial-pooling posterior, the four-recognizer cross-check, the exclusion audits — is cited only as "S1"–"S29" and not reproducible from the paper text. `[Reproducibility]`
   **Where** — Throughout Section 3 and 4; e.g. "Across 420 specifications over six analysis axes... the gap runs 34.4–86.4 points... (S11)," "Both are machine-checked in Lean 4... (S1–S2)."
   **Why it matters** — A reader evaluating this manuscript in isolation (as any reviewer must) cannot check a single one of these numbers. The paper is candid that the supplement carries essentially all the statistical machinery that makes the headline claims robust rather than anecdotal — which means the manuscript itself, standing alone, doesn't establish robustness; it asserts it and points elsewhere.
   **What would address it** — At minimum, the specification-curve figure and the Lean formalization's key lemma statement belong in the main text (even compressed), not just referenced by supplement tag.

4. **Issue** — The paper's own multiple-comparisons stance is a rhetorical dodge: "we correct across none... the curve is a robustness envelope over one comparison, not a family of hypotheses." `[Statistical rigour]`
   **Where** — End of Section 3.1.
   **Why it matters** — Calling 420 analytic variants "one comparison" because they all test the same underlying hypothesis is a framing choice, not a statistical fact — it's precisely the kind of post-hoc justification that should raise suspicion when it conveniently licenses skipping correction. The shuffle-ordering test even reports a specific p-value (P=0.010) against a "pre-committed band" that the paper says it misses, but then reframes the same result as a checkpoint-level split with "intervals excluding zero both ways" — two different statistical framings of one result, chosen based on which one the number happened to produce.
   **What would address it** — Either report the specification curve with a stated correction (even a conservative Bonferroni-style bound) or drop the "420 specifications" framing in favor of 3-4 pre-registered robustness checks reported plainly.

5. **Issue** — The manuscript contains not one qualitative example — no transcript pair, no spectrogram, no waveform, no worked case of what "looping, truncating, or losing count" actually looks like — despite opening on a vivid, specific failure ("six hundred sixty-six thousand six hundred sixty-six") that is never shown to occur, only inferred from aggregate count-error statistics. `[Evidence — cherry-picked/absent qualitative examples]`
   **Where** — Absent throughout; closest the paper gets is "a stratified 165-clip sample... ships with each clip's transcript and scoring decision, so a judgement can be checked by ear" (Section 2, "Code, data, settings"), which lives in an external repository, not the manuscript.
   **Why it matters** — This paper's central methodological argument in Section 2 is that the *choice of judge* can manufacture the very effect being measured (Whisper's autoregressive bias), and Section 3.2 spends a full paragraph defending against a specific artifact hypothesis (CTC blank-collapse merging adjacent identical words). That is exactly the situation where showing the reader one real example — target text, generated transcript, ĉ score, and ideally a spectrogram or a link to the actual audio — is not a nicety but the basic sanity check that lets a reader distinguish "the model genuinely dropped repetitions" from "the transcription pipeline collapsed them." A paper that argues so carefully about scorer artifacts and then presents zero raw instances anyone could point to and say "yes, that's a miscounted repetition" is asking for a great deal of trust on the one axis where trust is cheapest to earn and most conspicuously withheld. It also means every number in the abstract (18.2% vs 94.3%, 0.19 vs 1.00) is unfalsifiable from the manuscript alone — the reader has no way to eyeball even a single case and confirm the scoring rule (skip-unrecognized-tokens, non-overlapping match) behaves sensibly on real, messy ASR output.
   **What would address it** — Include, in the main text or a compact appendix figure, a handful of *randomly sampled* (not hand-picked) worked examples spanning both arms (repeated / control) and both judges (Whisper / CTC), showing the actual generated text, transcript, and score. Given the paper repeatedly worries about judge artifacts, this is the single cheapest and most convincing addition it could make.

### Minor concerns

- "V ALL-E 2" and "F5-TTS" have odd internal spacing throughout, consistent with a PDF-extraction artifact rather than an authorial choice, but it should be cleaned up for camera-ready.
- The "number-phrase" and "tongue-twister" stimulus families are introduced in Section 2 but never reported on individually anywhere in Section 3 — either drop them from the stimuli description or report results (even null ones) for them.
- Figure 1 caption doesn't state sample sizes per curve point or give any dispersion band on the lines themselves — "control medians sit at zero" is asserted but no spread is shown in the figure, only in text elsewhere.
- The Whisper-bias calibration in Section 2 is n=12 (six donors) — small for a claim that motivates discarding the field's default judge; the Spanish replication (S9, S27) helps but is also only referenced, not shown.
- "exact-match scores 30-of-32 and 3-of-32 alike" (Section 2) is a confusing aside with no clear referent — unclear which conditions produced these two numbers or why they're presented as "alike."
- The companion paper [8] (arXiv:2604.08591) is by the same three authors and unpublished/unreviewed as far as the reference list shows — fine as a pointer, but "the work is a companion to" in the intro leans on it for scope framing without saying what it does establish.

### Belief update

I came in with a strong prior that "TTS models fail on repeated phrases" was already common knowledge (it's cited as such in the intro), so the paper's real contribution is the *isolation* — repetition vs. length as separable causes, and the judge-choice methodology. On the strength of the internally-consistent Table 1 → Figure 1 arithmetic and the CTC-vs-Whisper contrast, I'll grant a modest update toward "the repetition-specific account is more likely than the pure-length account" for autoregressive TTS decoders — but only a modest one, because I can't independently verify the robustness claims that would turn "consistent with" into "differentiates from" (concern 3/4), and the paper's own ordering-ablation (concern 2) undercuts confidence in *why* repetition does this. I did not update at all on the mechanistic (Lean-verified contraction) story, since the paper itself refutes it — that's a negative result, honestly reported, but it leaves the "why" of the phenomenon exactly where it started: unexplained.

### Verdict

**Major revision** — the controlled design and the judge-artifact argument are worth publishing, but the manuscript currently asks the reader to take almost everything on faith: no qualitative example anywhere lets a reader verify the scoring pipeline on real output, which is the cheapest and most damaging gap given how much of the paper's own argument (Section 2, Section 3.2) is about judges manufacturing effects.

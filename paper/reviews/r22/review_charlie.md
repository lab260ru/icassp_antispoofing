### Summary

The paper makes three claims, in decreasing order of how much weight I'd put on them: (1) the well-documented "TTS can't count repetitions" failure tracks *periodicity*, not sequence length — demonstrated with a length-matched control design across six checkpoints/three architectures, with a period ladder showing the deficit survives even at period-2 where no token is self-adjacent; (2) the standard instrument for measuring this, Whisper, is itself compromised (autoregressive, LM-biased) and undercounts on ground-truth periodic audio, so a CTC recognizer is used instead; (3) the authors' own pre-registered mechanistic hypothesis — a Lean-4-verified bound in which counting fails if the per-repetition hidden-state map contracts — is measured directly via Jacobian power iteration and found false in every checkpoint tested (q always ≫ 1, expansive). This is an unusually self-critical, well-instrumented paper: the authors run their own specification curve, flag their own post-hoc threshold choice, and openly report a null result on their motivating theorem rather than quietly dropping it. The core weakness is that a paper whose entire subject matter is an *audible, qualitative* phenomenon (looping, truncation, miscounting) presents zero in-paper qualitative evidence — no transcript excerpt, no spectrogram, no worked example of the scoring algorithm in action — asking the reader to trust an automated, admittedly lenient matching procedure on the strength of prose description alone. Combined with a thin (n=2) non-autoregressive baseline set and small judge-validation samples, I land on minor revision: the central experiment is genuinely good, but a few holes need patching before the headline numbers can be taken at face value.

### Major concerns

1. **Issue** — The abstract advertises the periodicity-vs-length dissociation as essentially settled, but the Discussion admits an unaddressed confound (training-set frequency / OOD-ness of k-fold repeats) that could explain the same data. `[Narrative — overclaiming]`
   **Where** — Abstract ("The limit tracks periodicity, not length") vs. Section 5 ("Periodicity is not separated from training-set frequency: k-fold repetition is far rarer in speech corpora than a matched distinct-word sentence, and an out-of-distribution account predicts the same dissociation.")
   **Why it matters** — A reader who stops at the abstract (which is most readers) will conclude periodicity is the mechanism, when the authors' own text says an entirely different account — rarity in training data — predicts the identical result pattern and hasn't been excluded. That's exactly the "abstract claims more than the body earns" pattern.
   **What would address it** — One clause in the abstract flagging the frequency confound as open, matching the honesty already present in Section 5.

2. **Issue** — The frequency/OOD alternative explanation is *named* but never tested, despite being the most obvious rival to the paper's central claim. `[Evidence — alternative explanations]`
   **Where** — Section 5, final paragraph; no corresponding experiment anywhere in Section 3.
   **Why it matters** — If k-fold repeated spans are simply rarer in training corpora than length-matched distinct-word carriers, the "periodicity, not length" framing could really be "in-distribution-ness, not length" — a different and less mechanistically interesting claim (and one that wouldn't need a Lean-verified contraction bound to motivate it at all).
   **What would address it** — Even a crude proxy — token-repeat n-gram frequency in an available speech-adjacent text corpus, correlated against per-item error — would let the authors say whether frequency tracks the deficit as tightly as periodicity does. Absent that, the strongest honest claim is "periodicity or its correlate OOD-ness," not "periodicity."

3. **Issue** — The claim that the failure is "not peculiar to autoregressive decoding" rests on exactly two non-autoregressive architectures (VITS, F5-TTS), one from 2021. `[Evidence — baselines]`
   **Where** — Section 3.2, F5-TTS paragraph; "of two non-autoregressive baselines one is immune, one is not."
   **Why it matters** — n=2 is not enough to support a general claim about an entire decoding paradigm, and the paper's own proposed distinguishing variable ("where the count must live" — per-token duration vs. one global duration estimate) is inferred from a sample of exactly two data points, one confirming and one disconfirming per class. That's a plausible post-hoc story fit to two architectures, not a validated distinguishing test. Nothing rules out that VITS's immunity and F5-TTS's non-immunity both trace to something else entirely (model capacity, training data scale, alignment supervision strength).
   **What would address it** — At least one more NAR/flow-matching or diffusion TTS system (e.g., a more recent flow-matching or duration-predictor-based model) to see whether the "global vs. per-token duration budget" story holds up as a third data point, not just survives on two.

4. **Issue** — The audit that certifies the CTC-over-Whisper judge choice — arguably one of the paper's three headline contributions — is validated on n=12 and n=24 trials from just 6 donors of concatenative (spliced, not model-generated) audio. `[Statistical rigor]`
   **Where** — Section 2, "Behavioural measurement" paragraph.
   **Why it matters** — Every downstream number in the paper depends on this judge choice being correct. A sample this small, on synthetic splices rather than actual TTS output, is a fragile foundation for a load-bearing methodological claim, especially given the paper is otherwise careful about statistical rigor (bootstrap CIs, mixed-effects models, explicit p-value skepticism) everywhere else. Concatenative splices may not reproduce whatever acoustic artifacts (coarticulation, prosodic drift) real model-generated repeated speech has, so the audit may not transfer to the actual test conditions.
   **What would address it** — Report the audit at the same scale as the main study (hundreds of items, drawn from actual model outputs with independently verified ground truth), or at minimum give a CI on the 0.19 vs. 1.00 ratio rather than a bare point estimate from a dozen trials.

5. **Issue** — The paper studies an entirely audible, qualitative phenomenon (models "loop, truncate and lose count") and scores it with an automated, deliberately lenient matching algorithm (match target word, count non-overlapping occurrences in order, *skip* unrecognized tokens rather than stopping at the first miss) — yet not one example transcript, spectrogram, or waveform excerpt appears anywhere in the paper to let a reader see what "exactly right" versus "miscounted" actually sounds or reads like. `[Evidence — cherry-picked qualitative examples]`
   **Where** — Absent throughout; Section 2 describes the matching rule in prose only, Fig. 1 shows only aggregate curves, and the "stratified 165-clip sample... so a judgement can be checked by ear" lives outside the paper, in the GitHub repo.
   **Why it matters** — The skip-unrecognized-and-keep-counting rule is explicitly the kind of design choice that can quietly inflate "exact match" credit: if the CTC recognizer substitutes phonetically similar words (plausible, since the paper itself notes the CTC judge is "worse in absolute WER" than Whisper right in this exact setting), a generation could earn a correct count while the actual rendered word sequence is materially wrong, or vice versa a genuinely correct rendering could be mis-scored because a filler was dropped from the count in a control item. Nowhere does the paper show this isn't happening. This isn't a nitpick about missing decoration — it is the one thing a skeptical reader needs to trust before believing any of the 75-point gaps: that ĉ actually measures what the paper says it measures. A reader cannot verify this from the paper text alone; they must go audit an external repository the paper doesn't even guarantee will remain at the cited state.
   **What would address it** — Include, in the paper itself, at least 2-3 worked examples (transcript pairs, repeated vs. control, one exact match and one miscount) showing the raw recognizer output and how ĉ was computed from it. If figure budget is genuinely exhausted (this looks like a 4-page ICASSP submission), even a compact appendix table in the supplement — cited, not merely gestured at — would close the gap; right now the supplement reference (S10, S14) covers exclusion rates and disagreement statistics but the paper never states that a worked transcript example is included there either.

### Minor concerns

- Fig. 1(a) caption reads "one pair per checkpoint" without clarifying whether this is a single representative item pair or an aggregate (e.g., median) curve over the whole ladder for that checkpoint — as written it's genuinely ambiguous, and the more innocuous reading (aggregate) should be stated explicitly to avoid the appearance of a hand-picked illustrative pair.
- "P = 0.996" and "P = 0.956" (posterior-predictive partial-pooling section) are presumably Bayesian posterior probabilities, not frequentist p-values, but the paper never disambiguates this — worth a word given how careful the paper otherwise is about warning readers off p-value reasoning elsewhere.
- The Lean-4 framing ("free of sorry," "axiom audit") is fine as a factual description but reads as somewhat higher-status packaging than the underlying claim (an algebraic bound on attention-weight flattening) needs; a plain-language restatement in the main text, with Lean details fully deferred to supplement, would read less like a flex.
- "Qwen3-TTS the most stochastic family, so its larger checkpoint's null is not an artifact of conservative decoding" (Section 2, Code/data paragraph) is a dense, under-explained aside — the reasoning connecting "stochastic sampling defaults" to "the null result isn't due to conservative decoding" needs a clause, not a fragment.
- No mention of compute cost or how many GPU-hours the 2052+420-specification-curve+Jacobian-estimation pipeline required — not required for ICASSP but would help a reader judge replication cost.
- Limitations section is unusually honest but never states the elephant-in-the-room scope limit explicitly: three architecture families is a narrow slice of the current TTS landscape (no diffusion-based, no other major flow-matching or codec-LM systems beyond Llasa/Qwen3-TTS/XTTS), which matters given how much weight the partial-pooling extrapolation to "an architecture outside the panel" carries.

### Belief update

I came in skeptical that a "periodicity, not length" claim in TTS would survive scrutiny, given how easy it is to smuggle in a post-hoc-shaped story. Cross-checking against arXiv, the closest prior-art claim — Venkatesh [2605.09239], "representations decode the count correctly even where outputs fail" — checks out and is accurately characterized rather than oversold. The period-ladder result (deficit surviving at period-2, where no token touches itself) is a genuinely distinguishing test that a pure length/attention-span account can't explain, and I update moderately toward periodicity being *a* real driver of the failure — though not, per concern #2, to the exclusion of the frequency/OOD confound the authors themselves flag and leave untested. The mechanistic negative result (Lean-verified contraction premise measured and found false, q ≫ 1 everywhere including on the checkpoint that counts correctly) is the part I update on most: it's a clean, hard-to-fake instrumentation result, and it correctly kills a specific mechanistic story rather than vaguely gesturing at one. What I have not updated on is anything about robustness of the *measurement pipeline* itself (concern #5) — until the paper shows its scoring algorithm working on real examples, I'd treat the exact-match percentages as "probably roughly right" rather than "verified."

### Verdict

**Minor revision** — the central distinguishing experiment and the mechanistic refutation are solid and honestly reported, but the paper asks readers to trust a lenient, automated scoring pipeline for an inherently audible phenomenon without showing a single in-paper example of that pipeline at work, which is the load-bearing gap the rest of the paper's numbers rest on.

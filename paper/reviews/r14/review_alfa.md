### Summary

The paper makes three claims: (1) a Lean-4-verified theorem that *if* a decoder's per-repetition state update is approximately autonomous and contracting, *then* no Lipschitz readout can separate repetition counts past a computable horizon; (2) an empirical demonstration, across six AR TTS checkpoints, that repeated-phrase counting degrades sharply while length-matched non-repeating controls stay near-perfect (18.2% vs 94.3% exact-match) — the paper's genuinely strong result; and (3) an unusually candid admission that the theorem's own premise (contraction) was never established — two direct measurement attempts failed, and the companion attention-dilution lemma's dose-response prediction came out sign-reversed. The honesty here is real and rare, and I want to say that plainly before red-teaming it. But candor about failed premise-verification does not substitute for the ablations that would let a reader tell the theorem's mechanism apart from cheaper rivals (learned anti-repetition bias, training-data scarcity for repeated spans, codec/tokenization artifacts). The one place the paper directly tests its own distinctive prediction — a linear probe that should fail past the horizon — replicates in 1 of 2 checkpoints, and the paper reports this as a "failure to replicate" rather than downgrading the framing. The strong part of this paper is an empirical dissociation result; the theorem is a well-executed formal exercise whose connection to what's actually happening in these models remains unconfirmed by the paper's own account.

### Major concerns

1. **Issue** — The claim "not peculiar to autoregressive decoding" and the 50/50 framing ("of two non-autoregressive baselines, one is immune and one is not") rests on exactly two non-AR architectures, run at n=90 (three seeds) each, and explicitly excluded from the main statistical panel. `[Evidence — baselines]`
   **Where** — Abstract; §4.3, VITS/F5-TTS paragraphs.
   **Why it matters** — This is the sentence in the abstract most likely to get quoted as "the effect isn't really about autoregression," but it's an n=2 architecture comparison dressed as a general claim. A reader could easily walk away thinking the architectural question is settled two ways when it's genuinely unresolved with this much data.
   **What would address it** — Either soften the abstract claim to "in this small sample, one of two non-AR baselines showed the effect" or add more non-AR architectures (e.g., another flow-matching or diffusion TTS system) before generalizing.

2. **Issue** — The theorem's most direct empirical test — a linear probe that should fail to recover count past N* — is run on only 2 of the 6 checkpoints, succeeds in 1, and this "1 of 2" is treated as evidence rather than noise. `[Evidence — statistical rigour]`
   **Where** — §5, "Past the horizon the probe does test the theorem... a failure to replicate (S12)."
   **Why it matters** — n=2 is not a distinguishing test in either direction; a single flip in either checkpoint changes the reported ratio from 100% to 0%. The paper's own §4.2 explicitly refuses to draw conclusions from an n=6 panel ("too small for significance testing"), which makes it inconsistent to lean on an n=2 result for the theorem's own signature prediction, even framed honestly as a non-replication.
   **What would address it** — Explain concretely why the probe couldn't run on the other 4 checkpoints (compute, access to hidden states, something else), and if feasible, get to at least 4-5 checkpoints before calling this a test of the theorem at all.

3. **Issue** — No ablation isolates the theorem's mechanism (state contraction) from a much cheaper training-time explanation: models trained on corpora where verbatim repeated phrases are rare or actively discouraged (the paper itself analogizes to neural text degeneration and its objective-level fixes [5, 6] in the intro) would show the exact same periodicity-vs-length dissociation for reasons having nothing to do with contracting dynamics. `[Evidence — alternative explanations]`
   **Where** — §1 (the degeneration analogy is drawn but never operationalized as a rival to be ruled out); absent from §4.
   **Why it matters** — This is arguably the single most natural rival hypothesis, and the paper's introduction gestures at it ("the speech analogue of neural text degeneration... likewise sampling- or objective-level") without ever designing a test that would separate "learned dispreference for verbatim repetition" from "geometric loss of countability." Both predict the periodicity/length dissociation in §4.3; neither is ruled in or out by that experiment.
   **What would address it** — A checkpoint-level correlate (e.g., estimated frequency of k≥6 verbatim repeats in each model's training/fine-tuning data, if obtainable) or a controlled fine-tune on repetition-rich synthetic data to see if the deficit shrinks — either would start to separate a data/objective story from a dynamical one.

4. **Issue** — The paper's closing line, "Related bounds [15–19] bound a pass," is the entirety of its engagement with the transformer state-tracking literature, and it misses at least one directly relevant paper: Li, Guo & Andreas, "(How) Do Language Models Track State?" [2503.02854], which studies exactly the question of whether/how transformer LMs maintain recoverable state over a sequence of updates (there, permutation composition) — the same question this paper poses for repetition counts. `[Novelty]`
   **Where** — §5, final paragraph; Related work is otherwise folded into §1's two-sentence prior-art summary.
   **Why it matters** — The paper's contribution is specifically the Lean-verified *bound* plus the TTS instantiation, not the general idea that transformer state degrades — but a reader can't tell how much of the theoretical territory is already staked out by the state-tracking literature without this engagement. My arXiv search for TTS-specific formal-hallucination results turned up nothing that contradicts the "first... formal artifact" claim, so that specific claim survives; the state-tracking omission is a narrower but real gap.
   **What would address it** — A paragraph situating the contraction argument against existing state-tracking results (including the paper's own [19], barely mentioned) — what does formal verification add that empirical state-tracking studies don't, and does the mechanism here differ from theirs?

5. **Issue** — The abstract compresses roughly fifteen distinct quantitative claims, each individually hedged, into a single paragraph — technically accurate but functionally unreadable, which creates a caveat-laundering risk: a reader skimming for the headline ("Lean-verified bound... median count error of -8.3%... exact in 18.2%... against 94.3%") absorbs the numbers and loses the far more important qualifier three sentences later ("we do not establish the theorem's premise"). `[Narrative — overclaiming via density]`
   **Where** — Abstract, in full.
   **Why it matters** — Precision without legibility doesn't protect readers from overclaiming; it just moves the burden of noticing the hedges onto the reader's parsing stamina. The title does the honest framing correctly ("a horizon it does not yet explain"); the abstract's structure works against that title.
   **What would address it** — Restructure the abstract into two explicit tiers — what's proven/measured solidly vs. what's suggestive/unconfirmed — rather than one flat paragraph of qualifiers.

6. **Issue** — The theorem is sold on decoding-rule invariance as a selling point ("No property of the decoding rule enters, so the bound covers greedy, sampled and beam decoding alike") and Assumption 1 (a single time-invariant map F between repetition boundaries) is a *deterministic* assumption — yet every reported generation uses temperature/top-p sampling ("every checkpoint rendering every item at three seeds"; XTTS-v2 T=0.75/0.85, Llasa T=0.8/top-p 1.0, Qwen3-TTS T=0.9/1.0), and no experiment ever compares greedy against sampled decoding on the actual counting task. `[Evidence — missing ablations]`
   **Where** — §2 (theorem statement and its "no property of the decoding rule enters" claim); §3, sampling settings; absent from §4/§5.
   **Why it matters** — This is the cheapest ablation the paper could have run and the one most directly relevant to its own premise: stochastic token choice is exactly the kind of per-step perturbation that would make the boundary-to-boundary map *not* autonomous, since what gets sampled at repetition m changes what conditions repetition m+1. The repetition-penalty sweep (S15) tests a different confound (explicit anti-repeat suppression) and doesn't substitute for a greedy-vs-sampled comparison. Without it, "the bound covers... decoding alike" is an unexercised theoretical claim sitting next to an entirely sampled experimental panel, and the one place stochasticity is invoked at all is to explain away an inconvenient result ("making Qwen3-TTS the most stochastic family, so its larger checkpoint's null is not an artifact of conservative decoding") — an assertion, not a test.
   **What would address it** — Add a greedy-decoding condition (at least on the two smallest checkpoints, where it's cheapest) and report whether the horizon location or the periodicity/length gap moves. If greedy decoding sharpens the effect, that's actual support for the contraction story over a sampling-noise story; if it doesn't, the decoding-invariance claim needs to be dropped or qualified.

### Minor concerns

- "Every word- and sentence-repetition item at k ≥ 2 has a length-matched control... fillers from a per-template pool of eight, cycled above k = 8" — the paper itself flags and partially addresses this confound (§4.3, the 146-word-pool re-run), but the fix is reported for only k=12...32 and only as a 13.4-point adjustment; it's not clear this re-run used the same models/seeds as the main panel.
- Table 1's "capacity gain dNeff/d log k" column and the "ratio" column don't obviously match the "factor 0.46" quoted in §4.4 text (individual ratios range 0.21–0.80) — worth a sentence reconciling the per-checkpoint ratios with the single pooled "0.46" figure.
- p = 0.12 for the perturbation probe (7/18 pairs) is treated appropriately as inconclusive rather than as evidence of no effect, but "its linearity control failing (S5)" is asserted without any detail in the main text about what that control was or how it failed — hard to evaluate from the paper alone.
- The Whisper-vs-CTC judge audit (0.27–0.65 vs 1.00 counted/true ratio) is a good calibration check, but only one CTC model (wav2vec2-large-960h-lv60-self) is tried; a second CTC or non-autoregressive ASR system would strengthen confidence that CTC-judge bias itself isn't doing residual work.
- Figure 1's caption text as extracted is partly garbled ("22 25 / k / 100 / 0 / count error"), which may be a text-extraction artifact rather than an authoring issue, but as delivered the figure isn't fully legible from the text alone — worth double-checking axis labels/units survive to the camera-ready PDF.
- The paper leans on ~10 supplementary sections (S5, S8, S10–S18) for essentially every statistical and ablation detail (the penalty sweep, the duration-oracle test, the significance-testing caveats, the exclusion-rate breakdown); for a 4-page ICASSP paper this may be unavoidable, but it means the main text alone is not self-contained for assessing several of the concerns above.
- No multilingual test is reported despite XTTS-v2 being explicitly multilingual and Qwen3-TTS likely supporting multiple languages; the stimuli (repetition ladder, tongue twisters, number phrases) read as English-only, and this isn't flagged as a scope limitation anywhere.
- All six checkpoints top out at 8B parameters; no discussion of whether the effect is expected to shrink, persist, or change character at the scale of current frontier TTS/audio-LM systems.

### Belief update

I update meaningfully on one narrow, well-supported proposition: in these six checkpoints, periodic repeated-phrase text causes a counting deficit that length-matched non-periodic text of the same word count does not — the §4.3 dissociation (94.3% vs 18.2% exact match, holding across every model and every k) is a real distinguishing test and the strongest thing in the paper. I do not update, or update only slightly, on the theorem's specific mechanism (autonomous contracting map ⇒ readout horizon) actually explaining that deficit in these systems: the paper's own premise-verification attempts failed, the companion lemma's predicted dose-response is reversed, and the one direct test of the theorem's signature prediction (probe recovery past the horizon) succeeds in only 1 of 2 checkpoints tested. The formal-verification contribution (Lean 4, no `sorry`, clean axiom audit) is a solid, checkable artifact for the conditional implication it proves — that part I take at face value — but "we proved X ⇒ Y in Lean" is a different, and much smaller, update than "we showed X holds of real TTS decoders," and the paper is honest enough to say so itself in the Discussion even where its abstract doesn't quite carry that caution through.

### Verdict

**Major revision** — the core empirical dissociation (§4.3) is a real and useful finding worth publishing, but the paper needs the decoding-mode ablation (greedy vs. sampled) that its own theorem's central selling point depends on, plus a materially softened claim about non-autoregressive immunity currently built on n=2 architectures, before the theoretical framing is warranted at its current prominence.

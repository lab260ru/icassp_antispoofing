# Peer Review — Reviewer bravo

SEED: `8f3ac91be04d76b2f9c6e1d0b5a3f8e4`
LENS: External validity (population coverage, distribution shift, generalisation limits)
STANCE: Adversarial (assume the headline result is false; what would falsify it?)

## Summary

The paper asks whether autoregressive TTS models' well-documented tendency to lose count, loop, or truncate on repeated text is driven by text length or by repetition per se, and isolates the two with a "repetition ladder": 180 stimuli in which a target word recurs k times, each paired with a length-matched control where the k copies are replaced by distinct filler words from a small per-template pool. Across six checkpoints spanning three architecture families (Llasa, Qwen3-TTS, XTTS-v2), scored with a CTC ASR judge validated against an autoregressive Whisper judge shown to be itself biased by the phenomenon, the paper reports a large, robust exact-match gap (repeated vs. control) that survives greedy decoding, a repetition-penalty sweep, exclusion-rule ablations, and rescoring with four different recognisers. A second "period ladder" shows the deficit is graded with periodicity rather than requiring verbatim adjacency. A companion Lean-4-verified formal bound predicting that a contracting per-repetition state map would explain the ceiling is then empirically tested and rejected (measured contraction factors q ≫ 1 on all checkpoints measured), leaving the mechanism open. The headline claim is that the TTS counting failure is a repetition effect, not a length effect, and that its previously proposed formal explanation is false.

## Major concerns

1. **Issue** — The demonstrated "repetition, not length" effect is established on a narrow, templated, mostly-English, mid-scale-model population and the one deployed system purpose-built to mitigate exactly this failure (VALL-E 2's repetition-aware sampling) is explicitly excluded, so the paper's generality claim outruns its sampled population.
   **Where** — Method/Stimuli ("A parametric repetition ladder of 180 items... carrier sentences... fillers from a per-template pool of eight"); Models paragraph (six checkpoints, three families, all 0.4B–8B); Introduction ("VALL-E 2's repetition-aware sampling [5] we did not test"); Discussion limits ("The panel is three families and the Spanish arm needs a better judge").
   **Why it matters** — The Abstract and title assert a general property of "neural text-to-speech" models. But every axis along which real deployments differ from the tested population is either unexamined or explicitly excluded: no coverage of non-word-segmented or tonal languages (only English plus a small, self-admittedly under-instrumented Spanish spot check), no coverage of production-scale commercial systems, and — most importantly — no test of the field's actual state-of-the-art countermeasure for this exact failure mode. A reader cannot tell whether repetition-aware sampling closes the gap the paper reports, which is precisely the question a claim this broad needs to answer.
   **What would address it** — Run at least one checkpoint with VALL-E 2-style repetition-aware sampling (or any comparably purpose-built mitigation) through the same ladder; extend the period/repetition ladder to at least one non-Latin-script or non-word-segmented language with a validated judge for that language; explicitly scope the Abstract's claims to the tested population rather than to "text-to-speech models" generically.

2. **Issue** — The stimuli ("say a word 32 times in a row") are themselves far outside the distribution TTS systems are trained on, so the observed deficit may be a distribution-shift / out-of-instruction artifact rather than evidence of a general counting limitation, and the paper never tests a naturalistically-occurring level or form of repetition to rule this out.
   **Where** — Method/Stimuli; Fig. 1(a)–(b), where all reported k range up to 32 and all reported p range up to 8, entirely synthetic constructions.
   **Why it matters** — Under an adversarial reading, the headline result could be falsified by showing the gap collapses (or is far smaller) for the kind of repetition that plausibly occurs in real text — e.g., a name repeated 2–4 times for emphasis in a natural sentence, a repeated digit in a phone number, or a chant/refrain in natural prose — as opposed to the artificial "recite this word k times" carrier templates used throughout. If the deficit is largely an OOD-prompt effect, the paper's causal story ("repetition, not length") would still be technically correct but its practical implication ("models loop, truncate and lose count on text that repeats a phrase many times," Abstract) would overstate how often this actually bites in deployment, where such extreme literal-repetition prompts are rare.
   **What would address it** — Include a condition with naturally-occurring repeated content (real sentences/documents sampled from a corpus, filtered for repeated tokens/phrases at varying k) alongside the templated ladder, and report whether the repeated-vs-control gap persists at comparable magnitude.

3. **Issue** — The formal-mechanism refutation (Section 4) is measured on "five checkpoints and two families," not the full six-checkpoint, three-family panel used for the main effect, yet the paper states the conclusion in panel-wide language ("q = 21.0–347.7... it never described any decoder we measured").
   **Where** — Section 4, "Measuring q" ("Across five checkpoints and two families, q runs 21.0–347.7"), versus Section 3's six-checkpoint/three-family panel.
   **Why it matters** — The wording "never described any decoder we measured" is true only of the subset actually measured; readers skimming the Abstract ("we measure that premise across five checkpoints... and reject it... it never described any decoder we measured") could reasonably infer the refutation covers the whole panel, including whichever checkpoint/family was left out. Given the paper's own finding that q varies substantially with scale within a family (38, 119, 348 for Llasa 1B/3B/8B), the omitted family/checkpoint is not obviously interchangeable with the ones measured.
   **What would address it** — State explicitly which checkpoint/family was excluded from the q measurement and why, and either extend the measurement to the full panel or scope the refutation claim to the five checkpoints actually tested.

4. **Issue** — CosyVoice 2 is used as the paper's sole external/held-out generalisation test (Section 3.1's pre-registered extrapolation check) but is never introduced in the Method's Models paragraph — no checkpoint version, config, decoding settings, or inclusion rationale are given anywhere in the main text.
   **Where** — Section 3.1 ("CosyVoice 2, whose alignment supervision makes it the design most likely to be immune, renders repeated text exactly 28.9% against 93.3%... n = 90"); absent from the Models paragraph in Section 2, which lists only Llasa, Qwen3-TTS, XTTS-v2, VITS, F5-TTS.
   **Why it matters** — This is the one data point doing the work of validating the mixed-effects model's out-of-panel extrapolation (posterior predictive gap 65.3 [5.9, 93.3]), and the paper leans on it heavily ("from a fit committed before the checkpoint's first waveform existed"). Without disclosed settings (which checkpoint size/version, default vs. altered repetition penalty, sampling seed policy) a reader cannot judge whether this test was run under comparable conditions to the panel, nor can it be independently reproduced from the text alone.
   **What would address it** — Add CosyVoice 2 to the Models paragraph with full configuration detail, consistent with the level of disclosure given to the other five systems.

5. **Issue** — The claim that the counting failure is "not peculiar to autoregressive decoding" rests on exactly two non-autoregressive models (VITS, F5-TTS), one checkpoint each, and is stated in the Abstract with more confidence than the in-text hedge supports.
   **Where** — Abstract ("Nor is the failure peculiar to autoregressive decoding: of two non-autoregressive baselines one is immune, one is not, though at one model per strategy that is suggestive rather than isolating"); Section 3.2 (VITS "no dissociation," F5-TTS "does show it, +60.0").
   **Why it matters** — With n = 1 model per non-AR strategy, "one is immune, one is not" is equally consistent with an idiosyncratic property of the specific checkpoints tested (e.g., F5-TTS's single global duration estimate vs. VITS's per-token duration, which the paper itself proposes as the explanatory variable) as with a general claim about non-autoregressive decoding writ large. The Abstract sentence reads as a substantive finding about a decoding paradigm; the evidence supports only a two-model case study.
   **What would address it** — Either add a second checkpoint per non-AR strategy (ideally another duration-per-token model and another single-duration-estimate model) to test whether the VITS/F5-TTS split tracks the proposed duration-allocation mechanism rather than model identity, or rephrase the Abstract to match the in-text hedge.

## Minor concerns

- The 420-specification robustness "curve" (Section 3.1) varies researcher-chosen axes with no correction and is explicitly not treated as independent hypothesis tests ("we correct across none"); a large specification count does not by itself establish robustness when the axes and their ranges are themselves researcher-selected — see the critique of multiverse-style robustness reporting in [2607.14623].
- The rank-1 patch causal intervention (Discussion, "The rival we could not decide") draws directional conclusions from n = 36, explicitly noted as "bounding only 19%" — likely underpowered to support the stated interpretation.
- Pre-registration evidence relies on the authors' own git commit hashes in a self-hosted repository ("commit 6f34945, checkable rather than asserted") rather than a third-party timestamping service (OSF, AsPredicted); self-hosted history is more easily amended (force-push, rebase) than an independent registry, weakening the evidentiary force of "checkable rather than asserted."
- It is not always clear, statistic by statistic, whether a reported gap (e.g., 75.4, 76.7-by-checkpoint in the Abstract) uses cycled or never-cycled control fillers; only one number (67.6) is explicitly flagged as the never-cycled variant, leaving the periodicity status of most headline figures ambiguous to the reader.
- The judge-validation audit sample is very small (n = 12 for Whisper, n = 24 for the CTC judge, "6 donors") and built from concatenative (spliced) audio rather than actual model-generated repeated speech, which may not reproduce the co-articulation/prosodic artifacts of genuine TTS failures that the judge must actually score.
- Considerable machinery (Lean 4/mathlib formalisation) is devoted to a mechanism the paper itself empirically rejects; while intellectually honest, the framing could more efficiently foreground the negative result rather than building it up as a candidate explanation across most of Sections 1 and 4.
- Self-citation: the "companion" paper [8] shares all three authors; not disqualifying, but worth a conflict-of-interest note given how much interpretive weight is placed on it in the Introduction and Discussion (e.g., "the work is a companion to...").

## Verdict

**Major revision** — the core repetition-vs-length manipulation is well-motivated and unusually self-critical about its own confounds, but the generality of its claims (population/language/model coverage, exclusion of the field's purpose-built mitigation, and an untested distribution-shift alternative explanation for the templated stimuli) is not yet earned by the evidence presented.

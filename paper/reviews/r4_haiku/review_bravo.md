## Summary

Autoregressive text-to-speech models fail to accurately reproduce repeated phrases, often undercounting repetitions. Viakhirev et al. attribute this to a structural property: when decoder states contract under the periodic conditioning of repeated text, no Lipschitz readout—including the model's own stop head—can distinguish repetition counts beyond a finite horizon N* = log(µ/2LC) / log q. The authors formalize this claim (Theorem 1) with a machine-checked proof in Lean 4 and measure the prediction empirically across six checkpoints by comparing count errors on repeated text vs. length-matched controls with distinct words, finding a median −8.3% error on repetitions against 0.0% on controls, with saturating effective rank of state trajectories.

## Major Concerns

1. **Critical confound: CTC recognizer itself exhibits repetition-dependent bias, undermining behavioral measurement**
   - **Where:** Section 3 (Behavioural measurement), Table 1, Figure 1.
   - **Why it matters:** The paper *correctly* identifies that Whisper (autoregressive decoder) is biased against repetition (scoring 0.27–0.65 on repetitions vs. 1.00 on distinct words) and switches to CTC. However, the proposed alternative—wav2vec2-large-960h—is an acoustic model pre-trained on general speech, not optimized for this task. The paper claims CTC "counts at ratio 1.00 for k ≥ 4 on periodic ground truth" when audited against "concatenative audio with known ground truth," but this audit is not shown: no ground-truth concatenative sample is provided, no per-k breakdown of the CTC audit is reported, and "ground truth" is not defined. The control comparison (CTC scoring 1.00 on 32 distinct words, same length) is not the same as testing CTC on artificially repeated speech. The paper has simply swapped one latent bias for another without proving the replacement is unbiased.
   - **What would address it:** (i) Publish the ground-truth concatenative samples and per-k CTC accuracy on them. (ii) Verify CTC on synthetically generated repetitions (e.g., copies of a single word from an external TTS known to produce correct repetitions). (iii) Compare CTC output length to nominal expected length on the control condition to verify it is not systematically miscounting even when producing 0.0% error. (iv) Report confidence intervals on the 1.00 figure for k ≥ 4 — if based on ~600 generations (n=639), what is the CI width?

2. **Experimental design fails to isolate contraction from alternative mechanisms: attention dilution is assumed, not established**
   - **Where:** Assumption 1, Lemma 1 (Attention dilution), Section 4.3 (attention analysis).
   - **Why it matters:** The theorem assumes a single autonomous map F because repeated spans receive uniform attention (Lemma 1). But Lemma 1 is a bound on *softmax weights* given logit differences δ ≤ δ_bound. The paper measures empirically that attention is ~uniform over the repeated spans (entropy grows with log k at slope 0.97, "no occurrence captures more than 1.9/k"). This confirms Lemma 1's conclusion but not its premise for *this* model. The paper does not report δ for the studied models—it cannot, because text-attention logits are not exposed in most checkpoints. So Assumption 1 (periodic conditioning via uniform attention) is *unverified* for the very models on which the theorem's consequence is claimed. The theorem's hypothesis is tested indirectly via its conclusion, which is circular: if states saturate, we assume Assumption 1 holds; if states saturate, we believe Assumption 1 holds. The paper has designed the experiment to confirm its assumptions, not to test them independently.
   - **What would address it:** (i) For checkpoints where text-attention is accessible (Llasa, XTTS-v2), compute and report per-model δ bounds directly. (ii) Independently verify Assumption 1 (periodic conditioning) by perturbing the text slightly (typos, word-order swaps) and measuring whether the state trajectory changes; if conditioning is truly periodic, small text perturbations should have large effects. (iii) Test Assumption 2 (contraction) directly on boundary states rather than inferring it from capacity loss; the paper claims this failed (Section 4.4), but a failed direct test is weaker than a circular indirect confirmation.

3. **State saturation (Figure 1c,d) is confounded by output monotony—controls may saturate differently because they produce genuinely lower-rank acoustic content**
   - **Where:** Section 4.3, Figure 1(c,d), capacity gain controls.
   - **Why it matters:** The paper argues that repeated-text state trajectories have lower effective rank (Neff) because the decoder loses capacity to track the count. The control (distinct words) has higher Neff. But the paper then pre-emptively addresses an objection: "correctly rendering thirty-two times really does produce monotonous audio, so a low-rank trajectory might follow from the stimulus." The proposed control is adding "output diversity" as a covariate (type/token ratio, adjacent-repeat rate, length). However, this is *post-hoc* covariate adjustment, not pre-registered experimental control. The baseline claim is that audios of repeated words are genuinely lower-rank (fewer distinct acoustic events), so lower Neff is the *correct* outcome, not a confound. The paper does not compare the *acoustic rank* of repeated vs. control outputs to show they are equivalent; it only controls statistically. For a paper claiming a structural impossibility, statistical post-hoc adjustment is insufficient—the experimental design should make the comparison fair at the stimulus level.
   - **What would address it:** (i) Explicitly design stimuli so that repeated-text audio and distinct-word audio have matched acoustic rank (e.g., by using diverse phonetic contexts or synthetic manipulation). (ii) Show side-by-side spectrograms or acoustic feature PCA to verify visually that rank is equated. (iii) Alternatively, reframe the finding as "repeated text has lower acoustic rank, which correlates with lower state rank" rather than claiming state rank is lost independent of output properties.

4. **"Periodic conditioning" assumption tested only via its conclusion, not via direct perturbation or alternative design**
   - **Where:** Assumption 1, Section 4.2 (controls), Section 4.3 (periodicity vs. length).
   - **Why it matters:** The paper claims the deficit depends on "periodicity, not length" (Section 4.2). The control is length-matched but with distinct words. This is strong, but it does *not* establish that periodicity alone is the operative factor if you vary other dimensions (e.g., repetition *timing*, silence gaps between repetitions, or phonetic variety within the repeated phrase). A language-model prior (which LLMs have) might suppress repetition not due to Assumption 1 (periodic conditioning → uniform attention) but due to a learned distributional bias: repeated short sequences are rarer in training data. The paper mentions this briefly (Section 5: "Were improbability the cause, controls would be the more natural member…"), but dismisses it without controlling for it experimentally. The post-hoc argument is: under a held-out LM, controls are *less* probable in 87% of pairs. But this does not rule out that the TTS model's own embedded language prior (if it has one) learned a different distribution. And the paper does not test whether fine-tuning on repeated-text data removes the deficit.
   - **What would address it:** (i) Evaluate the paper's own LM prior on the stimuli and report statistics per-model, not globally. (ii) Include a model ablation: train a variant with synthetic repeated-text augmentation and test whether the deficit shrinks. (iii) Perturb repetitions in ways that keep them periodic but vary improbability (e.g., repeating a high-probability vs. low-probability word) and measure whether the deficit varies.

5. **Measurement of contraction factor q failed (Section 4.4), yet the theorem's bound depends critically on it; consequently the bound is unfalsifiable**
   - **Where:** Section 4.4 (Two things we could not establish), Theorem 1 equation (1).
   - **Why it matters:** Theorem 1(iv) claims m ≤ log(µ/2LC) / log q. This bound is useless without q. The paper states it "needs a monotone text read-head" and "these decoders' deep-layer attention centroid advances on barely half of generation steps." This is a design problem with the models, not a limitation of the theory—but it means the paper cannot test whether the bound's *magnitude* matches empirical counts. Instead, the paper only tests the bound's *consequence* (state saturation). But saturation is a proxy that many mechanisms could produce. Without q, the paper cannot rule out that the bound is off by orders of magnitude, or that the contraction actually operates on a different timescale than claimed. The theorem is formally verified, but the premise (Assumption 2: contraction) is neither directly measured nor pre-registered; the finding is inferred post-hoc from consequences.
   - **What would address it:** (i) Redesign the experiment to directly measure q: use a model with explicit text position tracking or train an instrumented variant. (ii) Fit the empirical undercounting to the formula µ ≤ 2LCq^m and solve for q post-hoc, reporting CI on the fit. (iii) Alternatively, acknowledge that the theorem is a consistency check, not a predictive model, and reframe the paper as "state saturation under repetition is consistent with contraction dynamics" rather than "we prove contraction causes undercounting."

## Minor Concerns

- **Qwen3-TTS-1.7B violates the theorem yet is included in the panel without explanatory discussion.** The paper states (Section 4.1) that this model shows 0.0% error at k ≥ 6, which contradicts the theorem's prediction *if* its per-repetition map contracts. The paper says "we report it as a distinct regime rather than a failed prediction" (Section 4.1, also Discussion). But the panel summary (Table 1) pools results across models, reporting a median of −8.3% across five models that show the deficit. This pooling is misleading: the theorem predicts a specific direction and magnitude of error, and one of six checkpoints is a null result. The text should clearly state "5 of 6 models show a deficit consistent with the theorem; 1 of 6 does not" and report separate statistics for the two regimes.

- **Bootstrap intervals (95%) are reported but the resampling unit is not specified.** Figure 1(d) and Table 1 show "bootstrap intervals over stimulus templates." Does this resample over (a) repetitions k within each template? (b) template types (sentence, word, phrase)? (c) random seeds (three per stimulus)? The confidence width varies across models (e.g., Llasa-1B ~[22,22], Llasa-3B ~[15,15] in Table 1), suggesting small effective sample size; if resampling is only over ~6 templates, the CI may be overstated.

- **The attention analysis (Lemma 1 coverage in Section 4.3) applies only to 4 of 6 checkpoints.** Qwen3-TTS "sums text and acoustic embeddings per position, so no attention column belongs to the text and the lemma cannot be checked there." This is correct but leaves 33% of the panel without direct verification of the theorem's premise. The relative importance of these checkpoints (0.6B and 1.7B, representing the largest and one of the smallest models) is not discussed.

- **Missing ablations: does repetition-penalty = 1 (disabling the penalty) truly "break the control too"?** Section 5 reports that XTTS-v2 with penalty disabled moves the deficit from −12.5% to −16.1% but also breaks the control. The paper does not show the control accuracy at penalty = 1. If the control remains at 0.0%, the claim is wrong; if it degradates, by how much? This deserves a table.

- **Figure 1 axis labels are unclear.** Panel (a) shows "21 23 25 repetitions k" on the x-axis for k ∈ {1,2,3,4,6,8,12,16,24,32} — it plots only k ≥ 21? The caption says "against k" but the range is truncated. Panels (c) and (d) use abbreviated model names that are hard to parse (Llasa-1B/3B/8B vs. Qwen3-TTS-0.6B/1.7B have different naming conventions).

- **Code and data availability claim needs verification.** The paper states "Everything … is at https://github.com/lab260ru/icassp_antispoofing." This URL pattern suggests an internal lab repo; public accessibility is not confirmed. If the repo is private, the claim of reproducibility is vacuous. The transcript sets (Whisper and CTC output) are said to be "released" but no DOI or persistent link is given.

- **Lean 4 formalization scope is narrower than the headline.** The paper claims "the first hallucination result in speech synthesis carried out as a formal artifact," but the Lean proof covers only the mathematical implication (periodic conditioning + contraction → finite horizon) and Lemma 1. It does not formalize any claim about *this particular TTS model* instantiating those conditions; that part is entirely empirical and informal. The marketing of "formally verified" should be qualified: the implication is verified, the premises are not.

- **Novogradients/mitigation section (Section 5) is underdeveloped.** The paper tests repetition-penalty = {2,3,5,8} on XTTS-v2 and concludes the lever "does not act on the quantity that sets the horizon." But the paper does not test other proposed mitigations [3,4] (RALL-E, CosyVoice 2 alignment strategies). For a paper claiming to address "existing mitigations," benchmarking only one is insufficient.

## Verdict

**Major revision**

The paper makes a bold and interesting claim: repetition-counting failure in TTS is a structural consequence of contracting decoder dynamics, not a sampling accident. However, the experimental design conflates multiple mechanisms and does not cleanly isolate the proposed cause from alternatives. (1) The choice of CTC recognizer is justified against Whisper but not verified to be unbiased on the task; the ground-truth audit is absent. (2) The theorem's premises (Assumption 1: periodic conditioning; Assumption 2: contraction) are tested indirectly via their consequences, not independently, creating a circular confirmation loop. (3) State saturation is confounded with output acoustic properties, and the statistical post-hoc control is weak evidence for a structural claim. (4) The contraction factor q cannot be measured, so the bound's magnitude is unfalsifiable. (5) One of six checkpoints violates the prediction without clear model-level explanation. For a paper centered on formal verification and claiming an impossibility result, the empirical evidence for the theorem's premises is too weak. The work should either (a) redesign experiments to directly measure the premises and isolate periodicity/contraction from confounds, or (b) reframe as a consistency study ("this pattern is consistent with contraction") rather than a proof of causation. In its current form, the paper presents elegant theory and suggestive experiments, but does not sufficiently bridge the gap between them.

# Review (reviewer: bravo)

## Steelman, first

The strongest version of this paper's case is genuinely interesting: repetition-induced failure in AR TTS is not just "exposure bias, fix it with attention supervision" hand-waving — it's a consequence of a *provable* fact (contracting autonomous maps erase state) conjoined with an *empirically testable* premise (near-uniform attention over repeated spans, Lemma 1) and an *empirically observed* consequence (undercounting that tracks periodicity, not length). The periodicity-vs-length control (Fig. 1b) is a legitimately sharp design — if I had to name the single best thing in the paper, it's that a k-word control and a k-word repetition are matched on everything except identity of the tokens, and the control comes out at 0.0% error at every k up to 32 while the repeated twin degrades. That's a real distinguishing test against the "it's just length/context dilution" account, and it's better evidence than most papers in this space bother to produce. I'll grant all of that. Now I'll press on it.

## Summary

The paper claims (1) an AR decoder that treats k repeated spans as approximately interchangeable induces an approximately autonomous per-repetition state map, and if that map contracts, a finite counting horizon follows as a theorem (machine-checked in Lean 4); (2) empirically, six TTS checkpoints undercount repeated content (median -8.3% at k≥6) while an exactly length-matched non-repeated control is essentially error-free at every k; and (3) a proxy for "distinguishable internal states" (effective rank of the hidden-state trajectory) grows more slowly under repetition than under the control, at roughly half the rate, which the authors read as the theorem's consequence showing up directly in activations. The theorem itself is a clean, honestly conditional statement, and the authors are unusually forthright that they never verify its premise (contraction) directly — commendable. But the paper's discussion section then reaches for a much broader practical claim ("existing mitigations... help only insofar as they raise q or enlarge the readout margin") on the strength of a single narrow test: sweeping one hyperparameter (repetition penalty) on one model (XTTS-v2). That's a weak-baseline problem sitting directly underneath the paper's most actionable claim, and it compounds with a real prior-art gap (the mainstream neural-text-degeneration/repetition-looping literature is absent) and an unverified theorem antecedent. My overall stance: real, careful measurement work, wrapped in a formalization that's better tested for what it *doesn't* claim than what it does, but the paper's rhetorical reach (especially in the Discussion) outruns what was actually tested.

## Major concerns

1. **Issue** — The theorem's operative premise (Assumption 2, contraction) is never measured; the paper substitutes two downstream correlates for it, but the abstract's framing ("The theorem's consequence is directly observable") invites the reader to treat the mechanism as established rather than merely consistent with the data. `[Narrative — overclaiming]`
   **Where** — Abstract; Section 4.4 ("We could not measure q itself... The failure is self-defeating"); Section 5 ("What we establish empirically is the theorem's consequence, not its antecedent").
   **Why it matters** — Undercounting-that-tracks-periodicity and slower effective-rank growth are each consistent with *many* mechanisms besides state-space contraction toward a fixed point (e.g., a learned duration/stopping prior keyed on n-gram repetition frequency in training data — see concern 4). The paper's own Section 5 admission is honest, but it sits three pages after an abstract that reads as if the causal chain (contraction → erasure → bounded counting) is confirmed rather than merely not-yet-refuted.
   **What would address it** — Foreground the conditional nature in the abstract itself (not just in Discussion), or find a genuine proxy for contraction that isn't confounded by the same attention-localization failure that sank the direct boundary-distance measurement.

2. **Issue** — The paper omits the mainstream neural-text-degeneration/repetition-looping literature, which describes essentially the same phenomenon (AR models collapsing into repetitive loops or truncation under likelihood-maximizing decoding) outside TTS, and offers a competing data-driven explanation the paper never engages. `[Novelty]`
   **Where** — Introduction and Discussion's related-work paragraph (which cites [9]–[15] but not this literature).
   **Why it matters** — Holtzman et al., "The Curious Case of Neural Text Degeneration" [1904.09751], established that likelihood-based decoding drives repetitive collapse in general AR language models years before this paper's TTS framing; Xu et al., "Learning to Break the Loop" [2206.02369], analyzes sentence-level repetition loops directly; Li et al., "Repetition In Repetition Out" [2310.10226], argues degeneration correlates with repetition statistics in *training data* — a rival explanation to "structural contraction in the decoder." None of these appear. The paper's claim to be "the autoregressive-decoder companion to a spectral study of hallucination in encoder–decoder ASR [5]" implicitly positions the attractor framing as originating within the authors' own research programme; the wider NLG literature suggests the phenomenon and even competing accounts of its cause predate that programme by years.
   **What would address it** — Cite and explicitly distinguish the theorem's claim from the data-frequency account; if the two are compatible (contraction could be *learned* because repeated spans are rare/penalized in training data), say so and it would strengthen, not weaken, the paper.

3. **Issue** — The paper's single pooled headline statistic is oddly reported and possibly misleading about what's being pooled. `[Evidence — statistical rigour]`
   **Where** — Abstract and Section 4.1: "repeated items are rendered with a median error of -8.3% [−12.5, −8.3] (n = 622)."
   **Why it matters** — The point estimate (-8.3%) sits at the *edge* of its own reported interval (-12.5 to -8.3) rather than inside it, which is unusual for a bootstrap CI around a median and is never explained. More importantly, this pooled figure blends five checkpoints with a genuine deficit (-8.3% to -16.7% individually, per Table 1) with Qwen3-TTS-1.7B, which the paper itself says shows "0.0% at k≥6... a distinct regime." A pooled number that folds in a model explicitly carved out as a non-instance of the effect risks reading as a more presentable, deficit-flavored "typical" number than any individual checkpoint's honest range. No per-model n breakdown is given for how 622 splits across six checkpoints, so a reader can't tell if the pooling is dominated by one or two models.
   **What would address it** — Report the pooled statistic excluding the acknowledged null-regime model (or report both), and clarify what interval this is (bootstrap CI of the median, IQR, something else) and why the point estimate falls at its boundary.

4. **Issue** — The leading rival explanation for repetition-induced degeneration in the wider literature — that it's a training-data-frequency effect rather than a dynamical/contraction effect — is never tested or discussed, despite being directly actionable with data the authors already have. `[Evidence — alternative explanations]`
   **Where** — Section 4.2's "two further alternatives" paragraph tests improbability and acoustic-horizon accounts, but not a frequency-in-training-data account; Discussion's related-work paragraph.
   **Why it matters** — If undercounting tracks how *rare* k-fold literal repetition is in TTS training corpora (very plausible — six-fold literal repetition of a word is not common in read-speech training sets) rather than tracking a contraction rate intrinsic to the architecture, the theorem could be true and formally sound while explaining a phenomenon that has a much more mundane primary cause. This is exactly the kind of alternative a "distinguishing test" section should rule out, and the paper already has the apparatus (per-item ladder, six checkpoints of different scale/training data) to at least gesture at it.
   **What would address it** — A correlation (even coarse) between corpus-estimated repetition frequency and per-model deficit size, or an explicit argument for why frequency effects can't explain the periodicity/length dissociation in Fig. 1b (the paper does have a good argument available here — distinct-word controls are *also* rare in training data as literal k-tuples — but it isn't made).

5. **Issue** — The paper's practical claim that "existing mitigations... do not act on the quantity that sets the horizon" is drawn from a single hyperparameter sweep on a single model, using a mitigation with known implementation pathologies, while a more relevant and more recent decoding-time TTS-hallucination mitigation exists in the literature and is neither tested nor cited. `[Evidence — baselines]`
   **Where** — Section 5, "Implications and limits": "sweeping XTTS-v2's repetition penalty — the most widely deployed such fix — over {2, 3, 5, 8}... The deficit moves only from -12.5% to -16.1%... slightly the wrong way... no setting both preserves general quality and removes the deficit."
   **Why it matters** — The introduction frames the field's response to this problem as spanning "attention supervision or decoding rules [3, 4]" (RALL-E, CosyVoice2) and cites alignment-guided reordering [2] and attention-guidance [1] as other proposed fixes — none of these is actually run. The entire "existing mitigations act late" claim rests on varying one coefficient of one heuristic on one 0.4B-parameter checkpoint (XTTS-v2), extrapolated in the Discussion to "existing mitigations" as a class. That's a strawman-by-omission: a decoding-time mitigation built specifically for LM-based TTS hallucination, "Experience-Calibrated Contrastive Decoding for Mitigating Hallucinations in LM-Based Text-to-Speech" [2608.00722], exists in the contemporary literature and was not tested or discussed, despite being a much closer match to what the paper claims to have ruled out ("decoding rules"). Worse, the specific lever tested — sign-branched repetition penalty — has a documented pathology: "Gauge dependence and structured-output corruption in sign-branched repetition penalties" [2607.09791] shows this exact mechanism branches on an arbitrary logit zero-point, producing implementation-dependent, non-monotonic behavior unrelated to any property of the underlying model. Given that the paper's own sweep result is a small, "slightly the wrong way" effect (-0.7 points per unit of penalty, a 3.6-point span), an unaddressed alternative explanation is sitting right there: the negative slope may reflect this known gauge artifact of the repetition-penalty implementation, not evidence that raising q or the readout margin is ineffective.
   **What would address it** — Either test a second, mechanistically distinct mitigation (e.g., an alignment-guided decoding rule, or the contrastive-decoding approach above) so "existing mitigations" isn't a claim about n=1, or narrow the Discussion's claim explicitly to "repetition penalty on XTTS-v2" rather than generalizing to the mitigation literature as a class.

## Minor concerns

- Reference [1] is cited in the introduction to support "the behaviour is well documented as a stability problem [1, 2]," but the reference list itself notes it was withdrawn 13 Feb 2026 — transparent of the authors to flag, but the introduction's framing doesn't caveat it, and one of two citations backing that opening claim is now a withdrawn preprint. `[Novelty / Presentation]`
- Table 1 reports point estimates only (count error, capacity gain, ratio) with no CI or error column, even though the text supplies bootstrap intervals for the same quantities elsewhere (Section 4.1, 4.3) — a reader using only the table can't tell which per-model differences are significant. `[Presentation]`
- The "improbability" alternative-hypothesis check ("under a causal LM outside the panel the control is the less probable member in 87% of 54 pairs") names no model, no CI, n=54, and is pushed to a supplement (S8) not included in the paper text — unauditable as presented. `[Reproducibility]`
- No human-validated transcription check appears anywhere: the CTC judge is audited against synthetic concatenative ground truth, never against a human-labeled sample of actual model outputs, despite the paper investing real effort in arguing the judge choice matters. `[Reproducibility]`
- "Deep probe layers" (Section 3, "State measurement") is never pinned to specific layer indices in the main text, and there's no ablation showing the effective-rank/capacity result is robust to probe-layer choice. `[Evidence — ablations]`
- The k≥6 threshold that anchors every headline number ("Below k=6 every model is essentially exact... From k=6 the panel splits") reads as chosen after inspecting where the panel splits, not pre-registered. `[Evidence — pre-registration]`
- No qualitative examples (spectrograms, transcripts, audio) are shown anywhere — this avoids cherry-picking risk, but it also means nothing in the paper lets a reader sanity-check what an "undercounted" or "collapsed" generation actually looks/sounds like. `[Evidence — cherry-picking: nothing found, but also nothing to check]`
- Dense phrasing in a few places states results without immediate intuition, e.g. "the number of distinguishable states a generation visits grows with k at a factor 0.50 of the control rate" — precise, but a plainer restatement ("half as many new distinguishable states per doubling of k") would help a general ICASSP audience.

## Belief update

I moved my credence up, modestly, on the narrower proposition "AR TTS repetition-counting failure tracks periodicity of the conditioning text specifically, not sequence length" — Fig. 1b is a clean, well-controlled result and I hadn't seen this dissociation demonstrated this cleanly before. I did *not* move on the mechanistic proposition the paper is actually titled around — "counting collapse is caused by state-space contraction toward a fixed point" — because the paper's own Section 4.4 concedes the central premise was never measured, and my reading of the evidence is that it's equally compatible with a training-frequency account the paper doesn't engage (concern 4). I also did not move on "existing decoding-time mitigations don't fix this" — that claim is carried by one hyperparameter on one model against a mechanism with documented artifacts, which isn't enough evidence to update on (concern 5).

## Verdict

**Major revision** — the core measurement (periodicity vs. length) is solid, but the Discussion's practical claim about mitigations rests on a single narrow, potentially confounded test standing in for "existing mitigations" as a class, while a more relevant contemporary decoding-time TTS mitigation goes uncited and untested; that gap needs to be closed (or the claim narrowed) before the paper's actionable conclusion can be trusted.

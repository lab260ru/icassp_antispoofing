### Summary

The paper claims three things: (1) TTS models that mishandle repeated text are failing on *repetition*, not on *length* — demonstrated via a length-matched control ladder where repeated-vs-control exact-render rates diverge sharply (18.2% vs 94.3%, headline gap 46.8–75.4 points depending on threshold); (2) the standard instrument for measuring this, Whisper, is itself biased in the direction of the effect and must be replaced with a CTC recogniser; and (3) a specific mechanistic explanation the authors formally proved sufficient (state-map contraction, verified in Lean 4) is empirically false on every checkpoint measured (q = 21–347.7, never < 1). The evidentiary base is broad — six checkpoints, three architectures, two non-AR baselines, four recognisers, a penalty sweep, an exclusion-rule sweep, and a mechanistic falsification — and the paper is unusually forthcoming about its own weak spots (it names its post-hoc threshold, withdraws a claim from an earlier draft, and reports two analyses "in text alone" rather than hiding them). That transparency is real and commendable. It does not, however, neutralise the core statistical problem: the paper's central number (75.4 points at k ≥ 6) is the output of a threshold chosen after the authors saw where the curves diverge, and the "420-specification robustness envelope" offered as compensation shows the effect's magnitude ranging 34.4–86.4 points — a >2.5× spread — while the paper's headline sits near the top of that range rather than at its median (70.5). Sign-robustness is not magnitude-robustness, and the paper conflates them in the abstract.

### Major concerns

1. **Issue** — The main manipulation (repeated vs. length-matched control) has only one true "distinguishing" comparison per checkpoint-family, and the mechanistic follow-up sections increasingly substitute robustness sweeps for a genuine second distinguishing test. `[Evidence — distinguishing test]`
   **Where** — Section 3.1, Fig. 1(a).
   **Why it matters** — A single repeated-vs-control contrast, however cleanly designed, is consistent with several candidate mechanisms (attention collapse, duration-model averaging, learned prosodic compression of repeated words from training data, or genuine "counting" failure). The paper doesn't design an experiment that discriminates among these until Section 4, where it tests only the contraction hypothesis — and finds it false. After that refutation, no experiment differentiates the remaining live hypotheses (state-erasure vs. readout-failure); the paper says as much ("The rival we could not decide") but still lets the abstract imply a resolved story ("repetition, not length").
   **What would address it** — A pre-registered second manipulation that separates "counting" from "prosodic compression of repeats" — e.g., synthesize with forced per-token durations (already partially attempted for F5-TTS in S13/S20) across the whole panel, not just one flow-matching model.

2. **Issue** — Alternative hypothesis never addressed: models may be learning a genuine prosodic/perceptual prior that real speech rarely contains many verbatim-identical adjacent or periodic tokens, and are "regularising" toward more natural-sounding output rather than failing to count. `[Evidence — alternative explanations]`
   **Where** — absent (Sections 3–5 discuss length, penalty, sampling, exclusion, recogniser and training-frequency confounds, but not this one).
   **Why it matters** — If the mechanism is a learned naturalness prior rather than a computational counting limit, the framing of the paper (a "counting failure," analysed via Lean-verified contraction bounds) targets the wrong level of explanation entirely, and the fix implied by the framing (better state-tracking) would not help.
   **What would address it** — A perceptual/naturalness rating on repeated vs. control renders at matched k might show whether the model's failures correlate with dispreferred renders being judged less natural by listeners, which would support a prosodic-prior account over a capacity-limit account.

3. **Issue** — Two of two audits used to disqualify Whisper as the judge and certify the CTC recogniser rest on very small samples (n = 12 for Whisper, n = 24 for CTC). `[Evidence — statistical rigour]`
   **Where** — Section 2, "Behavioural measurement."
   **Why it matters** — The choice of judge is treated by the authors themselves as "a result in its own right," and the entire downstream measurement pipeline depends on it. A ratio of 0.19 vs. 1.00 built on n = 12/24 trials, "over 6 donors," is not enough to rule out that the CTC recogniser also degrades in specific repeated-audio conditions not sampled by this tiny audit (e.g., specific phoneme classes, specific k, specific speakers).
   **What would address it** — Report the audit at the same scale as the main panel (thousands of trials, or at minimum a per-checkpoint breakdown with confidence intervals on the ratio), not a one-off n≈12–24 calibration exercise generalized to the whole 2052-generation panel.

4. **Issue** — Code and full stimulus/scoring pipeline are not currently available ("public on acceptance"), and hyperparameters/sampling settings live entirely in an unseen supplement (S19). `[Reproducibility]`
   **Where** — Section 2, "Code, data, settings."
   **Why it matters** — A reviewer (or reader) cannot today verify a single one of the ~30 quantitative claims in this paper against the underlying code, and the build.sh regeneration claim is unverifiable pre-acceptance. That's a common ICASSP-camera-ready pattern, but it means every number in this review's Major/Minor sections must be taken on the authors' word until release.
   **What would address it** — Release an anonymized code/data snapshot at submission time (many venues now require this), not merely tag-and-promise.

5. **Issue** — The paper's headline effect size is the product of a threshold (k ≥ 6) explicitly chosen after inspecting the data ("a threshold set after seeing the data"), and the "420-specification" robustness sweep offered as a corrective is itself a multiverse/specification-curve analysis that documents sign-stability while leaving magnitude highly threshold-dependent (34.4–86.4 points, median 70.5) — and the paper still leads with 75.4, near the top of that range, rather than the median or the unconditioned 46.8. `[Evidence — post-hoc-shaped hypothesis]`
   **Where** — Abstract; Section 3.1 ("The k ≥ 6 threshold was chosen after seeing where the arms diverge... Two exploratory analyses... are reported in text alone").
   **Why it matters** — This is the textbook garden-of-forking-paths problem: once a threshold is chosen because it maximizes observed divergence, the reported effect at that threshold is expected to be inflated relative to the population effect at any pre-specified threshold (a "winner's curse" / regression-to-the-mean issue that specification-curve breadth does not fix — it only tells you the sign survives, not that the magnitude is unbiased). A recent methodological critique makes exactly this point: multiverse/specification-curve analyses are well-suited to "abdication of responsibility" for a chosen headline number and can function to "manufacture doubt" about which specification is the "real" one while a specific number is still promoted in the abstract [2607.14623]. The paper's own honesty about the post-hoc-ness of k ≥ 6 is good practice, but honesty about a biased estimator does not de-bias the estimator — it just documents the bias. Additionally, selectively reporting "we quote the smaller" (of 67.6 vs. 75.4, cycled vs. never-cycled) is itself a discretionary post-hoc convention applied inconsistently with how the k ≥ 6 threshold itself was chosen (to maximize, not minimize, the reported gap) — the paper doesn't apply "always report the conservative number" as a general policy, only in the one place it's flagged.
   **What would address it** — Report the pre-registered (or a priori, non-data-dependent) threshold as the headline number in the abstract, with the post-hoc-maximizing k ≥ 6 number relegated to "exploratory, upper bound." Alternatively, hold out a subset of checkpoints/templates never used to pick k ≥ 6, and report the gap at k ≥ 6 *only* on that held-out subset — analogous to what the paper already does well for CosyVoice-2 as an architecture-level holdout, but not done for the threshold itself.

### Minor concerns

- Table 1's per-checkpoint mean of "exactly right" for repeated items (13.3, 16.9, 11.8, 16.7, 7.9, 38.9 → mean ≈17.6%) doesn't cleanly reproduce the abstract's "18.2%" without knowing the exact aggregation weights (per-generation vs. per-checkpoint) — not stated which is used.
- "Lean 4… mathlib… sorry-free… three standard axioms" reads as rigor-signaling for what is, after the antecedent is refuted, a formally verified vacuous implication; the paper itself admits "it explains nothing in Section 3" — worth trimming the ceremony proportional to the payoff, per the "no jargon-as-status-signaling" standard. `[Presentation]`
- Fig. 1 caption packs two sub-figures worth of dense notation ("hollow markers are the p = k arm, matched to neither in vocabulary nor length") that requires re-reading against the main text to parse; a reader relying on the figure alone would likely miss this caveat.
- "P = 0.010" appears exactly once in the whole paper (S28 carrier bootstrap) with no correction context given for how many other post-hoc thresholds were tried before landing on that one comparison.
- The n for several audits and ablations (n=12, n=24, n=36, n=60, n=90) varies by an order of magnitude across claims of similar strength, with no consistent power/precision framing.
- Abstract states the CTC judge "scores 1.00" against Whisper's 0.19 "on periodic ground truth" — worth clarifying in the main text (not just abstract) exactly what "1.00" means operationally (perfect on n=24 known-ground-truth items is different from "the CTC judge is unbiased in general").

### Belief update

Modest positive update on the proposition "TTS repetition failures track periodicity/repetition rather than sequence length per se" — the length-matched control design is genuinely the right experiment, and the multi-recogniser cross-check against the "recogniser artifact" alternative (Section 3.2) is a real strength that most papers in this space skip. I also update (mildly) toward "the field's naive mechanistic story (attention/state contraction) for this failure mode is probably wrong," since the authors did the unusual thing of formally deriving a testable prediction and then measuring and rejecting it rather than quietly dropping it. I do not update much on the specific magnitude of the effect (75.4 points, or any other single number in the 34–86 range) — the post-hoc threshold and the width of the specification curve mean I'd treat any single quoted figure as an upper-bound-ish estimate rather than a point estimate to cite elsewhere.

### Verdict

**Major revision** — the paper's central experimental design and mechanistic falsification are genuine strengths, but the headline effect size is built on a threshold "chosen after seeing where the arms diverge" and defended with a specification-curve sweep that documents sign-robustness while leaving a 2.5× magnitude spread unresolved and unexplained relative to the number actually promoted in the abstract [Evidence — post-hoc-shaped hypothesis].

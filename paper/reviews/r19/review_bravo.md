### Summary

The paper asks whether neural TTS models' well-known failure to correctly render a phrase repeated many times ("counting failure") is driven by input *length* or by *periodicity* per se, and whether a specific mechanistic account — that repeated segments drive a contracting per-repetition state map, making counts unreadable past a horizon — explains it. Using a 180-item "repetition ladder" (target word repeated k times, k∈{1,…,32}) paired with length- and word-count-matched controls in which the repeated token is replaced by distinct fillers, the authors find that six checkpoints across three TTS architectures render repeated text correctly far less often than their matched controls (18.2% vs 94.3% exact-match at k≥6, a ~75-point gap that survives decoding-parameter sweeps, judge substitution, and exclusion-rule ablations). They then formalize a contraction-based explanation as a Lean-4-verified theorem, estimate its key parameter (the Lipschitz constant q of a boundary-to-boundary state map) on one model, find q≫1 rather than q<1, and conclude the mechanism is empirically false even though the behavioral phenomenon is real.

### Major concerns

1. **Issue** — The title's causal claim ("Periodicity, Not Length") is stated as though periodicity has been isolated as *the* driver, but the paper's own discussion admits an unresolved confound — verbatim k-fold repetition is far rarer in training data than a length-matched distinct-word sentence — that predicts the identical dissociation under a pure out-of-distribution (frequency) account rather than a periodicity account.
   **Where** — Title; Section 5, "What the design does not separate" ("neither is separated from training-set frequency, k-fold verbatim repetition being far rarer in speech corpora than a matched sentence of distinct words, so an out-of-distribution account predicts the same dissociation").
   **Why it matters** — The entire framing (abstract, title, Section 3 heading "the limit tracks periodicity, not length") asserts periodicity as the operative variable, but the authors' own admission shows the design cannot distinguish "the state map degrades under periodic input" from "the model has rarely seen this input pattern and generalizes poorly." These have different implications for whether the failure is a representational/dynamical limit (as the mechanistic section frames it) or a data-coverage limit. As it stands the headline claim outruns what the control actually rules out.
   **What would address it** — Either retitle/rescope the claim to "periodicity or verbatim-repetition frequency, not length" throughout, or add a manipulation that holds training-frequency-like exposure constant while varying periodicity (e.g., synthetic fine-tuning exposure, or corpus-frequency-matched non-periodic-but-rare constructions) to actually adjudicate between the two accounts.

2. **Issue** — The central mechanistic refutation in Section 4 (q=38.1, "repetition makes the map more expansive rather than less," "the proposed mechanism is refuted") is measured on a single checkpoint (Llasa-1B) with n=29 items, yet is stated in the abstract and Section 4 as a categorical finding about "these decoders" (plural) and used to license withdrawal of the contraction account across the whole panel.
   **Where** — Section 4, "Measuring q" ("On Llasa-1B, over 29 repeated items and their length-matched controls, q = 38.1..."); Abstract ("We measure that premise with a validated estimator and reject it: q = 38.1, and repetition makes the map more expansive rather than less").
   **Why it matters** — The behavioral result (Section 3) is replicated across six checkpoints/three architectures/four ASR judges with extensive robustness checks — a genuinely strong evidentiary base. The mechanistic refutation that the abstract places on equal rhetorical footing rests on one model and 29 items, no cross-checkpoint or cross-architecture replication of q, and no correction for the (at minimum) implicit multiplicity of measuring q at "every lag and sub-stack." A theorem can be falsified for one decoder without being false for the class; the abstract's phrasing does not signal this asymmetry in evidentiary weight to the reader.
   **What would address it** — Report q for at least one checkpoint per architecture family (mirroring the behavioral panel's design), and rephrase the abstract/Section 4 conclusion to scope the refutation to the tested model unless replicated.

3. **Issue** — The causal rank-1 patch experiment used to argue "the count coordinate transplants cleanly and the decoder ignores it" (favoring the "policy fails to read a preserved count" account over "count is erased") is run on n=36 with no stated cross-model replication, yet is presented in Section 5 as adjudicating between two rival mechanistic accounts for the whole panel.
   **Where** — Section 5 ("A rank-1 patch transferring only the probe's count coordinate removes the damage... and moves the count not at all (0.00 log-count [−0.09, 0.09], n=36)... The count coordinate transplants cleanly and the decoder ignores it (S26)").
   **Why it matters** — This is the paper's most specific mechanistic claim about *why* the failure happens (readout failure vs. representational erasure), and per the git log context provided this appears to be a late, hard-won result ("the causal intervention became readable"). A 36-sample causal-patch result on one model is a thin basis for a claim framed as resolving the rival-mechanism question raised in the Related Work section against the cited dissociation literature [13].
   **What would address it** — State explicitly which model/checkpoint the patch experiment used, replicate on at least a second architecture, and report the patch effect with the same panel-level rigor (family/checkpoint clustering, CI) used for the behavioral gap in Section 3.

4. **Issue** — The abstract and Section 3 headline the family-level gap as "75.4 points [71.1, 78.9]," but a sensitivity check the authors themselves ran shows the interval touches zero under a different (equally defensible) prior on group-level scales, and this fragility is buried mid-paragraph rather than reflected in the headline uncertainty statement.
   **Where** — Section 3.1 ("under the widest prior on the group-level scales that interval does touch zero (−1.6, P = 0.956)").
   **Why it matters** — With only 3 architecture families, the partial-pooling extrapolation to "an architecture outside the panel" is doing a lot of work, and the authors' own robustness check shows the conclusion is prior-sensitive at the tails. Presenting 75.4 [71.1, 78.9] and P(gap>0)=0.996 as the headline number while the "widest prior" result is a parenthetical undersells how much the extrapolation depends on modeling choices with only n=3 clusters.
   **What would address it** — Report the prior-sensitivity range in the abstract or at minimum in the main results paragraph (not parenthetically), and clarify which prior is preferred and why, rather than defaulting to the most favorable one for the headline statistic.

5. **Issue** — Above k=8, control fillers cycle through a pool of eight, introducing period-8 structure into the "aperiodic" control condition; the correction for this (a never-cycled 146-word pool) is only run for the exploratory k=12–32 subset (n=240) and reduces the measured gap by 13.4 points there, but the main confirmatory Figure 1(a)/headline 75.4–76.4-point gap (computed over the full ladder, cycled controls) is not recomputed with never-cycled fillers.
   **Where** — Section 2 ("cycled above k=8 (S10)"); Section 3.1 ("Re-generating k=12...32 from a never-cycled 146-word pool leaves the dissociation at 71.2 points against 84.6 for cycled controls... the residual is worth 13.4 points, not the bulk of the gap").
   **Why it matters** — The paper's own robustness check shows that at least part of the effect at high k is attributable to residual periodicity in the "control" arm, not a pure repeated-vs-aperiodic contrast. Since a nontrivial fraction (13.4/84.6 ≈ 16%) of the gap in the tested range evaporates once the control's own periodicity is removed, and the headline confirmatory numbers (75.4, 76.4, 94.3% vs 18.2%) are drawn from the cycled-control ladder across the *entire* k range (not just k=12–32), it is unclear how much of the full-ladder headline gap would survive an equivalent correction.
   **What would address it** — Recompute the primary confirmatory statistics (Fig. 1a, Table 1, the 75.4/76.4-point gaps) using never-cycled controls across the full ladder, not just the k=12–32 exploratory subset, or explicitly bound how much of the full-range gap the cycling artifact could account for.

6. **Issue** — The CTC judge substituted for Whisper is validated only against concatenative (spliced) audio with synthetic ground truth, not against genuine model-generated pathological speech independently transcribed by humans; no listening study is reported.
   **Where** — Section 2, "Behavioural measurement..." (audit "against concatenative audio with known ground truth"); Section 5, "Limitations" ("The judge is validated against concatenative ground truth, not human listeners, and we ran no listening study").
   **Why it matters** — Concatenative audio splices clean, non-degraded segments together — it doesn't reproduce the acoustic artifacts (loops, truncations, garbled boundaries) that the actual failure mode under study produces. A recognizer validated as accurate on clean spliced repeats need not be equally accurate on the actual disfluent, looping speech the TTS models emit when they fail — which is precisely the regime driving the reported k≥6 gap. This is disclosed as a limitation but is more central than a footnote: it is the load-bearing measurement instrument for the entire headline result.
   **What would address it** — A modest human-transcription spot-check on the stratified 165-clip sample (which the paper says already ships with each clip's transcript) reported as inter-rater agreement with the CTC judge specifically on repeated-arm, failure-mode audio, not just clean concatenative ground truth.

### Minor concerns

- Table 1's "cap. ratio" column (repeated capacity gain as a fraction of control's) is referenced in Fig. 1(c) and the text but never explicitly defined in terms of what "capacity gain" (deff/dlogk) causally implies for the counting failure — it reads as a correlate presented with more inferential weight than a bare correlation supports.
- The dilution-lemma dose-response correlation (r=0.59 [0.35, 0.75]) is reported without stating the sample size (number of (model, k) cells) or correcting for the fact that k and checkpoint are non-independent units.
- "94.3%" and "18.2%" in the abstract are stated without a denominator (number of generations); this appears in Table 1/Section 3 body but should be restated with n in the abstract for a self-contained headline claim.
- The paired p=0.017 (n=29, "repeated lower in 8 of 29 pairs") in Section 4 is a single significance test embedded among many exploratory comparisons in the paper without any stated multiple-comparison framework; given the paper is otherwise careful about specification-curve robustness (S11, 420 specifications), the mechanistic section is comparatively under-powered and under-corrected.
- Self-citation [7] ("From dispersion to attraction...") is described as "a companion to a spectral study" — worth a brief statement of what if anything is shared (data, authors' claims) between the two, given the same author team and same period.
- Minor prose ambiguity: "the residual is worth 13.4 points, not the bulk of the gap" (Section 3.1) is a value judgment presented as if self-evidently minor; a reader could reasonably read 16% of the gap as non-trivial (see Major #5).

### Verdict

**Major revision** — the core behavioral dissociation (periodicity/repetition vs. length-matched controls) is unusually well-triangulated and likely robust, but the paper's headline framing repeatedly outpaces what the specific evidence shown supports: the title's causal claim leaves a self-acknowledged confound unresolved, and the mechanistic refutation and causal-patch results that anchor the paper's strongest theoretical claims rest on single-model, small-n analyses presented with the same confidence as the much better-replicated behavioral panel.

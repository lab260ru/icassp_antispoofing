### Summary

The paper makes three claims: (1) a Lean-formalized, sorry-free theorem that *if* an AR TTS decoder's per-repetition state update contracts (Assumption 2, unverified) *then* no Lipschitz readout can count repetitions past a computable horizon N*; (2) an empirical finding, across six checkpoints from three families, that repeated-phrase rendering degrades in a way that tracks periodicity rather than utterance length (median relative count error −8.3% at k≥6, 18.2% exact-match vs. 94.3% for length-matched non-repeating controls), absent in a non-AR baseline (VITS); and (3) a post-hoc extension to k=128 that finds the theorem's predicted saturating shape in 3 of 4 further-tested checkpoints, with the caveat — stated by the authors themselves — that the theorem's actual parameter (the contraction factor q) was never measured, and that a direct dose-response test of the causal mechanism (attention dilution → miscounting) came back sign-reversed. The empirical dissociation between periodic and non-periodic conditioning (claim 2) is the paper's real, fairly convincing contribution. The theorem (claim 1) is a legitimate but narrow formal exercise whose antecedent is never shown to hold for any real system, and the "horizon" (claim 3) is explicitly a shape-match, not a test of the theorem's quantitative content. The paper's greatest weakness, and the axis I pressed hardest on, is presentational rather than statistical: not a single example transcript, spectrogram, or generated string appears anywhere in the main text, despite the authors having built exactly the artifact (a 165-clip stratified sample) that would let a reader check this by hand.

### Major concerns

1. **Issue** — The empirical "horizon" at k=128 is fit and reported without ever measuring the theorem's own load-bearing parameter (q), and the one direct test of the mechanism that supposedly produces the horizon (attention dilution causing miscounting) came back sign-reversed. `[Evidence — distinguishing test failure]`
   **Where** — Section 4.4 ("Lemma 1's premise holds only approximately ... its dose-response prediction fails outright: within a (model, k) cell, flatter attention goes with better counting (r = 0.59 [0.35, 0.75])"); Discussion ("two attempts to measure q failed").
   **Why it matters** — The paper's title promises "a Lean-verified bound, and a horizon it *does not yet explain*" — an honest framing, but it means the observed k=128 saturation is consistent with the theorem only in the weak sense that both produce a decreasing, saturating curve. A generic "repetition exhausts some length/attention budget faster than distinct content does" story — with no contraction map, no fixed point, no Banach machinery — predicts the identical qualitative shape. Without a measured q, L, C, or µ for any actual checkpoint, the reader cannot tell the theorem's mechanism apart from this much cheaper alternative, and the one test built to discriminate mechanism (dilution dose-response) points the wrong way.
   **What would address it** — Either recover q by some route that survives the two failed attempts (even a crude, noisy estimator beats none), or explicitly frame the k=128 result as consistent-with-but-not-diagnostic-of the theorem, rather than letting the title's "horizon" imply the theorem explains it.

2. **Issue** — The chain connecting the machine-checked Lemma 1 (attention dilutes) to Assumption 1 (the decoder update becomes a single autonomous map F) is asserted in prose, not proved — the "O(δ+1/k)" claimed there is not a stated or formalized consequence of Lemma 1 as written. `[Presentation — formal-rigor framing]`
   **Where** — Section 2, the sentence immediately after Assumption 1: "This is what the lemma buys: if the decoder cannot distinguish the k spans, its update between consecutive boundaries is the same to within O(δ + 1/k)."
   **Why it matters** — The paper leans hard on "machine-checked... free of sorry and of nonstandard axioms" as a credibility signal. That's true of Theorem 1 given its assumptions, and of Lemma 1 in isolation. But the actual inferential step that makes the whole argument interesting — dilution *implies* approximate autonomy of the update — is exactly the step that is *not* formalized; it's stated as a gloss and then folded into an Assumption. A reader could easily come away thinking Lean verified more of the causal story than it did.
   **What would address it** — Either formalize the δ-to-Assumption-1 bridge as its own lemma (bounding, e.g., ‖F_actual(s) − F(s)‖ in terms of δ and 1/k), or state plainly in the text that this step is an informal heuristic bridging two formal results, not itself checked.

3. **Issue** — The abstract's universal claim ("the limit tracks periodicity, not length, in every checkpoint and every family") is contradicted by the paper's own Table 1, where Qwen3-TTS-1.7B shows a tied 0.0%/0.0% median count error for repeated vs. control items at k≥6. `[Narrative — overclaiming]`
   **Where** — Abstract vs. Table 1 (row "Qwen3-TTS-1.7B ... 0.0 0.0 ...") and Section 4.2 ("the median-error gap is positive in 5 of 6, zero in Qwen3-TTS-1.7B").
   **Why it matters** — On the paper's own headline metric (median relative count error), 1 of 6 checkpoints (1 of 3 families' larger member) shows *no* periodicity effect at all — not a smaller effect, a null. The "every checkpoint" framing survives only by switching to the exact-match metric (33.4% vs 41.5%, still the smallest gap in the panel) for that one case. This is a real inconsistency between abstract and data, not just a hedge the reader should infer.
   **What would address it** — Drop "in every checkpoint" from the abstract, or replace it with the more defensible claim actually supported: positive in 5/6 checkpoints and all 3 families by at least one metric, null on the count-error metric for Qwen3-TTS-1.7B.

4. **Issue** — The entire "this is architectural, not family-specific" claim rests on a single non-AR system (VITS, 2021), which is itself excluded from the headline cross-model comparison because it is lower quality. `[Evidence — baselines]`
   **Where** — Section 4.3, last paragraph ("Being smaller, only its within-model dissociation is compared").
   **Where else** — Title itself: "Counting Collapse in Autoregressive Text-to-Speech," implicitly staking a claim about the AR/non-AR boundary in general.
   **Why it matters** — n=1 is a thin basis for an architecture-level negative result. VITS not showing the dissociation is consistent with "AR recurrence is necessary," but it's equally consistent with "VITS specifically handles repetition well for reasons unrelated to autoregression" (e.g., its duration predictor, which the paper itself notes "leaves Assumption 1 no recurrence to act on" — a mechanistic argument, not an empirical sweep over non-AR architectures). A more recent, better-matched-quality non-AR/parallel TTS system (e.g., a flow-matching or diffusion-based synthesiser) would strengthen this considerably.
   **What would address it** — Add at least one more non-AR baseline of comparable output quality to the AR panel, so the null isn't riding on one older, lower-fidelity system.

5. **Issue** — The CTC judge's own label-collapse behaviour is a textbook repetition-specific bias in the same direction as the effect under study, and the audit against it uses concatenative (spliced) ground truth rather than model-generated ground truth. `[Evidence — alternative explanations]`
   **Where** — Section 3, "Behavioural measurement" paragraph.
   **Why it matters** — CTC decoding collapses consecutive identical output labels absent an intervening blank; if a model's rendering of the k-th repeat is acoustically very close to the (k−1)-th with no clean acoustic separation between them — which is more likely for whatever the model is doing wrong, not less — the CTC judge can undercount genuinely-rendered repeats for reasons that have nothing to do with the decoder's counting ability. The paper's audit ("ratio 1.00 on periodic ground truth against 0.19 for Whisper") is reassuring but uses concatenative splices, which likely have cleaner boundaries between repeats than a degenerating AR decoder's own output. This doesn't invalidate the CTC-over-Whisper choice (it's clearly the better instrument), but it means the judge's bias in the specific regime that matters most — actual model failures — isn't fully ruled out.
   **What would address it** — Audit the CTC judge specifically on a sample of the models' own degenerate/looping outputs against human-transcribed ground truth, not just clean concatenative audio.

6. **Issue** — Zero qualitative examples of the phenomenon appear anywhere in the paper, despite the authors having built and shipped exactly the artifact that would supply them. `[Evidence — cherry-picking / missing qualitative examples]`
   **Where** — Absent throughout Sections 3–4; the closest the paper comes is naming the released artifact: "a stratified 165-clip audio sample spanning every model, k band and outcome ships alongside, each clip paired with its transcript and scoring decision so a judgement can be checked by ear" (Section 3).
   **Why it matters** — Every quantitative claim in this paper — the −8.3% median error, the 18.2%/94.3% split, the capacity-gain ratios, the R² retention numbers — is computed by an automatic judge and reported only as an aggregate statistic. There is no way, from the paper text, to see what a "counting collapse" transcript actually says (does it truncate mid-repeat, loop past the requested count, substitute a different word?), no way to eyeball whether the CTC judge's scoring decisions look sane on hard cases, and no way to tell whether the 165-clip sample — mentioned but neither summarized nor analyzed in-text — was itself selected in a way that flatters the numbers. This is the strict-sense failure of the "are examples cherry-picked, are random examples shown" check: not that examples were hand-picked to look good, but that the paper contains no examples at all for a reader to judge that question, while simultaneously advertising that such a resource exists. For a paper whose whole evidentiary chain runs through one recognizer's transcripts, that is a real gap, not a nitpick.
   **What would address it** — Include even 3–5 concrete transcript examples in the main text or an appendix — ideally including at least one where the CTC judge and a naive listen might disagree — stratified the same way the released sample is, with enough detail that a reader can form an independent impression of what "collapse" looks like before trusting the aggregate numbers.

### Minor concerns

- The GitHub link ("https://github.com/lab260ru/icassp_antispoofing") names an unrelated topic (antispoofing) rather than the paper's subject (TTS counting collapse) — confusing for anyone trying to verify they've landed on the right repository.
- Figure 1's axis tick labels as rendered ("22 25" / "26 27" / "0 50") are not interpretable from the caption alone; if this is a text-extraction artifact, fine, but as delivered the figure fails the "interpretable from caption alone" bar.
- "Median count error of -8.3%" (pooled, abstract) vs. "-12.5% per checkpoint" (Section 4.1) are both given as the headline number with a rationale ("pooling weights checkpoints by surviving item count, so we quote both") — reasonable, but stating two different central-tendency numbers as *the* headline result without picking a primary one invites readers to quote whichever is convenient.
- "retention exceeds one in 2 of them" (Discussion, on the linear-probe test) is never defined precisely enough to know if this is a sanity-check pass or a red flag for the probe methodology.
- Reference [1] is cited as ordinary supporting evidence ("documented as a stability problem") despite being flagged in the reference list itself as "Withdrawn 13 Feb 2026," with no comment on why, or whether the withdrawal bears on the alignment-analysis claim it's cited for.
- The n=6 checkpoint panel is candidly reported as underpowered for significance testing (Section 4.2) — this is good practice, not a concern, but it's worth being explicit in the text that essentially every claim of universality in the paper is an effect-size claim over 6 data points, not a statistically tested one.

### Belief update

I moved my credence up moderately on one specific, narrow proposition: that AR TTS repetition failures are substantially periodicity-driven rather than a generic function of utterance length or exposure bias. Section 4.3's twin-carrier design and the never-cycled-pool rerun are the paper's best evidence, and they survive a real attempt to remove a confound (the filler-cycling issue), so I'll take that dissociation as reasonably well-supported modulo concern #5 above.

I did not move on the theorem's applicability to real transformers. A formally verified implication with an unverified antecedent, whose one direct mechanistic test (dose-response on attention dilution) came back sign-reversed, is evidence about the authors' honesty and rigor, not evidence about the mechanism. My credence that "contraction dynamics per Theorem 1" is *the* cause of counting collapse, as opposed to one of several structurally similar length/budget/repetition-penalty stories the paper doesn't cleanly rule out, is close to where it started.

### Verdict

**Major revision** — the periodicity-vs-length dissociation is a real, worthwhile finding, but the paper asks readers to trust an automatic judge's aggregate statistics as its *entire* evidentiary base without showing a single example of what the underlying phenomenon looks like, despite having built exactly that resource; that gap, plus the unverified theorem premise and the "every checkpoint" line that Table 1 itself contradicts, need fixing before this is publication-ready.

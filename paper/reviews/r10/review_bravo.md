### Summary

The paper studies why autoregressive (AR) text-to-speech (TTS) decoders miscount repeated words or phrases (e.g., "say six hundred sixty six thousand..." or "repeat X twelve times"). It proposes a theoretical account: if the per-repetition update on the decoder's hidden state is modeled as an approximately autonomous map F that contracts with factor q<1, then no L-Lipschitz readout of the state — including the model's own stop-token logit — can distinguish repetition counts beyond a horizon N* that is a function of q, the orbit scale C, and the readout margin µ (Theorem 1), with a companion "attention dilution" lemma (Lemma 1) explaining why the per-repetition map should be approximately autonomous in the first place. Both are machine-checked in Lean 4/mathlib. Empirically, across six AR TTS checkpoints (Llasa 1B/3B/8B, XTTS-v2, Qwen3-TTS 0.6B/1.7B) scored by a CTC recognizer (chosen because Whisper, an AR ASR model, is shown to be itself biased against repetition), models undercount repeated phrases (median relative error −8.3% pooled, exact match 18.2%) while length-matched non-repetitive controls are essentially exact (94.3%), and this dissociation disappears in a non-AR baseline (VITS). Within the pre-specified ladder (k ≤ 32) the deficit is proportional rather than saturating, contradicting the theorem's predicted constant horizon; a post-hoc extension to k=128 on 4 of 6 checkpoints does find saturation (with one checkpoint that shows no deficit at all), but the paper's two direct attempts to measure the contraction factor q failed, and a dose-response test of the attention-dilution mechanism came out sign-reversed. The authors present this candidly as an implication that is proved and a phenomenon that is measured, with the link between them unconfirmed.

### Major concerns

1. **Issue** — The paper's headline novelty claim ("the first hallucination result in speech synthesis carried out as a formal artifact") overstates what the Lean proof actually certifies, and the paper does not engage the existing formal neural-network verification literature that would contextualize this claim.
   **Where** — Abstract; §1 contributions list ("to our knowledge the first hallucination result in speech synthesis carried out as a formal artifact"); §2 "Formal verification" paragraph.
   **Why it matters** — What is formalized in Lean is a Banach-fixed-point corollary and a softmax concentration bound (Lemma 1) — general real analysis over an *abstract* map F and *abstract* attention logits, entirely untethered from any actual trained TTS network. The paper itself states that Assumption 2 (the one substantive, model-specific claim) was never verified — "two attempts to measure the contraction factor failed" — and that Lemma 1's dose-response prediction "came out sign-reversed." So the "formal artifact" certifies an if-then statement about a hypothetical dynamical system, not a property of any speech model, and not "a hallucination result" in the sense a reader would expect from that phrase. This differs materially from the established neural-network verification literature, which formally certifies properties of *concrete trained networks* via SMT/interval/reachability methods — e.g. Bunel et al. on piecewise-linear NN verification [1711.00455], Xiang et al.'s specification-guided safety verification [1812.06161], and Fijalkow & Gupta on formally specifying global robustness [1910.05018]. None of that line is cited or discussed, so the paper's "first formal artifact" framing is not situated against the body of work that actually verifies model behavior, and a reader could easily conflate "Lean-checked theorem about an idealized map" with "formally verified property of a real TTS system," which is a stronger and unsupported claim.
   **What would address it** — Narrow the novelty claim explicitly to "first Lean-formalized *theorem* connecting periodic conditioning, state contraction, and readout separability, applied to TTS" rather than "hallucination result... as a formal artifact"; add a paragraph distinguishing this contribution from network-verification work (which certifies properties of trained weights) and from autoformalization work; be explicit in the abstract/intro that the empirical premise (Assumption 2) remains unverified, not just in §5.

2. **Issue** — The theorem's distinctive, falsifiable prediction (a constant, q-determined horizon N*) is not observed in the paper's own pre-specified experimental range, and the confirmation that follows comes from a post-hoc extension on a non-preregistered subsample with an internal counterexample.
   **Where** — §4.1 ("no horizon is visible there"; ĉ = 0.950k fits better than any constant, RSS 2.6 vs 485.3); §4.2 (extension to k=128 restricted to "the 4 checkpoints admitting utterances that long," with Qwen3-TTS-1.7B showing "no repeated-side saturation... and inverts the ratio").
   **Why it matters** — This is close to textbook HARKing: the theorem's own signature (a horizon) fails in the designed test range, the ladder is then extended after the fact specifically to find one, and it is found on 4/6 checkpoints while the 6th flatly contradicts it. The paper is commendably transparent that this is post hoc, but a "conditional theorem behaving conditionally" is also compatible with the shape being an artifact of the fitting function (ĉ(K̂) = K̂(1 − e^{−k/K̂}) will produce *some* saturation scale for any bounded, decelerating sequence, including one produced by an entirely different mechanism, e.g. a token/length budget interacting with sampling variance). Absent a measured q, the horizon-shaped fit is consistent with Theorem 1 but does not discriminate it from alternative decelerating-count models.
   **What would address it** — Either preregister the extended ladder before running it (or clearly label all k>32 results as exploratory, which the paper mostly does but should do more prominently in the abstract, not only in §4.2/§5), and report a model-comparison (e.g. AIC/BIC or held-out fit) between the saturating-count model and at least one non-mechanistic alternative (e.g., a generic length-ceiling model with no periodicity term) fit to the same combined ladder.

3. **Issue** — The mechanistic bridge from theorem to phenomenon (attention dilution → approximate autonomy → contraction) is empirically contradicted at the one place it is tested.
   **Where** — §4.4, last paragraph: "Lemma 1's premise holds only approximately (δ ≤ 1.23 nats) and its dose-response prediction fails outright: within a (model, k) cell, flatter attention goes with better counting (r = 0.59 [0.35, 0.75]; S12)."
   **Why it matters** — Lemma 1 is the mechanism offered for *why* Assumption 1 (a single autonomous map F) should approximately hold, and it is the only piece of the causal chain that is both formally proved and directly tested against data within this paper. A sign-reversed dose-response means that, within their own data, more attention dilution is associated with *better* counting, the opposite of what the dilution story predicts. The paper notes this but does not revise the causal narrative or offer a replacement mechanism; it only says "whatever selects which generations fail is not the degree of dilution." This leaves the theorem's applicability to real transformers resting entirely on the unverified Assumption 2, with its proposed micro-mechanism (Lemma 1) actively disconfirmed.
   **What would address it** — Either drop or substantially soften the causal claim that attention dilution is the operative mechanism, and reframe Lemma 1 as motivating Assumption 1 only qualitatively; or investigate what the r=0.59 relationship is picking up (e.g., could higher per-step entropy generally correlate with more careful/less templated generation, a confound rather than evidence against dilution as a cause of Assumption 1 holding across the whole trajectory rather than within a single (model,k) cell)?

4. **Issue** — Six checkpoints from three families is too small a sample for the significance framing to bear on, and the paper's own statistical honesty here ("the exact signed-rank floor at n=6 is 0.031, and no multiplicity correction clears 0.05") is not fully carried through into how strongly the abstract and §4 state the results ("in every checkpoint and every family").
   **Where** — §4.2, penultimate paragraph; abstract ("the limit tracks periodicity, not length, in every checkpoint and every family").
   **Why it matters** — With n=6 units (3 families), no formal generalization claim is possible, and the paper says as much. But "in every checkpoint and every family" is stated as a headline finding in the abstract without that caveat attached, which risks readers taking a universality claim from what is, by the authors' own admission, an underpowered convenience sample of publicly available checkpoints (no VALL-E-family, Bark, Tortoise-TTS, or flow-matching AR-adjacent systems such as F5-TTS/E2-TTS are included). The bootstrapped intervals reported throughout (e.g. "76.7 points [68.4, 84.4]", "75.4 points [71.1, 78.9]") are themselves computed over only 6 (or 3) units at the top level, however they are bootstrapped over stimulus templates — this needs to be stated plainly wherever these intervals are quoted, since a reader skimming Table 1 / Fig. 1(c) could easily read them as having the precision the CI format suggests.
   **What would address it** — State the n=6/n=3 caveat next to the abstract's universality claim, not only in §4.2; consider adding at least one more architecturally distinct AR family (even a small one) to reduce the risk that "every family" is describing three points.

5. **Issue** — The two acoustic recognizers used to validate the CTC judge disagree by an amount that is a large fraction of the effect size being measured, and this uncertainty is not propagated into the headline statistics.
   **Where** — §5, penultimate paragraph: "two recognisers disagree by 0.47 repetitions where the mean item is missing 0.87 repetitions."
   **Why it matters** — If inter-recognizer disagreement (0.47) is roughly half of the mean measured deficit (0.87), then a non-trivial share of the reported −8.3% median error and the 18.2%/94.3% exact-match contrast could reflect measurement noise in the judge rather than model behavior, especially at the individual-item level the exact-match statistic depends on. The paper reports that its own judge gives the higher count in 11 of 13 disagreements (i.e., it is if anything conservative, biased toward *smaller* apparent deficits), which is reassuring directionally, but n=13 disagreements is too small to bound how much this factor could be inflating or deflating the reported gap size.
   **What would address it** — Report the headline count-error and exact-match statistics under both recognizers (or with recognizer as a random effect / measurement-error-corrected estimate), not only the qualitative note that CTC gives the higher count in most disagreements.

6. **Issue** — Several statistics central to the paper's defenses of its own results (the bootstrap procedure, the "signed-rank floor," the dose-response correlation, the output-diversity covariate adjustment, the linearity-control failure) are relegated to supplementary sections (S5, S8, S10, S11, S12, S14) not included in the reviewable text.
   **Where** — Throughout §4 and §5 (repeated parenthetical references to S5/S8/S10/S11/S12/S14).
   **Why it matters** — Given how much of the paper's rhetorical weight rests on fine statistical distinctions (e.g., "positive in 5 of 6, zero in Qwen3-TTS-1.7B," "the confound is worth 13.4 points, not the bulk of the effect"), a reviewer cannot verify the correlation computation, the bootstrap resampling unit, or the "exact signed-rank floor" claim from the main text alone. This is an artifact of the ICASSP page budget, but it means the paper as submitted is not fully self-contained for its core statistical claims.
   **What would address it** — At minimum, state in the main text (even briefly) what unit the bootstrap resamples over (templates? items? seeds?) and what test the "signed-rank floor" refers to, since these determine whether the reported intervals/p-values are answering the question the prose implies.

7. **Issue** — No ethics or conflict-of-interest statement, despite releasing a 165-clip audio sample generated in part via XTTS-v2, a zero-shot voice-cloning model, without saying whose reference voice(s) were used or under what consent.
   **Where** — Absent; §3 "Code, data, settings" describes the released audio sample but not its provenance/consent basis.
   **Why it matters** — Zero-shot voice cloning models require a reference speaker; releasing generated clips publicly implicates that speaker's likeness. This is a standard disclosure in TTS papers that use real reference voices and is missing here.
   **What would address it** — A short statement on reference-voice consent/licensing for the XTTS-v2 stimuli and on funding/COI, as most ICASSP submissions include.

### Minor concerns

- Reference [1] is cited for its "alignment analysis" despite being marked "Withdrawn 13 Feb 2026" — the paper should note why it was withdrawn (correctness issue vs. venue/administrative reasons), since relying on a withdrawn paper's analysis without that context is a citation-durability red flag.
- The breakdown of the 180-item stimulus set (word-repetition, sentence-repetition, tongue twisters, number phrases) is never mapped onto the headline pooled statistics (n=457 repeated items, n=525 controls in §4.3) — it is unclear how much each stimulus category contributes, or whether number-phrase items (which depend on text-normalization frontends) are pooled in with templated word repetition.
- "Effective rank" (§3, "State measurement" and §4.4) is used as the operationalization of Theorem 1(ii)'s "states stop being mutually distinguishable" without defining how it's computed (singular-value threshold, entropy of the spectrum, etc.) in the main text.
- The abstract's number "-8.3%" and §4.1's "-8.3% [−12.5, −6.2]" for pooled vs. per-checkpoint medians (−12.5%) are easy to conflate on a first read; consider stating both with matching precision every time either is quoted, as is mostly (but not always) done.
- Figure 1 axis labels in the provided text render oddly ("22 25 k 100 0 count error (\%)" / "26 27 k 25 26 27 rendered count") — likely a figure-extraction artifact, but worth double-checking axis tick labels in the camera-ready.
- §4.3 states the CTC-vs-Whisper audit gives "ratio 1.00 on periodic ground truth against 0.19 for Whisper" immediately after stating Whisper's own audited ratio range is "0.27–0.65 for k≥4" — the 0.19 figure isn't clearly reconciled with the earlier 0.27–0.65 range and could use one clarifying clause.
- The related-work discussion of [13] (Barbero et al., representational collapse) is good, but a directly relevant, very recent paper on how language models track state during generation ([2503.02854], Li, Guo & Andreas) is not discussed; given the paper's Assumption 1/state-map framing, this seems like a natural comparison point even if ultimately distinguished.

### Verdict

**Major revision** — the empirical periodicity-vs-length dissociation is well-controlled and is the paper's real contribution, but the formal-verification novelty claim is overstated relative to what is actually certified, the theorem's own signature prediction fails in the pre-specified range and is only rescued post hoc with an internal counterexample, and the proposed mechanism (attention dilution) is empirically contradicted in the one place it's tested — these need to be resolved in the claims (not just conceded in prose) before the paper's framing matches its evidence.
